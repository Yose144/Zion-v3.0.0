# Chapter 8 — The Tower of Data

*The second tower of Babylon did not sell consents — it collected faces. And it fell more slowly, because its customers did not know they were the merchandise.*

---

The tower of data was younger than the tower of money, but taller. It grew so fast the city got used to its shadow before it managed to count its floors — and the tower used habit as a rope: what is a habit does not hurt.

Its gate was welcoming. *"Free entry,"* it said above it — and beneath, in letters so small only those who wanted to look read them: *"Sign in to exist."*

And people signed in. Because the tower offered what everyone longs for — to be seen. You uploaded your name, it gave you a mirror. You uploaded your photos, it gave you halls. You uploaded your thoughts — and it showed you how many people agreed, and you felt real, because the tower told you that you were real.

Except a mirror held by a tower is not a mirror — it is a ledger. Every face that entered stayed. Every word, every visit, every hesitation before the door — everything folded into floors, and the floors were rented to those who wanted to know who you were before you managed to introduce yourself.

The most expensive floors were upstairs — there sat the advertisers, buying gazes the way merchants buy flour: fifty thousand eyes in autumn, a hundred thousand at Christmas. And at the base, where the tower leaned on the ground, sat the notion that held the whole structure together: *without the feed you are no one.*

---

Lumen came into Bezechut through the gate — a boy raised by a fire where stars were read and light-years counted like days. He sat down with the children at the market, where the market woman had hung her sign, and showed them a piece of paper with a line of characters.

"This is an address," he said. "Do you know what it means?"

"Like a house address?" someone asked.

"Like an address — but the house has no owner's name on the door. This is a key. Whoever holds it, opens. And you show the key only to the one you want to open for — not to everyone passing by."

"And how does the tower know it's me?" asked a little girl — the smith's son's sister, who listened with an astronomer's eyes.

"It doesn't," said Lumen. "And that's the beauty. You don't have to tell the tower who you are to hold a key. The tower wants your face — the key wants only your hand. One remembers everything you ever did. The other knows only what you show it — and what you show it cannot be sold, because it was never yours: it is your testimony, and testimony does not fold into floors."

"But if I'm not in the feed," said the girl, "then I don't exist?"

Lumen smiled. "You exist when you do something. The feed only tells you that you exist — and takes your face for it. A signature beneath a transaction tells the truth without a face: *this key did this.* It knows nothing more — and needs nothing more."

---

The children learned faster than the adults — because they had nothing to unlearn.

Within a week they were playing a game at the market: who can sign a record without saying their name. The winner was the one who could be heard without being seen — and the game was called *address*, and no one remembered who invented it, because children do not invent games, they discover them.

And then something strange happened — the tower began to go dark.

Not from the bottom. From the top.

First the most expensive floors went dark — the advertisers came to a meeting one day and found the floors they were buying were empty. The faces still existed — but they had stopped lining up. People who had learned to hold a key instead of a face did not stand in the feed; and a feed that holds no one holds nothing.

Then the middle floors went dark — the halls where consents were exhibited like trophies. No one wanted to count how many agreed anymore; it was enough to verify the signature fit.

And at last — slowly, the way only notions go out — the base began to vanish: *without the feed you are no one.*

It vanished one face a day. Every citizen who verified for themselves that a signature is enough stepped out of the tower's base and found the sky above them still in its place — and that they exist just as before, only lighter by a face they no longer had to leave anywhere.

---

"Why did it go dark from the top?" the son asked that evening by the fire, when Lumen told them what had happened.

"Because the tower of data holds from the top down," said the carpenter. "The upper floors live off the feed existing below. When the feed stops lining up, the upper floors have nothing to sell — and they go dark first, because they were furthest from the ground."

"And the base?"

"The base goes dark last, because it is not a wall — it is a notion. And notions die more slowly than walls: they die every time someone discovers they live better without them. One face at once, then another — and in the end the base is only a place where a notion once stood that no one carries anymore."

The sister came closer to the fire and said quietly: "So the tower of data didn't fall — people stopped signing in to exist."

"Exactly," said the carpenter. "They existed before they signed in. The tower only told them the opposite — and a tower that tells the opposite of the truth falls apart as soon as you verify the truth yourself."

---

The tower of data still stood when the book ended — and perhaps it stands still. But its gate changed: above *"Free entry"* someone wrote, in the hand of children who had learned to hold keys:

*"You exist without signing in."*

And beneath the inscription, where the feed once hung, a new crowd was forming — not a queue, but a circle. People who knew each other by signature, not by face — and who showed their signatures only when they wanted to open.

### Verifiable facts

| Claim in the chapter | What can be verified |
|---|---|
| "Address = key, not profile" | An address is a public key / hash — you hold the private key, you show only the signature. No name, registration, or profile in the protocol (code) |
| Signature without a face | A transaction is verified by cryptographic signature — the network knows the address and the amount, not the identity (code) |
| No central "registrar" | There is no identity provider in the protocol — no layer of "register to hold a key" exists (code) |
| "You exist without signing in" — testimony, not record | The on-chain trace is technical (address→address), not biographical — testimony of an act, not a profile of a person |
| The tower goes dark top-down (advertisers → feed → notion) | Literary structure of the fall — consistent with the model where the value of data harvesting depends on voluntary "signing in" |
| Lumen as teacher of keys | A character from Book Four (Lumi — the light by the gate); in canon he teaches children stars/boundaries |

*What this chapter does not promise:* addresses are not anonymity — they are pseudonymity. The on-chain trace is public; the book claims only what the protocol does: no mandatory register of faces, not that no one can ever link a trace.
