# PROLOGUE — The Signature Beneath the Egg

*An hour after the rain. A year after the smith set the last hinge on the ark's door.*

---

The rain that came was not a flood.

It fell once a week, quietly, like someone turning a page — and most of Bezechut didn't even notice. The market still smelled of rain and ashes, the towers of Babylon still stood in their places, only a little quieter than before. But in the middle of the city, on the empty square where children once played on a broken cart, stood a finished ship.

It was whole. The hull's ribs smooth as a whale's back, the deck tarred, the doors hung on hinges — and no one knew when it would sail. Not the smith who had forged those hinges, not the saddler who had carried planks that would otherwise have burned in the furnace. The builder who spoke of rain did not build cities, but he did build a ship — and the ship already stood.

Every morning the smith circled it before lighting his forge. Not because he didn't trust it. Because he didn't know what to do with a ship that is finished before it is needed.

One morning, while dew still held on the keel's base, he found a carving on the underside of the hull. Small, deep, cut by a hand that did not err:

> *Maitreya Buddha, 2025*

The saddler found him kneeling before the carving, brushing away with a bare finger shavings that were not there.

"That wasn't there yesterday," the smith said.

"Nor the day before," the saddler confirmed. "I circle it too. After you."

Nobody remembered anyone carving it. Not the smith, who knew every bolt. Not the saddler, who knew every plank. Not the carpenter, who sometimes came, sat down in its shadow, and stayed silent so long he seemed asleep.

That night they sat by the fire as they once used to — the smith, the saddler, the market woman, a merchant back from a journey across the sea, and the carpenter. And at the smith's side, where he remained even after work, sat his son — he did not listen to the words; he listened to the gaps between them, the way he listened to the gaps between hammer blows. His sister sat a little apart, reading the stars. The fire was smaller than during the building; the ship no longer needed light for work. But storytelling did.

"It's a signature," the market woman said. "A signature goes under a work. So the ship is finished — and it was signed a year ago."

"Or it's an inscription," said the saddler. "An inscription goes on a ship so it won't be lost. Whoever signed it claims it's his."

"No one can own it," the smith objected slowly. "I forged the hinges. You carried the planks. The market woman paid for half the tar. The carpenter said where the keel should be drawn. If it belonged to anyone, it would be ours — and it isn't ours."

Meanwhile his son had gone back to the ship. In the light the fire now watched only from afar, he ran his finger along the carving — along the arc, along the stroke, along the digit — the way a blind man reads a hand. His father saw it and told no one: the boy was not reading the letters. He was feeling them.

Then the smith said aloud what he had long carried quietly: *"Heir of what?"*

In the answer they heard no voice. They heard the carpenter murmur, more to the fire than to them:

"Have you read the proclamation? The one about the egg?"

They had all read it. It stood in the Book of Genesis, fourth page. Year 2035, ten years after genesis — somewhere in the chain a great egg lay hidden, and beneath the proclamation a single name no one had ever seen as a person: *The first to find it inherits the entire fortune. — Maitreya Buddha, 2025.*

"Same year," said the merchant. "Same name. A signature beneath two things that belong together — a ship and an egg. A ship no one owns a claim on. An egg from which something is to be born."

"So heir of what?" the smith insisted.

The carpenter looked at him long — the way he had once looked at a woman who had buried three children and asked what happens if the salt loses its savour.

"An egg from which something is to be born is not inherited," he said. "It ripens. And inheritance is not what you receive. It is what you are able to hand on — and nothing happens."

For a long time no one spoke. The market woman looked at the ship, whose keel gleamed in the firelight like the back of a whale waiting for water.

"And if no one is found who can hand it on?"

"Then the egg waits," said the carpenter. "That's what eggs are good at. Waiting."

At that moment the smith's son turned away from the ship — so quietly that only his father noticed — and asked the only question of the whole evening:

"And who inherits the egg? The one who finds it — or the one it was written to?"

No one answered. Not even the carpenter.

---

Behind them, beyond the market, beyond the last tower of Bezechut where no lantern had burned in the topmost storey for three weeks now, a drum began to play. Not loud — like a heartbeat you notice only when every other sound falls silent.

Someone said *Maitreya* and someone said *the one who comes*. Both were right.
