# Chapter 9 — The Tower of Energy

*The third tower of Babylon drew current from the sky by cable and sent the invoice down the stairs — and it fell the day the last citizen understood that current can be shared.*

---

The tower of energy was the only tower that shone.

That was its genius: the other towers sold the invisible — consents, orders, meanings — but the tower of energy sold light itself, so no one begrudged it its height. *It shines,* people said. *Being tall is its job.*

The cable came from the sky — from places where current is born in factories so large their chimneys wrote spells across the horizon. It ran down the tower, floor by floor, branching at each — and at the end of every branch, in every house, there was a wall. And in it an outlet. And behind the outlet current — and behind the current an invoice.

The invoice always came at the end — that was a rule older than the tower itself: first it shines, then you pay. And it never occurred to anyone to ask why current that falls from the sky for free costs so much by the time it reaches the outlet — because every generation knew the answer: *the tower.* The tower is the middleman between heaven and the socket — and a middleman charges for standing in the middle.

---

The first to ask differently was the merchant from overseas — the one who had returned from a journey on which he had seen twelve hearths.

"Do you know what I saw across the sea?" he said one evening by the fire, and the citizens gathered to listen, because the merchant only ever told of things he had verified himself. "Twelve places where people light their own light. Each has its own fire — panels that catch the sun, mills that catch the wind, waters that catch the current. They are not in a tower. They are in the landscape — like fires burning each beside its own tent."

"And invoices?" asked the market woman.

"There are none," said the merchant. "The sun bills for free. The wind sends no statement. You pay — but for roofs and wires, not for current. Current is free from the moment you build your own roof that catches it."

"And the tower?"

"There is no tower there," said the merchant. "Not because they tore it down — they simply never built it. Where everyone carries their own fire, nobody raises a tower to sell it back to them."

---

The saddler pondered — and saddlers, when they ponder, think of the land.

"It's like a well," he said at last. "When everyone has their own well, no one buys water from the one who owns all the wells. The tower of energy is really a well one man built in the middle of the village — and then sold the water that fell on everyone for free."

"But we don't have a well," said one of the citizens. "We have roofs, but the wires run from the tower."

"Then build a roof that shines," said the merchant — and pointed to the city's roofs, lying under the sun all day and gathering only heat. "The twelve hearths started the same way — one roof, then two, then a street. They didn't wait for the tower to permit roofs that shine on their own — they simply built."

---

It began with one house.

The smith — who knew how to forge hinges that hold a hundred years — built a roof of panels onto his workshop that the merchant had brought across the sea. The next day the workshop shone with its own current — and the smith hung the outlet on the wall as a curiosity, not a necessity.

Then the market woman — a stall beneath a roof that caught the sun, and in it a lamp that shone at night from what it had gathered by day.

Then the saddler — barn, mill, well.

And then something happened that no one noticed at the moment it happened — because the fall of towers is not heard, it is seen: the houses that shone with their own light began to share it.

When the smith's roof gave more than the workshop needed, he sent the surplus to a neighbour — not for an invoice, but for bread, for wood, for a good morning. When the market woman's lamp shone longer than the stall needed, it shone for the street. The current became what it always was — a gift from the sky — only this time it was not dealt out by the tower, but by the street.

---

"That cannot work," said one of the clerks who had come from the tower to look at the houses that shone by themselves. "Someone must apportion the current. Without the tower no one knows who has how much."

"We know," said the saddler. "Each knows how much they caught. And when they caught more than they need, they share — because the sun rises again tomorrow. That is the difference between accounting and sharing: you account for what you hold — you share what flows. Current flows. The sun does not stand still."

"But the tower maintains stability—"

"The tower maintains the outlet," said the smith — and pointed to the outlet in his workshop, which no longer carried current, only the memory of a time when current arrived with an invoice. "Stability is maintained by a roof that catches and a neighbour who shares. The tower maintains only that without it you forget that current falls for free."

The clerks left — not because they were convinced, but because the argument they wanted to make did not exist: *"without the tower there is no current"* no longer rang as truth but as the tower's slogan — and slogans, once the reader verifies them, turn into their opposite: *there is current — and the tower stands in its way.*

---

The last citizen unscrewed his outlet within the year.

Not symbolically — practically. He was an old maintenance man who had spent his whole life repairing the tower's wires — and when he came home to the last apartment outlet still holding the tower's current, he turned the screwdriver and the outlet came loose.

"Why are you doing it?" asked his granddaughter, who was helping gather the shards.

"Because now I know current can be shared," he said. "And an outlet is a boundary — it keeps current inside, wires, and you pay for the current to flow behind it. When you unmount it, current does not flow through an outlet — it flows through the street. And the street does not bill."

The tower of energy remained standing — and shining. But it shone only for itself now, into floors where no one lived, onto outlets no one visited. A beacon showing what the world was like when people still paid for what fell from the sky.

And in the streets, between houses with roofs that shone, a rule was born that was never written, yet everyone knew:

*"Current is not sold. It is shared — because the sun bills for free."*
