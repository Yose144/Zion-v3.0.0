# Chapter 17 — The View from the Window

*And finally — a window. The same window from which a planet once looked up. This time it looks down.*

---

The station was called Issobella — and it had borne her name since the moment the world decided that the tithe would go to the stars as well.

Sarah Issobela sat by the porthole — and below her turned a planet that was no longer Babylon. Not the blue marble of a picture — a living planet, with cities shining with their own light, with ships sailing through the waters they carried, with fires burning where they were needed, and going dark where they were not.

Behind her, in the silence of a station that smelled of metal and time, stood her little brother — the one who had been at the bow of the ship, who had held the seat of fire, who had learned to hold air without owning it. Down there, on Earth, he held the ship together — not with his hand, but by letting others bear their pieces.

"Come and look," she said — and pointed down.

---

She was not discovering a planet — she was discovering meaning.

The first time she had looked out the window as a child, she saw a map: lights, rivers, coasts. The second time, as a student, she saw a network: nodes, links, flows. Now she looked as a guardian — and saw something else: *one planet, one people, one love* — not as three words in a picture, but as what remains when the towers go dark.

"Do you see Babylon?" she asked her brother.

"I see where it was," he said — and pointed at a shore where five towers stood, still stone, still tall, but only monuments now. "They stayed — because we did not demolish them. We just stopped needing them to shine for us."

"And now?"

"Now the houses shine," he said — and pointed at the lights that had come on all along the shore, street by street, house by house. "The tower shone down — the houses shine outward. The tower showed where to go — the houses show that there is nowhere to go anymore."

---

They watched together — and below them turned a planet that already knew how to tend itself.

"One planet," said the sister — and her voice carried the silence of the station. "I see it whole — not its borders, not its flags, but its circle. One world that holds because everyone holds it."

"One people," said the brother. "Not as a crowd — as a crew. Each bears a piece, and when a piece gives way, another bears it. No one is above it — everyone is within it."

"One love," said the sister — and smiled, because she knew love cannot be shown on a map, but it can be seen from a window. "You carry it always — in the tithe that flows, in the signature that holds, in the light that came on when the towers went dark."

And below them, on the deck of a ship sailing through the world, the mantra rang out — this time not sung, but steered, the way a ship is steered:

*"One Love · One Human · One World."*

---

"When did it begin?" asked the sister — and her question sounded like the question of those who already know the answer but want to hear it again.

"With an egg," said the brother. "An egg that did not break until it ripened. A signature the father wrote in his son's name, because he knew a name is not carried — it is passed on. Keys that are not bought but inherited — and inherited when you show you can hold them for others. A seat that is air — because power that holds space does not serve itself, it serves all."

"And the towers that fell?"

"Towers that fell by departure, not attack," said the brother. "The world did not change when we demolished towers — it changed when we stopped needing them. And love and truth prevailed — not because they defeated, but because they endured."

The sister watched the planet a long time — so long it seemed she could see the blocks being born, one after another, each beginning with a golden germ, each ending in samadhi.

"And now?" she asked.

"Now we hold," said the brother. "We hold the ship, we hold the space, we hold the light — and we hold it by not holding it. We pass it on. And when she comes, the one for whom the chair is empty, we will still be here — because air does not leave while it has something to hold."

---

And from the window of the station that bore her name, Sarah Issobela looked down at the world — and the world looked up at her.

Not because she was above — because she was in the window. And windows are for looking both ways: down at what you hold, and up at what holds you.

One planet, one people, one love — and towers that went dark so the light could shine differently.
