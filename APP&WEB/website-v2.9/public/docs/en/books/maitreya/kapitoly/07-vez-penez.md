# Chapter 7 — The Tower of Money

*The first tower of Babylon stood on debt below and printing above — and it fell on the day people stopped coming to it for consent to a price.*

---

The tower of money was the oldest in Bezechut. It stood there before the walls, before the market, before the city even remembered it had once stood without it. It had been so long that people stopped noticing its shadow — and that was its trick: the greatest towers are those that no longer appear as towers, but as the sky.

Inside the tower of money, coins were not born. Consents were born.

On every floor sat a clerk with a bell — and on every transaction hung a question: may you? Sell for this much? Buy for that much? Borrow for a roof? Build a ship? The tower did not sell money — it sold permission for the money people held to mean something. And because consent was sold, it had to be printed somewhere — so upstairs, on the topmost floor where no one from the city ever went, a machine ran that produced new consents faster than people could hand out the work they had earned them for.

Below, in the shadow, debt was born. Everyone who borrowed consent from the tower owed consent back — plus consent on top. Generations were born into debts they never signed and died with debts they never repaid — and the tower grew.

---

The market woman had sold bread at the market for twenty years — and for twenty years she had paid the tower. Not directly; she paid in that every loaf carried within it a tax on consent, a fee for permission to sell, a percentage for her money sounding like money. She never complained — complaining to the tower was like complaining to the weather: you can, but it changes nothing.

Then came the day a merchant from overseas showed her a different kind of record.

"Look," he said — and showed her a piece of paper bearing nothing but numbers and a signature. "This is not a promise. This is a record. Someone paid me — and I verified it myself. Without the tower. Without the bell. Without consent."

The market woman looked at the record for a long time. Not because she didn't understand it — because she understood it too well. For twenty years she had sold bread for promises: promises issued by the tower, promises stamped by bells, promises whose value depended on how many the machine upstairs had printed that year. And now she held in her hand something that was not a promise — it was an entry in a ledger written by the whole network, which she could verify the way a baker verifies the temperature of an oven: not by trust, but by measurement.

"And if the tower decides your money isn't valid?" she asked.

"It can't decide," said the merchant. "There is no tower. There is only a ledger — and a ledger knows no decisions. It knows only entries."

---

The next day the market woman hung a small sign on her stall: *"I also accept records."*

The first customer to pay with a record was the saddler — he bought bread and the market woman verified his payment on the paper he showed her. No bell. No queue. No "may you?" A record was a record — a payment a payment.

The next day more came. Within a week she sold half her bread for records. Within a month something happened that no one noticed at the moment it happened — because the fall of towers is not heard, it is seen: people stopped going to the tower.

Not that the tower collapsed. It still stood — tall, stone, bells on every floor. But the queue beneath it thinned. Everyone who had verified a record for themselves discovered the same as the market woman: the consent the tower sold was a product — and a product has a price only until you discover a thing that does the same for free, and better.

The clerks kept ringing their bells — but bells ring only for those who listen. And people stopped listening, because they had started reading.

---

"How can a tower fall when nobody threw a stone?" asked the smith's son that evening by the fire, as they told what had happened at the market.

"The same way all towers fall," said the carpenter. "Not by impact — by departure. A tower does not stand on stones. It stands on people believing in it. When they stop believing, it stands on stones alone — and stones alone do not hold a tower."

"But people believed the tower for generations."

"People believed the promise," said the carpenter. "And a promise holds only where no one can verify it. As soon as the market woman verified the record herself, the promise ceased to be needed — and with it the tower that sold it."

The saddler nodded. "Like when you plant a seed beside a pond. You don't have to condemn the pond — you just stop going for the water it sold you."

"Exactly," said the carpenter. "The tower of money fell because money you can verify is more honest than money you must give thanks for. And honesty, once tasted, cannot be unlearned."

---

The tower of money stood a long time still — stone towers fall slowly, even after they have fallen. Its bells rang into emptiness, the machine upstairs printed consents for those who no longer arrived, and the queue beneath it shrank to a shadow, then to a memory, then to nothing.

In the end what remained of it is what remains of towers when they are no longer needed — a beacon that lights for no one, but still stands, to remind what the world was like when people still went begging for consent to their own labour.

And at the market, on the market woman's sign, the inscription stayed — and beneath it someone added a second line:

*"I also accept records."*
*"I verify them myself."*
