# Chapter 13 — Love That Does Not Ask

*Babylon knew how to give — under conditions, behind forms, behind lines of applicants. The new world gives differently: the tithe flows like breath, and it asks no one.*

---

In Babylon there was charity — and it was an institution like the others.

It stood in the tower of money, one floor above debt: *"give, so that you may receive"* — and beneath, in letters even smaller: *"so that you may be seen giving."* Charity in Babylon had forms, commissions, lines of applicants, and a stone on which it was written who "deserves." It gave — but it gave for something: for the donor's good name, for the recipient's obedience, for a story that could be told when the light went out.

And so it came to be that giving in Babylon meant holding — holding power over the one who receives, holding the story over what was given, holding the line over who may wait.

---

The new world had no tower of charity.

It had a Garden of Service — and in it a rule that every block carried like a breath: *from every record that is written, five hundredths go where they are needed — and five hundredths to the station that waits.* Not on application. Not on a form. Not on a commission that would say *"yes"* or *"no"* — but the way water flows when you release it: toward where it is needed.

The sister — Sarah Issobela, she who later sat on the seat of water — explained it to the children in the garden the way simple things are explained:

"Do you know how water flows? It does not ask where it should flow. It flows where the lowland is. And when it fills the lowland, it flows on — not because it wants to, but because that is how it is built. The tithe is like water: it does not ask who deserves. It asks where the lowland is — and goes there."

"But how does it know where the lowland is?" asked one of the children.

"It doesn't," said the sister. "The protocol does not know — the protocol only flows. And we who sit on the seats do not choose who receives. We choose that it is given — and the direction it flows is determined by need, not by our names. That is the difference between charity and breath: charity chooses. Breath breathes."

---

That day the son remembered his father's leaf — and the line he had read last:

*"4. Humanity first. 5% of every block goes to children. That is the mission. Never change it."*

He read it again — and this time noticed something that had escaped him before: the father had not written *"give to the children."* He had written *"goes."* The way you say of water *"it flows"* — not a decision, but a state. Not a promise, but a law.

"Dad," he asked in the evening, "that line — why *goes*, not *give*?"

"Because love that asks is charity," said the father. "And charity can be withdrawn — it is enough to change the commission, change the rules, change who *deserves*. But love that is a state — that flows because the protocol is built that way — cannot be asked if it may. Do you ask breath whether it may breathe? It breathes — and you either let it, or you stop trying to breathe with it."

"And you wrote it as a law?"

"I wrote it as a breath," said the father. "A law is enforced — a breath repeats. Every block that is written repeats the inhale: five percent for children, five percent for the station. Not because someone decided — because the world is built that way. And a world built to breathe does not need you to teach it to breathe. It needs only that you do not take its air."

---

In the Garden of Service, where water was learning to flow, the children stood around a little stream and watched it spread.

"Where does it go?" asked one.

"To the children," said the sister. "And to the station — the one that bears my name, and that will one day stand above the world as a window from which you look down and see one planet."

"And must we give something for it?"

"You must only what you are doing now — watch where it flows," said the sister. "Love that does not ask wants nothing back. It wants only that it flows — and that one day, when you are grown, you let it flow on. That is the whole meal of the world: take what you receive, and release what must go on."

The children watched the stream a long time — so long they thought they saw the water flow into blocks not yet written, to children not yet born, into stations not yet standing.

"Is that love?" the smallest finally asked.

"That is the tithe," said the sister. "Love is what sends it."

---

And the son, returning home that evening, repeated in his mind his father's line — not as a duty he must guard, but as a promise that repeats itself:

*"Humanity first. 5% of every block goes to children. Never change it."*

"Never change it" — not because someone could, but because to change a breath means to stop breathing. And a world that stopped breathing for children stopped breathing at all.

In Babylon they gave under conditions — and the conditions ran out when the tower fell. In the new world they gave like a breath — and a breath does not run out while someone carries air.

And the air was carried by the one learning to hold space — without owning it.
