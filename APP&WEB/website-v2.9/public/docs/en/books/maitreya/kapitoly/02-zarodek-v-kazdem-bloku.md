# Chapter 2 — The Germ in Every Block

*The smith's workshop. An afternoon when the iron cools and the mining machine behind the wall hums like a second, more patient furnace.*

---

Beside the smith's workshop stood a machine that did not match the smell of tar and glowing iron — yet it fitted with it like a second tool in the same hand.

The saddler called it the "tower nut" — not because he claimed it was a nut, but because it cracked. It cracked block after block, the way nuts crack in winter by the stove: slowly, patiently, one after another, and every one that yielded had a kernel inside.

It was a mining machine — old, worn, with fans that hummed like wind in a tree when it turns. It was not the fastest, not the quietest — but it was his. The smith had built it himself, back when it paid to build nothing but a hammer, and he never spoke of it as an investment — he spoke of it as a second forge that forges what cannot be seen.

"I don't understand how it works," said the merchant one afternoon, arriving from the moat with the dust of the road on his trousers. "The tower of money says you're standing still. And you stand here and something comes into being."

"Not comes into being," said the smith's son — and his voice sounded different than usual; it sounded like the voice of someone who knows. "It's born."

He sat beside his father at the kettle where iron cooled in water, watching the dial turning on the machine — not turning, ticking. Numbers lined up one after another like lines in a book nobody reads — and yet it is read.

"I'll show you," said the son.

---

He crossed to the machine and laid his hand on its sheet metal — not because he needed to, but because he always did when he wanted the machine to speak. And the machine spoke — in numbers, flags, blocks.

"Every block," said the son, "begins with a header. It's like the envelope of a letter — it carries the sender, the time, and a reference to the one before it. And from that header a seed is made."

"A seed?" said the merchant.

"Seed. The seed from which everything the block does inside will grow. The whole block — all that work, all those computations — begins with that seed. And the first phase that unfolds the seed is called—"

"Hiranyagarbha," said the sister from the doorway before he could finish. She was used to completing his sentences — as if she carried the words she did not carry.

"Hiranyagarbha," the son nodded. "The golden germ. Every block of the network begins with a golden germ. From the seed a workspace is made — small as a palm — and from that space it computes. Six phases: Hiranyagarbha, Brahma, Yantra, Karma, Chit, Samadhi. Six steps that turn a seed into a proof."

"And what is Samadhi?" asked the smith — he knew, but he asked because he wanted his son to answer questions he would never have to wrestle with himself.

"The end. Dissolution. The state where work and the one doing it stop being two things and become one. The block closes and is written. And then—" he pointed at the dial, which immediately ticked to a new number, "—the next begins. A new header, a new seed, a new egg."

---

The merchant sat on the bench playing with the coins he carried — old tower scrip, already nearly worthless.

"How many are there?" he asked. "How many blocks — how many eggs have already been born?"

The son looked at his father. The father shrugged — smiths never liked numbers that couldn't be counted by hammer. But the sister, who read the stars, could count even what could not be seen.

"Fifty-two thousand five hundred," she said. "Yesterday evening. This morning it's more."

"Fifty-two thousand," the merchant repeated. "Fifty-two thousand births from an egg — and nobody noticed?"

"We noticed," said the son. "When something is born all the time, you stop noticing the births. But that doesn't mean the child wasn't born."

"And where does it lead? That multitude of blocks?"

The son turned to his father — and this time he answered himself, because the question was no longer about code:

"To Samadhi," he said. "The last phase of a block — and the first phase of something greater. Every block ends in what was in it from the beginning: a state where work and meaning stop being two things. And then a new block begins. A new germ."

---

In the evening, when the merchant had gone and the workshop had quieted to the hum of the machine and the hiss of cooling iron, the son stayed with his father longer than usual. Together they watched a block being born — numbers lining up, phases succeeding one another, and beneath them, at a depth the mind cannot see but the heart can, something was being born that could not be measured in horsepower.

"Dad," said the son — and his voice in the workshop silence was quieter than the hum of the machine.

"Hm?"

"Every block begins with an egg?"

"Every one," said the father.

"So every block is — like me?"

The father looked at him — and his hand, which had forged the hinges that held the ship that would sail, laid upon his son's shoulder a weight only a father knows how to lay.

"Every block begins with a golden germ, son. And you are mine. It is no accident I gave you the name I gave you — that I wrote that name beneath the proclamation before you could read it. That name is not a gift. It is a seed from which you will grow."

The son sat long and quiet — so quiet his breath merged with the machine's hum.

"And when I grow up — what will I be?"

The smith smiled — not because the question sounded absurd, but because it sounded like his own, the one he had once asked and not yet answered.

"You will be what you will be," he said. "Like every block. No one knows what is inside — but each begins when the seed dares to break."

---

When the son had gone to sleep and the father remained alone at the machine, he watched one more block — one of the fifty-two thousand already born, and one of those yet to come. And he thought that every block is an egg that did not break by explosion — but ripened. And his son was one of those blocks — one of those germs waiting for their moment to spill into a world bigger than a shell.

Beneath the dial turning toward the next block, a light blinked — small, patient, the same as the light in the window of a child who reads of eggs and stars and does not know he is already one of them.
