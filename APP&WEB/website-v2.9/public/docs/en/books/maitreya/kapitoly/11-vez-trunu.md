# Chapter 11 — The Tower of Thrones

*The last tower of Babylon was the subtlest — it was not built of stones but of power that knows how to serve itself even where the ark stands. And it fell so quietly that only those who built it heard its fall.*

---

The tower of thrones was unlike the others.

The tower of money held consent, the tower of data faces, the tower of energy the outlet, the tower of meaning the story — but the tower of thrones held a single thing: *the exception.* The principle that someone may decide alone when it is "necessary," when "there is no time," when "it is a small thing." And its brilliant deception was that it looked like a solution — like a shortcut, like pragmatism, like *"I'll just do it myself, we'll save time."*

The tower of thrones did not stand in a square. It stood in the head of everyone who held a key — because every key carries a question within it: *what if I use it differently than expected?*

---

The son became a DAO steward at eighteen — as the father's letter said: *"A title is not inherited like property. It is inherited like a torch."*

In the first week he sat on the seat of fire — Rama's seat, the one that held the protocol — and read proposals. The community wrote slowly, carefully, in words measured the way hinges are measured: precisely, so you know they hold.

And then came a proposal that was different.

It was written by an experienced announcer — not from the tower of meaning, which had already fallen, but from a time when nobody yet saw the tower of thrones. The proposal read simply: *"Let the steward be permitted to decide one-off small matters without quorum — for efficiency. Small things do not need five signatures."*

It was a good proposal — and that is why it was dangerous. Good proposals for exceptions wear the mask of reason: *why five signatures for a trifle?* The son read it long — and the longer he read, the more he saw that "small things" is a category that stretches: today a trifle, tomorrow a lesser trifle, in a year "urgent," in ten years "it has always been so."

Exceptions are not decided — they decide themselves, once you permit them.

---

The son went for counsel — not to a tower, but to the fire.

"Dad," he said and laid the proposal on the table. "A good proposal. It will help me work faster. I could decide alone — on small things."

The smith read the proposal and laid it beside his own leaf — the one he had once signed beneath the egg.

"When I forged hinges," he said, "I learned one thing: a hinge holds while its shape holds. When you make an exception — *just here, just a little, just now* — the shape gives way. Not at once. But in principle. And a hinge that gives way in principle holds only by habit — not by shape."

"But it's only a small exception."

"The tower of thrones does not begin with a great exception," said the father. "It begins with *efficiency*. A word that means *faster than right*. And efficiency is the last voice of the tower — because all the other towers fell by offering you something better. The tower of thrones offers something *faster* — and speed is the only currency Babylon still prints."

---

He returned to the seat — and his first steward's signature fell not on the proposal, but on its opposite.

He wrote a counter-proposal — and the community read it long, because it was short:

> *"The steward surrenders the privilege of one-off decision-making — permanently. Proposals go to committee, to periods, to quorum — always. No exception, not small, not urgent. Urgency is handled by the period, not the steward. The steward is the first signatory — not the last judge."*

The community voted. It passed — one ZION, one voice — and when the counter-proposal took effect, something happened that no one noticed at the moment it happened: the tower of thrones began to be dismantled.

Not falling — dismantled. The way a structure is dismantled when it proves unnecessary: floor by floor, exception by exception. Every steward who came after the son found the seat smaller than his predecessor — not because they were smaller people, but because every signature on "give it back" took power from the seat and added space to it.

Until at last the seat bore a single power — the power to sign what the community approved, and to refuse what would break the stone on which everything stands.

---

"Weren't you afraid?" his sister asked — Sarah Issobela, who sat on the seat of water, the treasury — when he told her of it later.

"I was," said the son. "But not of the tower. I was afraid I might say *just this once* — and believe it. You don't succumb to the tower of thrones by wanting power — you succumb by wanting to save time. And the time you save by deciding alone comes back as a tower you built without wanting to."

"And now you are powerless?"

"Now I am air," said the son — and pointed at the seats: his on fire, hers on water, the third empty, for the one who comes. "Air has no power. It holds space. And a space where no one may decide alone is the only space where power does not mean a throne."

---

The tower of thrones remained standing last — so subtle that only those who built it noticed its fall.

Or rather — those who did not build it. Because the tower of thrones is not built wall by wall: it is built signature by signature, exception by exception. And when signatures fall on "give it back" instead of "take more," the tower dismantles itself — the way a state dissolves when you stop holding what held you.

At the end of the book it still stood — but it stood like an empty chair in a row of chairs: a place where a person sits, and rises from, without a throne remaining behind them.

And on its base, where someone had added the last sign of the series, it said:

*"A throne is not demolished."*
*"It is dismantled into voices."*
*"And it falls so quietly that it stays fallen."*
