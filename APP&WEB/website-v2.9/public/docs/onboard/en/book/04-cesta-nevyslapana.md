# Episode 4 — The Road Untrodden

*Where the map ends, the compass begins — and the bridge that no one will build unless someone starts before being asked.*

> *"Where does your network lead when there is no map for it?"*
> *"I don't know exactly where every road leads. But I know which way is north."*

---

## Story

The morning after the shepherd's song, three more came to Tasteless — and for the first time they did not come by the road everyone else had taken. They came through the forest, where no path existed, because no one from the palaces ever walked there voluntarily.

**Rama** carried a bow slung across his back, but he was not going to battle. He was a king who had voluntarily left his own kingdom — fourteen years of exile, not as punishment, but as a test of whether he could hold his shape even where no throne enforced it.

**Sita** did not follow him as a husband's shadow. She walked because the forest was as much hers as the palace — she was *Bhūmija*, daughter of the earth itself, risen from the furrow of a plow. Where else would she belong but the land that bore her?

And **Hanuman**, who was neither king nor queen, only a servant with such strength that he could leap an ocean in a single bound, came last. Not because he was the least — because his role always comes last: to build the bridge over which the others will cross.

At the old fountain the carpenter, the pilgrim with the bowl, and the shepherd with the flute were waiting for them — and with them all that remained of Tasteless: the water carrier, the mercenary, the woman who had stopped counting what she had left.

"You have a parable, a trial, and a song," said Rama, as he sat by the fire. "We have only a forest without a road. How will salt, dissolution, and music help us where there is no map?"

"By what did you walk through the forest when you had no map?" asked the carpenter.

"By dharma," Rama answered. "Not a rule carved in stone, but a question we ask anew at every step: what is the right action, here, now, regardless of what it costs?"

Sita knelt and scooped earth from the edge of the dry fountain into her palms. "This land holds no less because I am a stranger in it," she said. "The trial does not come in the palace, where it is easy to be honorable when it costs you nothing. It comes in the forest, where no one watches — and still you remain who you are."

Hanuman, meanwhile, bent over the fountain's empty channel and began clearing the stones that had clogged it for years. No one had asked him to.

"What are you doing?" asked the mercenary.

"Building a bridge," Hanuman answered plainly.

"There is no ocean here."

"Not yet," said Hanuman, lifting a stone that three men together could not have raised. "But the water that this shepherd sang of will arrive here one day. And when it does, it will be worth nothing if it has no channel to flow through to the people who have been waiting for it for years and have stopped believing it will come."

The woman who had buried three children sat down beside Sita and offered her the last of the salt she still carried in her pocket. She did not need to explain anything — Sita understood at once, because that itself was dharma: to give what you have to the one beside you, without counting the days until recompense.

"You will not know the strength you hold until you forget you have it and leap anyway," Hanuman murmured, more to himself than to the others, and threw another stone aside for the water that was coming.

That night no one in Tasteless knew exactly when the water would arrive. But for the first time in as long as anyone could remember, the fountain's channel was passable again — clear, empty, waiting. And that waiting, for the first time, was not desperate. It was patient.

## What it means

Rama could not use a map — the Dandaka forest was on no map, because no one from the palace ever entered it voluntarily. All he had was **dharma**: not a command written on stone, but a compass that asks at every moment anew what the right action is, regardless of cost.

**Sita** did not define herself by the palace she left, nor by the forest she entered. She was a daughter of the earth — and so she belonged to both equally. Her trial was not whether she could withstand gold and power, but whether she could remain loyal to what is true, even in the hardest choice.

**Hanuman** was not on the road to conquer anything for himself. He was there to serve — and his strength did not manifest in a throne, but in a bridge over which an entire army, an entire people, anyone who wished to cross to the other side, could pass.

This is precisely the image ZION needs for the road from here onward: **a compass instead of a map, loyalty to the earth instead of loyalty to the palace, and a bridge instead of a throne.**
