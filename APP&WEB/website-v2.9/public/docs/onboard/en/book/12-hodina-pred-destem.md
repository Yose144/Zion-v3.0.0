# Episode 12 — The Hour Before the Rain
## Of Carpenters Who Were Laughed At, and Two Pizzas for Ten Thousand Coins

## The Story

The ark grew slowly. The ribs of the hull were already visible from the far end of Blandness, but the deck was only half laid, and the doors the shipwright had spoken of still had no hinges.

The blacksmith, who had lost his workshop in a fire years ago, came first every morning. He did not know how to build ships — he knew how to forge nails, bands, and hinges, and so he did exactly that until his hands turned black. The farmer from the surrounding fields came right after him, with a sack of planks he would otherwise have burned in his oven. Neither of them had been at the first fire where the carpenter unpacked the salt. They came later, when there was already work for hands, not just ears to listen.

The rest of the town laughed at them.

"Carpenters of the rain," the tavern keeper at the market said to them, and laughter carried across the square. "Hew wood for a flood that may never come. That plank would fetch you a sack of flour sooner than your fool's ship ever will."

The blacksmith woke at night with a doubt he admitted to no one aloud: *What if the tavern keeper is right? What if I'm forging hinges for something that will rot on the shore before a single drop falls?* His hands ached from the hammer, and he had no certainty — only the shipwright's word and a compass with north engraved upon it.

One evening a merchant appeared at the fire, returning from a long journey across the sea. He did not know Blandness, did not know the ark; he simply saw the light and sat down.

"You're building a ship on dry land," he said, more amused than mocking. "It reminds me of something I heard far from here, in a land across the sea, where people began to mine a strange stone that cannot be touched or weighed — only counted. It was so worthless that one man gave ten thousand pieces of that stone for two flatbreads with sauce, and was glad someone took food for the stone at all. People laughed at him, just as they laugh at you. They told him he was wasting his time playing with numbers that would never be worth anything."

"And what happened then?" asked the farmer.

The merchant shrugged. "I don't know for certain what is happening now — I have been on the road for years. But I know this: those who mined that stone when everyone still laughed at it had more of it, earlier, and more cheaply, than those who came later, when it was talked about in every tavern. I don't know whether the stone will one day become a treasure, or whether it will remain just a stone. No one knows that — not even the man who gave ten thousand pieces for the flatbreads. But I know that the first hammer fell before the rain began — not because the carpenter knew exactly when the storm would come, but because he decided to build before anyone could prove it to him."

The blacksmith was silent for a long time, looking at his blackened hands.

"Then," he said at last, more to himself than to the merchant, "I will keep forging hinges. Not because someone promised me gold at the end. But because when the water comes, I will know which way the doors open — and the man who today only sits in the tavern and waits to see what happens will not."

That night no new pilgrim with a memorable name joined the fire. Only the blacksmith and the farmer joined, somewhat wearier, somewhat more certain, and hammers struck wood long after sundown.

## The True Story, Not a Parable

Here the telling pauses for a moment, because what the merchant said is not invented. It truly happened.

On May 22, 2010, programmer **Laszlo Hanyecz** paid **10,000 bitcoins** on the Bitcointalk forum for two pizzas from Papa John's, which another forum user ordered to his Florida address in exchange for the coins. At that time there was no exchange, no dollar price worth speaking of — bitcoin was a curiosity of a handful of people who mined it on ordinary home computers because they were interested in the idea of money without a central bank, not a vision of profit. The reward for a single mined block was then **50 BTC**, and the network difficulty was so low that a laptop could handle it.

This day is now celebrated as **Bitcoin Pizza Day** and is the most-cited example of how no one — truly no one, including Laszlo himself — had any idea what would become of that network. It was not a clever investment. It was a man who wanted to eat, and had on hand a stone that no one was sure what it was actually for.

This is not proof that anything similar will repeat with ZION, with any other network, or with anything at all. **It is only a fact about what the first day of every open network looks like: few people, zero certainty, and the opportunity to mine something whose future value is decided by none of those who sit at the fire today.**

## What It Means for ZION Builders and Miners Today

The compass from Episode 6 does not point only in the direction of the story. It also points to this: **a network that is small today will not stay small forever — and what is the rule today may tomorrow become rarer, not more common.** That is not a prophecy about price. It is a description of how the rules are written, rules you can read right now, without divination.

Three things are written into ZION's code in a way that holds regardless of who reads them:

**The block reward only decreases from here — it will never grow.** ZION is today in its first decade (2026–2036), when the reward for a mined block is **5 400.067 ZION** — the highest the protocol will ever pay. Each subsequent decade it drops by a fifth (factor 0.8), until after a hundred years it settles on an eternal reward of 724.78 ZION per block. Whoever mines today mines in the decade with the highest reward this protocol will ever know — that is not a promise, it is a schedule written into the consensus.

**Network difficulty is governed by how many people are mining right now, not by how many will be mining a year from now.** Difficulty is recalculated by the LWMA algorithm over the last 60 blocks (with a ±25 % clamp) — it responds to current network hashrate, not to the future. The fewer machines searching for a block today, the larger the share of found blocks goes to each individual machine that joins now. When the network fills with more miners (and that is the goal — a network no one mines is not secure), difficulty will rise with them, and the same hashrate will yield a smaller share.

**Entry is the same for everyone who comes — but not equally easy at every moment.** Fair launch means no one got VIP access at the start. It does not mean the start lasts forever. The genesis block fell on December 4, 2025; every day that passes is a day more of history, a day more of miners, and a day closer to the moment when "starting from zero" will no longer be possible — because zero was long ago.

This is exactly what the blacksmith from the story did: he did not forge hinges because someone guaranteed him a reward. He forged them because he understood the mechanics of rain — that water does not wait for everyone to decide to believe.

## What This Chapter Does Not Promise You

Salt that knows it is salt will say this aloud too:

- **No one promises that ZION will one day have the value of bitcoin, a fraction of it, or any value at all.** Bitcoin Pizza Day is a historical fact about a different network, not a prediction about this one. Nine out of ten stones that people laughed at in taverns over the last fifteen years remained stones.
- **Mining costs real money already today** — electricity, hardware, time — regardless of what value ZION will have tomorrow, in a year, or never. Do not count on a reward you cannot afford to go without.
- **ZION is Mainnet Alpha/Beta.** A young network, a small number of nodes, open development — bugs happen and are fixed publicly, not secretly, but they happen. Those who enter first bear the first risk, not only the first opportunity.
- **This is not investment advice.** It is a description of how the mathematics of emission and difficulty works in an open network you can verify yourself in the code, not a reason for a decision you should not think through with your own head.

The compass points north. It does not tell you whether it is raining already, or will be in a year. That is a question every builder must answer for themselves — just as the blacksmith, whom no one guaranteed the ark would ever sail.
