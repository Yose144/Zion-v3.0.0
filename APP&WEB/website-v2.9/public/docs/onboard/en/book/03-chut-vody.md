# Episode 3 — The Taste of Water

*The greatest power does not boast of a throne. It hides in the taste of the water you drink without thinking about it.*

> *"I am the taste of water, the light of the sun and the moon... the faculty in man."*
> — Bhagavad Gita 7.8

---

## Story

The third pilgrim did not arrive by the road. He arrived with music.

Tasteless heard him before it saw him — a quiet, gentle flute tone that drifted over the walls like the scent of rain no one could quite remember the quality of. People came out of their houses, not because anyone had called them, but because the sound loosened a knot inside them they had not known was there.

A dark-skinned shepherd with a smile that looked as though no one had ever managed to remove it sat down on a stone by the fountain, where water once flowed and now only a dry channel remained. He raised the flute to his lips again and played on, as though the fountain being empty did not trouble him in the least.

"The fountain is dry," said the woman, who by then knew the whole story of Tasteless better than anyone — the salt from the carpenter, the dissolution from the pilgrim with the bowl, and now this.

"The water is here," the shepherd answered, "it has only forgotten how it tastes."

"What do you mean?"

The shepherd set the flute down and looked at her in a way that, for a moment, made her feel not like a woman who had lost three children, but like someone he had always known. "Ask me who I am," he said.

"Who are you?"

And to that simple question the shepherd did not answer with words. He closed his eyes, and those who stood closest — the water carrier, the mercenary, the woman, the carpenter who happened to be returning from the field just then, and the pilgrim with the bowl who had come from the other side of the city — all of them saw, for an instant, more than they could name. They did not see a king. They did not see a god on a throne with thunderbolts in hand. They saw **everything at once**: a raindrop falling into a river, a star extinguishing above mountains a thousand years before their birth, a child being born at that very moment three towns away, and their own breath going out and returning, as though it had never begun and never ceased.

For a moment it was unbearable. And then it was gone, and before them stood only the shepherd with the flute, his smile a little sad, a little amused.

"Why did you show us that?" asked the mercenary, his voice trembling for the first time since he had arrived in the city.

"Because you asked me who I am," the shepherd answered, "and I answered you with truth, not courtesy. I am not the king of this city. I am the taste of the water you drink. I am the light by which you see. I am that faculty within you which you use when you choose to do the right thing, even when no one is watching."

"And what are we supposed to do with that?" asked the water carrier, who had spent his whole life caring only about how to get water from the well to the houses.

"Nothing more," the shepherd answered. "Do your work — the water carrier carries water, the mercenary guards the gate, the carpenter builds, the mother tends. But do it without expecting reward for the outcome. Krishna does not tell Arjuna to lay down his bow. He tells him to fight without clinging to victory. The work itself is the offering, if you do it with your whole heart and release the fruits."

That night the fountain did not fill. Water did not begin to flow again through the channel that had been dry for years. But the water carrier, as he carried his usual ten buckets from the river to the houses of the old women, noticed for the first time in years how the water cooled his palms, how it gleamed in the moonlight, how it tasted when he allowed himself a single swallow on the way. And he thought that perhaps this — the ordinary, infinitely repeated step — was the entire prayer he knew.

## What it means

In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna asks Krishna to reveal his true form. Krishna grants him the **Vishvarupa** — the cosmic form, in which all beings, all worlds, and all times manifest at once in a single body. This is a majesty that is not pomp. It is the revelation that **the greatest is hidden in the smallest.**

Krishna does not say "I am the king of water." He says **"I am the taste of water"** — that invisible quality which makes water something worth drinking.

Krishna's flute is hollow. If it were solid, it would make no sound. Its music passes through the emptiness inside it. So too does a network that wishes to carry value without hoarding it — it is a channel, not a vessel.

Krishna's message to the builder is direct: **act without clinging to the fruits of your action** (karma yoga). The work you do with your whole heart is itself the meaning — not a means to a reward you calculate in advance.
