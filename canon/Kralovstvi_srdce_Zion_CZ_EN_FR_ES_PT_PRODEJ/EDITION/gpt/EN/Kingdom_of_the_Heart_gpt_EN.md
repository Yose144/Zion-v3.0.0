### A word before the second voyage

In the first part we followed the Carpenter from the workshop in Nazareth to the empty tomb and to the fire on the shore. From here the story widens across the ages. It does not say that the same historical man walked the world for two thousand years. The Carpenter who returns in Bezechuti, on Ararat or beside a future drawing of a ship is a figure of this legend: he carries on a way of working, not a historical proof.

In this part the ship does not move by itself. Someone has to sand a peg, admit a mistake, listen to hosts, hold the lantern and sometimes also say: "We do not know that yet." The Carpenter stays with these tasks — as long as he can, and then he hands over the tools.

## Chapter 10 · Navicella — the ship learns to sail

### Story

In the morning, when the fish had been eaten on the shore and the coals in the fire had cooled, the Carpenter took the wet oars out of the boat one by one. The first had a crack by the handle. The second was sound. The third had warped so badly that in a stroke it would catch on its own water.

Simon pointed at three empty places on the bench. "More will come. Who will tell them how this boat is steered?"

"You," said the Carpenter.

Simon laughed. "Only yesterday I was saying that I do not know you."

"And yet this morning you came to the fire." The Carpenter handed him the cracked oar. "First repair it. Then show them where it can break."

Simon turned the oar over as if looking for a way out inside it. "If I show them the crack, they will not believe me."

"If you hide it, they will believe you until the moment it drops out of their hands."

Together they sanded the edge. The Carpenter fitted a thin wooden brace to the handle and let Simon drive the last wedge. He struck too hard and the wood split. Simon lowered his head.

"I will not write that in the journal," he said.

"Then I will write it there," answered the Carpenter and handed him the graver. "But your name will be beside the repair."

They launched the boat only after noon. No one named it the captain's. Whoever came aboard got work: one rowed, the second watched the seams, the third read the horizon. The Carpenter sat on the stern, but he left the tiller to Simon.

"When you are tired, someone will take your place," he said. "A boat that only one hand can steer is not finished yet."

In the distance a wind rose. Simon looked back at the Carpenter.

"Watch the water," said the Carpenter. "I am watching you."

> *The oar holds again because someone admitted where it cracked;*
> *the tiller is handed over so that the boat can carry more than one.*

## Chapter 11 · Bezechuti — the salt that must dissolve

### Story

They reached Bezechuti when the first rain had washed the dust off the road. The Carpenter walked beside the cart, on which he carried two things: a clay cup with salt and a piece of cedar that would not fit into any crate.

The city was not destroyed. Only everything in it was offered on a condition. The tower of money issued consent, the tower of data wanted faces, the tower of energy sold current, the tower of meaning told people what they were to think, and the tower of the throne offered an exception to every holder of the key.

The Carpenter sat down under a fig tree with the water-carriers, a baker woman and an old mercenary. He set the cup in the middle of the circle.

"Is that all?" asked the carrier. "Salt?"

"No," said the Carpenter. "There is also bread. And your hands."

The baker woman laughed. "We have had hands for a long time. Bread still costs us money."

"Then today we will bake one loaf together."

None of them knew exactly how the city was to change. They had only flour, water, salt and an oven whose door did not close. The Carpenter first repaired the hinge. Then he noticed that the baker woman was putting in too much wood, and he did not lecture her — he stood beside her and waited until she asked him.

"How do you know the oven is ready?"

"By how the flame behaves," he said. "Not by how much more wood you push into it."

In the evening they divided the bread among the people waiting at the market. Someone brought more flour. Another offered a place by the well. The tower still stood and its bells still rang. Only under the fig tree more people began to gather than at its gate.

"Will it fall?" asked a boy.

"That is not our work," answered the Carpenter. "Our work is to stop needing it to let us bake."

Then he laid the piece of cedar on the trestles. It was not a throne and not a weapon. It was the first part of a keel that the city was to build itself.

> *A tower does not have to fall for people to find a way out;*
> *sometimes shared bread and a repaired hinge are enough.*

## Chapter 12 · The fire of three pilgrims

### Story

When the first part of the keel had been worked, three groups of pilgrims sat down by the fire. The first was a prince who had left the palace and learned that wealth cannot silence suffering. In this legend they called him **the Buddha**. The second carried a bamboo flute and smiled at children; the shepherd **Krishna** sat down beside him. The third came with **Rama**, with **Sita**, who walked barefoot, and with **Hanuman**, who each time bent down for a stone from the road.

"Which of you is first?" asked the smith.

"That depends on where you are looking from," said the Buddha and set his bowl on the ground. "If you look at suffering, first is the one who sees it — and does not mistake compassion for the owning of another person."

"If you look at work," said Krishna and put down the flute, "first is the one who does it with the whole heart, but does not tie the result around his neck like a trophy."

"And if you look at a bridge," said Sita, "first is not the one who stands in the middle. It is the one who leaves the other room to cross." Hanuman meanwhile was clearing stones from the path to the water; Rama held the lamp for him.

The Carpenter was silent and fed the fire. The boy noticed that the pilgrims did not treat him as a teacher. They spoke with him as with a man who must get up again tomorrow and work again. No one claimed that these roads really crossed in history; at this fire they met as images that may laughingly question one another.

When the fire burned lower, the smith pointed at three different woods they had brought: cedar, bamboo and olive.

"Shall I make one pillar out of them?"

"No," said the Carpenter. "Let each grow in its own way. Just look at how you join them so that they carry a shared roof."

In the morning the smith made three pegs — each from a different wood — and joined with them pieces that would not otherwise have been chosen together. The Carpenter tested them with his palm. One gave way. Everyone looked at it.

"Good," said the Carpenter. "Now we know which joint needs repair."

Around the fire no new truth was proclaimed that was to replace all the others. After breakfast each pilgrim set out on his own road. What remained of them was a question that the smith wrote on a board: *What if unity resembles a roof — not one wood, but joints that can be inspected?*

> *Three voices do not melt into one;*
> *they learn to hold the same fire.*

## Chapter 13 · The ark that he did not build alone

### Story

On the square in Bezechuti lay planks, and around them people who were not yet sure whether their work would change anything. The Carpenter drew the outline of a hull in the clay. Then he handed the square to the smith.

"You will determine where the pegs go."

The smith shook his head. "You are the carpenter."

"I know how wood bends. You know how iron behaves in water. Without your measuring I would invent a certainty."

A farmer brought wood, the baker woman pitch, the women from the well clay for the floor. No one gave everything. Each brought what he could do without, and also said what he could not do without. The Carpenter wrote that second list down as carefully as the first.

When it came to the doors, one of the wealthier merchants proposed a smaller side entrance for those who had contributed most.

"And the main door?" asked Sita.

"For everyone else."

The Carpenter did not look at him sternly. He only turned the drawing so that everyone could see that both doors led into the same narrow passage and that one of them would be blocked at high tide.

"We do not know that yet," he said. "First we will test it with water."

The merchant frowned, but he helped bring the bucket. The test showed that his door would indeed jam. The repair was proposed by the children, who noticed that the hinge needed more play.

The Carpenter accepted their solution. "A ship that has room for everyone must also be able to hear everyone."

The ark grew, but it was not finished. Sometimes a plank had to be taken off and repaired; sometimes a whole day brought nothing visible. The Carpenter stayed at the work. When someone addressed him as "builder", he answered: "Today I am the one holding the plank. Tomorrow someone else may hold it."

At dusk two words appeared on the keel, carved from the inside so that no one could use them as a banner:

> **THE KINGDOM OF THE HEART**

> *The ark did not grow from one hand;*
> *it grew from work that people allowed each other.*

## Chapter 14 · The first ray — the lantern the Carpenter carved

### Story

The ark pulled away from the shore at dawn. Not everyone came aboard. Some stayed in Bezechuti to repair roofs, others to watch the well, and others said they would decide once they saw where the ship was sailing.

The Carpenter did not look back at them as at cowards. He wrote their names on a list of the people on the shore, so that the ship could return to them.

"Why are you writing them down?" asked Lumi.

"So that we do not confuse who came aboard with whom we still want to speak to."

On the third night the water lifted the hull again. It was not a Kinneret storm but a long swell that raised and released the ark like a child's palm. The Carpenter walked the deck and checked every joint. At one seam he felt water under his finger.

"It leaks here."

One of the sailors wanted to smear the whole seam with pitch at once. The Carpenter stopped him. "First we find out where it comes from." They shone the lamp. The water was not coming from outside; it ran down from a barrel that someone had lashed too high. They repaired the strap, dried the floor and left the seam uncovered until morning so that they could check it again.

In the morning Lumi stood at the narrow window. In the hour before dawn she pointed into the mist. Where before there had been nothing but grey, a thin, golden-blue band cut through the air.

"The shore?" she asked.

"Not yet," answered the Carpenter. "Only light."

He took an offcut of cedar and a shard of ships' lamp glass. All day he worked at the table while the others cut canvas and stacked tools. He carved no sign and no throne; he made a lantern small enough for a child to carry.

When he handed it to her, Lumi looked inside.

"It is empty."

"For now."

Together they held it up to the ray. The glass divided it, the cedar held it, and the light stayed quiet.

"Will it show me the way?"

"No. It will show you what is — and what could be. You will choose the way."

> *The ray did not show the mainland;*
> *it showed where to begin looking.*

## Chapter 15 · Lumi's house and the seed by the river

### Story

On the slope below Ararat stood no palace. There stood a house with a stone hearth and two doors that opened into the same room. One led to the road. The other to the garden.

The Carpenter fitted a cedar lintel above the door. On its lower edge he left the name of the ship carved; on the visible side he left room for palms. When Lumi handed him a nail, she asked why he was not adding a lock.

"Because we store nothing in the house that would give us the right to keep people inside."

"And if someone leaves?"

"We leave them a road."

That day Sarah came from the sea. She brought an empty alabaster vessel whose bottom had a hairline crack. She put it on the table and wanted nothing for handing it over.

"Miriam told me not to fill it," she said. "But she did not say what to do with it afterwards."

Lumi raised the vessel to the lantern. In the crack a tiny seed had caught. It was not miraculous. It was not rare. It could have come from any garden that once stood by a road.

"Shall we plant it?" she asked.

"If you want," said Sarah. "When it grows, it will not be ours."

The Carpenter handed Lumi a small trowel, but he did not dig for her. Sarah loosened the soil, Lumi watered the seed drop by drop. Meanwhile the Carpenter cleared a blocked channel so that the water would not stay only at the threshold.

"May we name it?" asked Lumi.

"Ask it when it grows," answered the Carpenter. "For now a place is enough for it."

In the evening the Carpenter wrote three things on a wooden tablet: where the seed lies, who planted it and where the water flows to it. Under the tablet he left empty room for another entry.

"Is this the house of names?" asked Sarah.

"No," he said. "This is a house that tries to remember only what is needed — and to forget claims that no one made."

> *An empty vessel received a seed;*
> *an open house left it a door out.*

## Chapter 16 · The bridge must return

### Story

From the house a road led to a strait. On the far bank lay an island where things were recorded in another language. Between the banks stood a bridge, but its middle arch ended in the air.

"Is it broken?" asked Lumi.

"It is drawn," said the Carpenter.

At the foot of the bridge waited five guardians. Each held one key. The youngest of them pointed at a drawing on which it was written that the bridge would one day carry a load.

"Then let us build it," he said.

"Before we begin," said the Carpenter, "tell me how we bring the load back if the far bank does not answer."

The young man was silent. One of the guardians admitted that he did not know the answer. A second pointed at the return plan; a third found a mistake in it. Lumi lowered the lantern and lit exactly the place where the doors were to open, but not the place where the means would return.

"So what now?" she asked.

"For now we carry nothing across," said the Carpenter. "First we run a test with an empty cart."

They crossed dry. Then they tried a small load, checked the record and brought it back to the original bank. Only when everyone could read what had happened did they nail a sign to the unfinished arch:

> **This bridge is not yet open. Here is the reason.**

"The sign is not beautiful," said the young man.

"It is useful," answered the Carpenter. "Beauty that hides a hole is only a sail above the water."

Lumi looked at both banks. "When the bridge is finished, who will own it?"

"Those who maintain it will not be the owners of the bank," said one of the guardians. "They will be answerable for the crossing."

The Carpenter handed him a hammer. "Then begin by writing down also what did not work."

> *An arch is not a bridge until the load crosses there and back;*
> *a sign with a truthful "not yet" also protects the road.*

## Chapter 17 · The table without a head

### Story

When they returned to the house, six women were waiting by it. Each brought something from a place she knew: a handful of clay, a coal in a shell, a knot of palm fibre, a ring of flax cord, a leaf of an old tree, and a small mirror in which the stars were reflected.

The Carpenter brought the seventh thing: an unfinished board.

"Where will the head be?" he asked.

"Nowhere," said the woman of the ring.

"And who decides here?"

"First we listen. Then we look for a proposal we can live with. If there is none, we come back to it."

The Carpenter laid the board on the trestles. The wood was warped; one arm rose. He could have straightened it himself, but he did not. He invited the women each to show where the table needed support. Three of them proposed different legs. One said that instead of a table she had wanted benches. Another objected that six places were not all six gardens and that the seventh was not occupied.

The smith was already about to start cutting. The Carpenter stopped him.

"What if for now we build a model?"

On a small table they spread out stones and strings. They moved one stone, then another. They changed the shape of the circle until each place touched the others and none was in the middle.

"And that empty seat?" asked Lumi.

One woman laid the lantern beside the place, not a crown.

"When someone comes, we will ask whether they want to sit. We will not tell them that we were waiting for them and that therefore they owe us something."

The Carpenter bowed. "Then your table is finished, even though the board is not."

"And yours?"

"Mine is a tool. The table is yours."

In the night a ring of charcoal remained on the wood. In the morning no one covered it with varnish. They left it as a reminder of a model that could be rebuilt.

> *A table has no head because a decision has no owner;*
> *the empty place waits — but owes nothing to anyone.*

## Chapter 18 · The window that does not fly

### Story

On the top of the cliff stood a small building with a single window turned to the sky. The Carpenter did not help to build it; he came late and brought only a piece of paper on which he had drawn a tree.

Inside sat Lumi and an old woman who had once read the stars. On the table lay calculations of a circle in space, a schedule of care for the body, and several sheets with questions to which no one knew the answer.

"Could there one day be a station here?" asked Lumi.

"Could be," said the Carpenter. "But a possibility is not an address."

"And when it is?"

"Then someone will be there to water a plant. Not before."

Lumi looked through the window. On the horizon shone points that could be ships' lights, stars, or only a reflection in the glass. The woman showed her a calculation of a rotating ring. The Carpenter looked it over, turned it a quarter of a turn and found an error in the scale.

"I overlooked that," said the woman.

"Then we know it now," said the Carpenter. "Write it on the first page, not the last."

On the paper there was also the name **Issobella**. Lumi touched it with her finger.

"Is it a promise?"

"It is a name in a story and a research question in the documents," said the woman. "Nothing up there yet."

The Carpenter closed the window so that the rain would not come in. "I am not a builder of heaven. I can make doors and repair a table."

"Then why did you come here?"

"Because someone here is trying to build something and needed a second pair of eyes."

They stayed until evening. Then the Carpenter walked down on foot, while Lumi stayed with the papers and the lantern.

> *The window showed heaven, not a finished station;*

## Chapter 20 · Tres Marías — a model and real wood

### Story

In the docks by the Atlantic a wooden model of a ship stood on a table. It was small enough for one man to lift and large enough to show where the sail would catch on the mast.

The Carpenter ran his finger along the hull. Beside him stood the captain Inés, Lumen with a folder, and two women who knew the sea better than all their maps.

"Here will be Santa María la Mayor," said the Pilgrim. "And there the second, for the Pacific. The third has no keel yet."

"Then why do we already call her by name?" asked Lumen.

"So that we remember for whom the design may be," said Inés. "Not so that we pretend the ship exists."

The Carpenter tried a miniature sail. The first cut tore. The second was too heavy. The third caught on a crosspiece and turned the model across the table.

"That would not work on the ocean," he said.

"It is only a model," someone objected.

"That is precisely why we can find the mistake now."

Lumen wrote down who said what and what needed verifying. Inés added that a route through ports is not determined only by the shortest line. Hosts, season, safety and local rules belong on the map as much as the depth of the water.

## Chapter 21 · The chronicler who let the error be seen

### Story

Lumen kept the ship's log so carefully that there was no room in it for a spilled drop. Then she noticed that there was less water in the tank than the log claimed.

Before going to the captain she found the Carpenter by the tool chest. He was repairing a wooden frame and had two measures in front of him.

"One of them is wrong," she said.

"Which one?"

"I do not know. I used both of them on different days."

"Then write that in the book."

"But the record will look as if I am incompetent."

The Carpenter laid the measures side by side. One was cut short and its zero began elsewhere. "If you write only the number, people will think the measure was certain. If you write its uncertainty as well, they will know what they can use."

Lumen went to Inés. She admitted the error in the quantity of water and the captain changed the plan so that the supplies would be measured again. They corrected the record with an addition: date, original figure, new figure, source and signature. No line was erased.

Then the Carpenter found his own old drawing of the keel. It contained an angle that he had estimated by eye years before.

"I drew this without measuring," he said.

Lumen looked at him. "Do you want to rewrite it?"

"No. I want to add what I did not know then."

At the lower edge he wrote the date and a note: *The original angle was an estimate. After the test it turned out that the joint needs a brace.* Beneath it he put his name.

"Do you still make mistakes?" asked Lumi.

"I still measure," said the Carpenter.

> *Not a crossing out, but an addition;*
> *a record holds even when it shows where it went wrong.*

## Chapter 22 · The letter that does not prescribe a road

### Story

On board the red ship the Pilgrim found a scroll that someone had tucked behind a beam. The paper was ordinary; the string around it was tied so that it could be untied without a knife.

"It is for Surta," said the Carpenter. "But no one has to give it to him if they do not want to."

Lumi asked why he did not send the letter by the ship's network.

"Because sometimes it is better to hand words to a person than to send them into the distance."

The Pilgrim unrolled the paper. There was no map on it and no list of orders. It said:

> *Son,*
> *I cannot promise you that the road will be calm. I can leave you the tools I learned to hold, and the room to decide what you will do with them.*
> *You are not obliged to continue where I began. If you find a better road, show it to us. If you decide to leave, no one will charge you for returning.*
> *When you ask what you will inherit, try also to ask what you can pass on without a debt.*

"Is that all?" asked Lumi.

"That is enough," said the Carpenter. "A letter that decides for its recipient is not a letter. It is an order."

The Pilgrim tied the scroll again and put it in his satchel. He did not say when he would hand it over, nor whether the addressee would read it. The Carpenter did not ask for a message.

In the evening they sat by the fire. The Carpenter held in his hand a small boat he had carved from the rest of a plank. He handed it to the child sitting on the other side.

"You can play with it," he said. "You do not have to give it a name."

"And if I break it?"

"Then we will see whether it can be repaired."

> *The letter did not show a single road;*
> *it left the recipient the right to answer or not to answer.*

## Chapter 23 · The fourth vessel — questions first

### Story

On the table lay the largest drawing of all. A hull for a hundred people, decks, a greenhouse, a workshop, room for seeds and books. In the upper corner it said: **STUDY — NOT A PRODUCTION PLAN**.

The Carpenter put on the spectacles he had borrowed from the cartographer. He read slowly. Someone had already written how much water would be needed; no one had yet stated how the filter would change after five years. Someone had drawn a seed archive; it was not clear who would verify that the seeds still germinate. Someone had proposed an auxiliary fuel; no one had calculated what happens when more of it is consumed than can be replenished.

"This is not a ship," he said.

"We know," answered the Pilgrim. "It is a study."

"Good. Then we can ask it questions before we make a ship of it."

At the edge he added questions: who may come aboard, who is responsible for rescue, how decisions are made in scarcity, how local people in the ports are protected, and how the ship admits what it cannot do.

"Who goes back for those left in the water?" asked Lumi.

"First we find out whether we have enough vessel, crew and fuel not to drown anyone else," answered the Carpenter. "A rescue without the capacity to help can be only another danger."

The Pilgrim closed his eyes. "And if the study shows that it cannot be done?"

The Carpenter closed the boards. "Then we bury it properly. And we use what we learned."

On the last page of the drawing he wrote: *The keel is not laid. Before the next step we need an independent review.* Then he returned the pen to the authors.

> *A ship that returns begins by admitting what it cannot yet carry;*
> *a study is good when it allows even a negative answer.*

## Chapter 24 · A hundred years — who will carry the plane

### Story

In the morning at the gate the Carpenter found his tools laid out on a bench. The plane had a worn blade. The mallet had a cracked handle. The graver had lost its point.

"I should get new ones," he said.

"You can repair them," said Lumi.

"I could."

Beside the bench stood a young apprentice who had joined them on the road. He carried a small box of wedges and looked at the plane as if he were afraid to touch something that belonged to a legend.

"Do you want to try?" the Carpenter asked him.

"Me?"

"No. The plane."

The apprentice laughed and sat down. The Carpenter showed him how to take out the blade, how to check the edge and how to let the metal itself say where it is too thin. The first attempt failed. The second was better. The third held.

"Now it is yours," said the Carpenter.

"May I keep it?"

"You may use it. That is not the same as owning the work someone did with it."

Lumi looked at the lantern. "When I am big, will you still be here?"

The Carpenter did not answer at once. "I do not know. But the plane will be repairable. And someone will know where the instructions are."

Elizabeth stood at the gate and held the light low so that everyone could see the threshold. The Pilgrim laid on the bench a book with an empty page.

"What will be left of us?" she asked.

"Tools that someone else can use," said the Carpenter. "And the freedom to put them down."

The apprentice took the plane, but first he laid it back. "May I come back tomorrow and continue?"

"Yes," said the Carpenter. "And you may also not come. The door is open both ways."

Behind them it grew light. No one proclaimed that Mise Amenti was finished, that the station was flying or that all the ships had arrived. Someone carried water to the garden. Someone went to check a cracked hinge. Lumi wrote down what she had actually seen that day, and left a line empty beneath it.

The Carpenter raised his empty hands. For the first time in the whole journey he was holding nothing in them.

"There," he said. "Now you know what can be handed on."

> *The plane stayed in other hands;*
> *the empty page did not stay empty because the story had ended.*

## Epilogue · The Carpenter stays at the threshold

The Carpenter did not vanish in the middle of a sentence. He stayed sitting at the threshold where the garden touched the house. He laid the plane beside him — not as a relic but as a tool. On its handle he left a legible mark, so that the next person would know who had last sharpened it and when.

Lumi sat down beside him. At the table Radha divided bread, Elizabeth held the lantern and the Pilgrim read a map. The apprentice repaired the last hinge and forgot to sign the record, so he had to come back and add his name.

"We are still missing the carpenter," said someone from the table.

The Carpenter raised his head. "I am here. But he has already repaired the hinge."

At the gate a puddle remained from the night rain. In it were reflected the lantern, the house and a piece of the sky. Above the lintel the name had once been carved with the Carpenter's chisel. The wood was darkening, but the groove remained legible.

The ship was not finished once and for all. It never was.

"What will you do tomorrow?" asked Lumi.

"Tomorrow we will look at the threshold," said the Carpenter. "The wood around it has swollen a little."

Then he lifted the empty jug. The apprentice rose too. Together they went to the well — one knew how to carry a jug, the other where to take it.

> *The Carpenter stayed at the threshold, not so that it would be his;*
> *he stayed until other hands had taught the wood to hold.*
