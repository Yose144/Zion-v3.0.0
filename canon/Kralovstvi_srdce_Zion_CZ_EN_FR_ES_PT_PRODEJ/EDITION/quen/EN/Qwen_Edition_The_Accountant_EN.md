# Prologue · The account that has no owner

> Posted: nothing.
> Received: nothing.
> Carried forward: we have nothing to start with — and yet it is being counted.

They asked me why I write.

At first it was not curiosity but an order. In the workshop where year after year wood, nails and time were issued and where nothing was received — because the boat was not for sale — the carpenter said: "Someone has to hold it in numbers, so that we do not get carried away by it." I took the shards and the eye. The workshop's accountant. He gave me no name; we called each other that, and here it is the abbreviation of my account, not of a witness to history.

Since then I keep two books. The **Journal** tells what happened on the boat. The **Ledger** tells what it cost — and what remained owed. The Journal likes a story; the Ledger likes balance. The quarrels between the two of us are the most honest pages of the boat.

I learned three things that repeat in that boat for twenty centuries:

1. **Nothing is free — and yet it is given away.** That is the whole riddle the story carries.
2. **A number you must not say aloud does not obey you.** I count fish, but I do not count hearts. When I tried, the eye skipped. I wrote that down too.
3. **An account closed with a zero lies twice: in the amount and in the silence.** So no closing with zero. I carry it forward.

You can check this book. At the end of every account there is a table that says what is verse, what is tradition, what is history, what is story and what is myth — and what the account does not accept at all. You will not find live network numbers in it on purpose: a page that froze them would start lying the day after it was written.

A story may be beautiful. The ledger must be straight. And when it is not straight, I write that it is not straight.

> *Count what you can issue. / Do not count what you cannot receive. / And do not forget to carry it forward.*

# PART I · WOOD

# 1 · The workshop — items that cannot be sold

> Posted: wood, in time, strength, years in silence.
> Received: nothing that could be shown.
> Carried forward: a boat nobody asked for.

In Nazareth the workshop smelled of cedar and olive, and on the wall hung chisels smoothed by three generations. There they made yokes for oxen, tables, doors. People said: what the carpenter makes, holds.

And in the back corner, under a cloth, a thing was growing that brought in nothing. A **boat**.

My first task was simple: keep the workshop's books. The second was hard: keep the boat's account.

> **Workshop account**
> Issued: cedar (origin: \_\_\_\_\_\_), ribs (oak, ash), pitch, cloth, years.
> Received: — an empty column.
> Balance: a debt whose creditor is not named.

"Who will you sell it to?" the neighbours asked. "Here in the hills? The lake is a whole day's journey."

"I am not selling it," said the carpenter, and did not lift the plane. "I am building it for those who will need it one day."

I entered it as an **item I cannot value** — sale price zero, because no sale took place; and use value unstated, because the use had not come yet. Accountants do not like such items. They are exactly the items that then outlive all the others.

He laid every plank three times before fixing one. When the apprentice dared to ask about the pace, he said: "A boat meant to carry people through a storm is not built in haste. It is built over years, in the days when there is calm — so that it stands when the calm is gone."

I wrote: *Issued: calm. In advance, not to order.* That is the riskiest outlay I have ever seen — calm cannot be returned when the storm comes.

And one sentence I must write carefully, because it appears in my lines for the first time and for the second time in centuries:

**Origin of the cedar: \_\_\_\_\_\_.**

I do not know it. The story does not know it either, it just dislikes admitting it. I dislike admitting it with a blank page. Whoever fills in a guess has a boat made of wood that never grew.

> *Workshop account: years without interest.*
> *Debtor unknown, creditor patient.*
> *The keel grows where nobody is counting.*

# 2 · Pitch — the item the ledger does not accept

> Posted: pitch, inside and outside.
> Received: tears, sweat, rain — all unmeasured.
> Carried forward: what covers, carries.

He was demanding about wood. He walked through the yards and the piles, turned one plank twice, set a third aside. The apprentice could not bear it: "Master, it is only wood."

"Every piece of wood grew differently — one in the wind, one in the shade, one bent by a rock," he said. "The boat must not destroy them. It must take them as they are. People are like wood: each is bent to a different side. A good boat does not remake them — it carries them."

Then came the pitch. Dark, thick, smelling of resin. He smeared every seam **inside and out**, until the hull shone like washed honey.

"Why inside?" I asked with my first accountants' question. "The water does not get in there."

"That is exactly where it does. From sweat, from tears, from the rain that falls on the people in the boat."

I wrote: *Outlay: pitch, twice. Once where the neighbours see it; the second time where nobody sees it.* Bookkeeping can handle the first line. The second it can note, but not value.

In the old texts that were read aloud in the workshop and that nobody fully understood, it stood about Noah: *wood of gofer… and smear it with pitch inside and out* (Genesis 6:14). And the interpreters said that **kofer** — Hebrew for pitch — shares its root with the word for covering and reconciliation: *kapóret, kipur*, Yom kippur, the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:30–34). "Remember," said the carpenter, "what covers people is what carries them over the water."

I tried it: I set a column "amount" next to the word *covering*.

The result: **cannot be booked.** The ledger accepts the outlay of pitch. It does not accept forgiveness. It does not even accept the inference of how much forgiveness the boat "carries" — because what is forgiven can neither be given nor taken. I entered it as **NOT ON THE SCALES**: neither evidence, nor claim, nor merit.

And yet — or precisely therefore — no page of that ship's chronicle gets by without it.

> *What comes from outside, the customs house sees.*
> *What comes from inside, only the water sees.*
> *The ledger cannot do it. The boat can.*

# 3 · The flaw — the complaints the boat accepted

> Posted: cracks, knots, bends.
> Received: their telling.
> Carried forward: nobody covered up.

Beside the big ledger I keep a small one. I call it **the complaints book**.

A plank with a crack goes back in an ordinary workshop. Here it was written down: *crack, depth, side, how it grew.* And it was built in so that the crack could be seen. "You do not cover a crack with a worthless plank," said the carpenter. "You go around the crack and let it tell how it grew."

When I first added up the defective pieces, a number fell out that was called *the crew*. Not because one could calculate how many people fit into a flaw — but because every flaw had a name and every name had a bent side. The inventory of faults turned out to be an inventory of people.

By contrast, the carpenter's list of perfect pieces is short: **the keel.** "Everything else depends on my mended hands; the keel depends on the tree," he said. I did not put that anywhere. Some sentences cannot be written down, because they would become a claim.

The prophet said: *we are like a light dust, the work of your hands* (Isaiah 64:7); Job: *you formed me from clay* (Job 10:9); and the one who later writes about Christians finds the exact accounting word: *we have this treasure in fragile clay vessels, so that the surpassing power may be God's, not ours* (2 Corinthians 4:7). Treasure in a clay vessel is the only currency this workshop trades in: **the value is not in the vessel; it is in what was entrusted to it. And a crack does not fall to the creditor.**

> **Returns note**
> Received: everything. Returned: nothing.
> Repair: not "remade", but "carried".
> Balance: hulls with a flaw = a crew without shame.

> *A flaw is not a loss. A flaw is the memory of the tree.*
> *Whoever covers it steals the boat's own story.*
> *Whoever leaves it knows whom they are carrying.*

# PART II · WATER

# 4 · Seaworthiness — a day without profit

> Posted: one day, rollers, forty backs.
> Received: the surface.
> Carried forward: interest nobody collected.

All night they hauled empty nets. That is an item I knew from the workshop: **an outlay without income, repeated, without appeal.** The fishermen had it in their tables better than anyone.

In the morning the carpenter brought the boat down to the water by Capernaum. It lay on the surface quietly, as though it had been there since the beginning of time. I wrote *RECEIVED: water* — and right beneath it a note: *received without claim; the water will name its price only when it names its own.*

And then something happened that I, as an accountant, did not believe: he sat down in the bow, facing the shore, and **taught the crowds from the boat** (Luke 5:3). The water carried the voice further than the synagogue wall. Even those who would not have stood at the doors heard. Simon listened, let down the nets where he no longer believed, and drew them up torn.

"Why did you build that boat? You will not sell it," said Simon in the dripping silver glory of fish.

"I built it so that one could teach from it," he answered. "Fishermen, come with me — I will teach you to catch people." (Mark 1:17)

They left the nets and the little boats and went. I wrote: **Written off: property. Credited: vocation. The account is not closed — because what they received has no column.**

There is one daily account that never closes with a profit, and yet is worth keeping. It is the day when something first touches the water it was built for.

> *The day the boat lost the shore, it gained the sea.*
> *Account: one voyage, zero yield, everything issued.*
> *Carried forward: a voice the water did not return.*

# 5 · The storm — the item "fear", variable

> Posted: wind from the mountains, water over the side, shouting.
> Received: sleep.
> Carried forward: a silence nobody paid for.

The Kinneret can make a storm in an hour: winds fall from the mountains into the basin like water into a funnel. The ledger has a line for this that we laugh at — **exceptional event** — because "exceptional" in bookkeeping means: *it will happen again.*

That evening they sailed to the other shore. The disciples, people of the water, pulled at the oars and shouted: "Master, do you not care that we are perishing?!" And the carpenter slept on a cushion he had planed himself — the way one sleeps who built every rib and knows what it will bear.

He rose. He looked at the wind and the sea, not angrily, but as a craftsman looks at work not yet finished — and said to the lake what is said to a boat that is finished: **"Be silent, be still."** (Mark 4:35–41)

A great silence came, so that they heard drops running down from the steering oar. And then the question that is the most expensive in any book of accounts: *who is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?*

I tried to add it up. Wind: an item. Wave: an item. Fear: a variable item, the currency of hopelessness. Silence: —

Silence has no rate. It cannot be bought, cannot be sold, cannot be carried to the next page; it can only be **received**. So I entered an exceptional event with a note such as has no place in a register:

*The account of this voyage is at a loss in every currency except one we do not have. The silence that fell here is the most expensive item we have ever received, and we do not know what it is paid with.*

The old psalm tried it before me, clumsily: *they go down to the sea in ships… they cry out in trouble… he stills the storm… and brings them to the harbour they long for* (Psalm 107:23–30). And Jonah slept in the storm in the bottom of the boat until the sea wanted its price (Jonah 1) — a prefiguration the carpenter himself will name when his night comes (ch. 6).

> *Fear has a rate. Silence has no price tag.*
> *The craftsman who sleeps in his own boat knows that.*
> *And he teaches it to every crew after him.*

# 6 · Three days — the zero book

> Posted: a beam, not a boat. Three days.
> Received: —
> Carried forward: — (and that is the whole entry)

Anyone who has ever kept a book knows the feeling: **the duty to write into emptiness.**

This time it was not an error of the pen. The carpenter knew it three times and the crew did not believe him, because nobody wants to write that the boat they know must go under the water. There was a betrayal and a kiss; there was wood of another kind — not a boat, a **beam** (*cursed is anyone who hangs on a tree*, Galatians 3:13 / Deuteronomy 21:23). And then three days, about which Scripture is silent in such a way that it can be heard.

The only thing that remained of him in the numbers is his own sign: *as Jonah was in the belly of the great fish three days and three nights, so will the Son of Man be in the heart of the earth* (Matthew 12:40; Jonah 1:17).

In bookkeeping a zero is sad but honest. A zero book — *nothing is written* — is worse. I tried it. I held a table with three lines and empty columns until I realized it is not a loss but **a state in which one cannot keep accounts**. The only thing not empty there was what should not have been: guards, a seal, a stone.

> **Three days**
> Posted: a beam. Received: —
> Balance: — Carried forward: —

I will not leave that page blank in the book. A blank page can be torn out. **The record that one cannot keep accounts cannot be erased.**

And then, on the third day at dawn — the stone rolled away, the cloth empty, two women running the last road that became the first message (Mark 15–16; Luke 24; John 20; 1 Corinthians 15:3–8). By the lake again: mist, empty nets, a fire on the shore, bread, and a man asking: "Children, have you anything to eat?" — and a full net (John 21; see ch. 9).

The only closing this book accepts is the morning after the zero book.

> *Three days: no entry. And yet:*
> *an income that surpassed what might have been expected —*
> *it cannot be entered without opening the next page.*

# 7 · The centuries — journal and ledger

> Posted: crews that changed. Received: storms that remained.
> Carried forward: the name on the keel.

Until now I kept one account. From here I keep two, and I must explain it, because the whole of that century is in it.

The **Journal** tells. The **Ledger** adds up. When a crew mixes the two, a boat is made that sails — and a list of losses is also made that nobody wants to talk about. This edition stands on the fact that both are true and that they must not silence each other.

Every good boat has an anchor. This one has it **upward**: *hope as an anchor of the soul, firm and secure, reaching into the inner sanctuary behind the curtain* (Hebrews 6:19–20). I tried to book it downward, the way a guarantee is booked. It worked. Then I held nothing. An anchor dropped down holds a boat in place; an anchor dropped upward holds **direction**. In bookkeeping that is called a receivable that cannot be collected — and without which one cannot sail.

It put out to sea after the carpenter: first twelve, then thousands on the day of Pentecost (Acts 2), then centuries. *Navicella* — the ship of the Church — in mosaics and seals from the 3rd–4th century; the *nave*, the main body of a church, is its daughter in every church. Under the hammers of emperors it leaked and did not sink; under Constantine's banners it made such a career that it forgot who built it: **when a boat is too big, the helmsman thinks an empire built it.**

It split into parts and every part claimed to be the whole boat. In accounts that is called consolidation, and in history schism; both hurt the same.

Each time the account became unbearable, someone came from behind: a man who spoke with birds; a woman who gave herself to the poor; a priest in front of tanks. They had no new number. They had a **reminder of the name** — carved on the keel, where nobody sees it: *Kingdom of the Heart* (Luke 17:21: *entos hymōn* — "within you / among you", a translation that cannot be closed with one word; I leave that page open too).

> **The centuries**
> Issued: uncountable. Received: immeasurable.
> Record: two books. Consolidation: impossible.
> Debtor: the one who comes. Creditor: the same name.

> *The journal lies when it forgets to hurt. The ledger lies when it forgets to hope.*
> *The anchor that goes upward can do both.*
> *That is why we have been sailing for two thousand years with an unfinished closing.*

# PART III · WHAT THE LEDGER HOLDS

# 8 · Inventory — fire and seed

> Posted: twelve fires, salt, seed, memory.
> Received: what survived.
> Carried forward: what cannot be lost when everything burns.

The face of an inventory always asks the same question: **what will remain here when nobody is here?**

In the Old Testament they have a hard, good device for memory: *the covenant of salt is an everlasting covenant* (Leviticus 2:13; 2 Chronicles 13:5); and after the flood God hangs in the sky a **bow — a weapon without a lock, without arrows**: *a memory that cannot be revoked* (Genesis 9:13–16). That is the oldest inventory I know: a list of what is to remain.

Nehemiah brings the people back from exile and keeps **lists** (Nehemiah 7; Ezra 2) — families, towns, numbers. When they are erased, nothing is left but a nickname, "the sons of Adonikam…". The accountants' lesson: **a list is a covenant with the future**, and whoever fails to keep it has only a name without a number.

The last ship I see is therefore the ark: **not a shop, but an archive.** A double-bottomed hull, three decks, a seed archive at −18 °C, eight cubic metres of water a day, autonomy of a hundred and eighty days, at most a hundred people, sails as roof and as engine, a voyage alongside humpbacks of thirty thousand nautical miles. (Deck plan B3, a study — parameters to be verified, **no keel has been laid**: the public page, *Nirvana* 14, *Bodhi Gaia* 03.)

And around it twelve fires that *The Circle of the Goddesses* names — a garden that you keep without owning; **salt that you do not give when you keep it only for yourself**; seed that is counted by letting it break open.

My inventory book therefore has a column nobody in my guild has: **to issue, and thereby to preserve.** Seed is entered in it whole, because only what is not allowed to be buried is lost.

> **Inventory**
> Fire — do not let it go out; seed — let it die.
> Salt — give it away; memory — copy it by hand.
> Loss: acknowledged as a form of holding.

> *The ark is not where there is little water.*
> *The ark is where there is enough memory*
> *to let even the one who comes later read it.*

# 9 · One hundred and fifty-three — the number that owns nothing

> Posted: the net, the night, the name "do you love me?"
> Received: one hundred and fifty-three.
> Carried forward: a reason to count ≠ the right to own.

Professionally I made my living by counting. So it occurred to me that in the middle of the Gospel there is a single fish that has an exact number:

**And they hauled it ashore, full of large fish: one hundred and fifty-three.** (John 21:11)

I counted it twice, because it looks like a typo: why exactly that? Early tradition explained it variously — 153 = 100 + 50 + 3; or "the large fish = the nations"; and one Father calculated that 153 is the sum of the numbers from 1 to 17, "completeness under the law and the firstfruits of grace". (TRADITION — interpretation, not finding.) It has always been a number that **designates** something, not something that could be **traded**.

I made a quiet contractual agreement: **I will count fish; I will not count hearts.** When I broke it the second time (see the Prologue), the eye skipped. When the crew broke it later — with the number that has a different language in Revelation: **a hundred and forty-four thousand sealed from all the tribes of Israel, twelve thousand from each** (Revelation 7:4–8), and behind them a multitude "that nobody could count" (Revelation 7:9) — I received a lesson accountants rarely receive: **in the middle of a book that counts there stands a sentence that refuses to count. And God likes it.**

That is why this page must carry a warning no other page has:

> **The hundred and forty-four thousand in Revelation is not a block height, not a price, not a cap, and not a list of the elect to be owned.** Do not merge the numerals of a vision with the technical constants of the network. Whoever conflates them makes a table out of a vision — and a claim out of a table. (`144 000` as `DAO_TREASURY_LOCK_HEIGHT` exists in the chain code; in Revelation it is the name of a multitude. The same numerals. A different language. There is no accounting relation between them — and this book forbids making one.)

What remained in the account after the net was hauled was something other than a catch: *feed my sheep… tend my lambs…* (John 21:15–17), three times a question that cannot be entered as property: **do you love me?** Love that counts is stewardship; **stewardship that counts ownership is a trap, not a pasture.**

> **Entry in the book**
> Received: 153 fish — countable.
> Received: a multitude — uncountable.
> Instruction: count what can be returned. Do not count what can only be received.

> *One hundred and fifty-three can be written down — and it obliges nothing.*
> *One hundred and forty-four must not be merged — and it owns nothing.*
> *Between them is pasture, not possession.*

# 10 · The fleet — one account, many columns

> Posted: an age. Received: ships.
> Carried forward: the same name, other hands, another script.

When the crews returned from the future and from other shores, I saw on the quay a thing you could measure with a metre rule, if you dared:

Three hulls of different colours — **Tres Marías**: Santa María la Mayor, de la Esperanza, del Camino (dark, wine-coloured, sails of pale canvas with threads of light), and behind them a fourth, sailing: the ark, the thirteenth node of the earth, and above, where you do not get without a covenant — the station. And on the keel of every ship, **by another hand and in another script, the same name: Kingdom of the Heart.** (Corpus: *Nirvana* 13, *Surta of the Heart*, *Nabukano* — MYTH/BUILD/HORIZON, not a finished thing; *Maitreya* — the mantra of three embodiments, not a claim about reincarnation.)

As an accountant I have one note on the fleet that makes sense: **this is not a consolidation.**

In large companies accounts are merged to make them bigger. Here the opposite rule holds — *to bring all things together in Christ* (Ephesians 1:10): "that everything in Christ might be **completed**". Consolidation cancels columns. A fleet multiplies them. One debtor, many creditors, the same name on every keel.

They say that *every age has its boat, but all of them sail to the same harbour: the heart that opens*. I wrote it as a **note below the balance sheet** — below it, not in it, because it cannot be weighed.

And then I did the most radical thing I can do: **I closed nothing.**

> *One boat — a story. Two — a parable.*
> *A fleet — an account that cannot be consolidated,*
> *and yet holds: because the name is a shared debt, not property.*

# 11 · The harbour — the last page

> Posted: every sea. Received: every silence.
> Carried forward: — this time legitimately.

At the end of the Bible there is a book, and in it a harbour that does not come but **descends**:

*A new heaven and a new earth, and there was no longer any sea… the holy city, the new Jerusalem, coming down from God… Behold, the dwelling of God is with people… he will wipe every tear from their eyes, and death will be no more, nor mourning, nor crying, nor pain; the first things have passed away.* (Revelation 21:1–5) *A sea of glass, like crystal.* (Revelation 4:6; 15:2) *The river of the water of life… the tree of life, twelve times fruit, leaves for the healing of the nations.* (Revelation 22:1–5)

An accountant looks at the harbour like this: it is the page where **the carrying forward finally stops** — because nothing passes to the next page any more, because no next page is needed.

I tried to count it. It escapes me. In the harbour there is no balance, because there is no debtor — everyone is a guest, and **a guest cannot be a balance**.

To make it clear to children too: when the closing is finished, nothing will have to be remembered from the previous page. There will be no line "carry forward to next year", because there will be no next year to carry into. There will be no "unpaid", because the unpaid will be paid — not with an amount, but with a **face**. *And they will see his face.* (Revelation 22:4)

The last line I wrote in my life stands under this page and reads: **The Spirit and the bride say: Come. And let anyone who hears say: Come.** (Revelation 22:17)

That is the last sentence that can be written in an account. It is not a total. It is an **invitation** — and an invitation is not entered in the books.

> *A harbour has no closing. It has a door.*
> *There was no longer any sea. The carrying forward is not filed.*
> *An end that cannot be booked can only be received.*
