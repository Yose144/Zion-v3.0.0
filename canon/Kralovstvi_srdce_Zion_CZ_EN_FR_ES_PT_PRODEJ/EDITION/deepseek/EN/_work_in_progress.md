# PROLOGUE · The Trunk

> **GROWTH RING 0 · THE HEARTWOOD** · a mountain ridge above Byblos, sometime at the turn of the second and first century before our era
> *"The cedar grows where nothing else would."*

---

## Story

At first I was nothing but patience.

It was a silence that cannot be described, because in it there was nothing that could realize it was silence. It was simply matter slowly learning to be wood. Every year I added one ring. When the summer was dry, the ring was thin and dark as a scar from a fingernail. When the year was soft and rainy, the ring spread wide and was pale as milk. When the frost came, I stopped growing for a while and a thin line remained in my body that later only someone looking really closely would see.

Nobody looked. I stood high above the sea, where the air tastes of stone, and I was one of a thousand. Cedars on a ridge are not like park trees. Their trunks are split by lightning, their crowns irregular, branches dead on one side and thrust far out on the other. They grow slowly, because nobody waters them. They grow crooked, because they must. They grow hard, because it blows.

And then came the winter that decided.

It was not the storm you would imagine. It was not a roar, it was not an impact. It was pressure that lasted three days. The wind leaned into the slope and did not leave — it lay on us like a palm on a chest, all night, all day, and I felt the fibres inside me shift by a centimetre at a time. Wood is not stone. When you press on stone, stone either holds or breaks. When you press on wood, wood *bends*, and by that it saves itself — until it stops bending.

On the fourth night the root did not let go of the soil. I was too hard to be uprooted like a spruce.

Instead I broke two metres up.

I remember that sound more precisely than anything else. It was not a crack. It was a *sigh* — long, deep, as though a knot had come untied inside me that I had not known was there. And then I was falling. Slowly at first, because I was long, and the longer I was the more slowly I tilted. I saw the sky as I fell, which I had never done before; a cedar looks only sideways, never up. The sky that night was full of wet light, and I took it in from below for the first and last time.

I hit snow and moss, and the silence I left behind me was the loudest thing in the forest.

I lay there two years.

Two years is nothing to you. To lying wood it is two hundred and fifty seasons: autumn, when dampness entered me; winter, when frost stretched me and pulled me back; spring, when beetles woke inside me and began drawing maps; summer, when the resin from my own body coated me like varnish. I did not rot. Cedar does not rot quickly — it smells of resin, which is bitter to every organism, and so it takes centuries for the forest to take it back. But even so I changed. On the side turned to the ground I darkened. On the side turned to the sky I faded to silver. Where the lightning had struck me a hundred years before, a crack opened two fingers wide, leaves fell into it, and I stopped being one piece. I became two halves that did not know about each other.

When they came for me, I had no idea that anyone had come for me. People had walked past all those two years. They were men who can reckon how many planks there are in a cubic metre. They came with an axe on the shoulder, with a sash, with letters. They looked at me and said what everyone says: *broken, cracked, crooked.* Too soft for a chimney, too short for beams, too twisted for planks. Someone tapped me with a boot and left.

Then came the one who sat down.

He was young. Not young like a boy — young like a man who already has enough work behind him, but not yet enough years for property to settle in his face. He wore a grey cloak dusty from the roads, and in his hands he carried a carpenter's bundle whose handles were smooth not from his father but from him. He bent to the crack and pushed two fingers into it until he packed my old rings under his nails.

He was silent a long time. Then he did the thing nobody had done before him: he laid his palm on me, the way a hand is laid on a child's forehead. I felt his warmth. And he felt mine — that bitter, resinous warmth that stayed in me even after two years under the snow.

"You gave this to nobody?" he asked the dealer.

"Who would take it, master. It is broken."

"I know," he said. "I am asking whether anyone wanted it."

"Nobody wanted it."

"Then it is mine."

I understand why the dealer remembered that and why he told it afterwards. The young carpenter had no money for choosing. He had not even a family name in the trading books. He had only one word, which he said afterwards — and I did not hear it then, because wood hears only what sounds within its own body. I heard it much later, when he repeated it to other people, in another place, and with another hand:

*"The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone."*

When they loaded me, the dealer's rope snapped. Nobody laughed; that young man braced my broken half with his shoulder before it slid into the mud, and whispered to me — or at least I think so, because wood invents as gladly as people do:

"Do not be afraid. I know what I will make of you. You will be the one that carries."

From that moment I knew two things.

First: that someone had chosen me who had a choice and chose what others had set aside.

Second: that I would be a boat — and that before I could be a boat I must stop being a tree. I must lose my crown, my bark, my dampness, my name. I must be cut into pieces and joined again otherwise than nature joined me. That is the price of sailing.

I did not know then that I would pay that price many times. That once I would be a boat, once mud, once a dug-up thing behind glass. And that every time they wanted to use me again, they would ask the same of me: **to stop being what I was, and become a shape that carries someone else.**

But that belongs further on.

## From Scripture, Tradition and History

- **Psalm 118:22** — "The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone." The verse Jesus, according to the Gospels, applied to himself (Matthew 21:42; Mark 12:10; Luke 20:17; Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7). The psalm originally sang it about Israel — a nation the great powers set aside.
- **Isaiah 53:2** — "He had no form or beauty that we should desire him." An Old Testament image the Church has read from the beginning as a portrait of the rejected. The wood nobody wanted is its carpenter's version.
- **Cedar in Scripture** — 1 Kings 5:6–10 (cedars for Solomon's temple), Psalm 92:13 ("the righteous will grow like a cedar"), Ezekiel 31 (the cedar as an image of grown power). The cedar of Lebanon (*Cedrus libani*) is, across the ancient Mediterranean, a synonym for wood that lasts — and therefore expensive.
- **The cedar of Lebanon is botanically documented** — a slow-growing conifer, living for hundreds to thousands of years, with hard, aromatic, naturally preserving wood. Its resin repels insects; that is why it became building and temple wood.
- **The carpenter's word about the rejected stone** is documented in the Gospels. That he said it over a broken trunk at a saw is not. It is the image of this book.

## Measure

- **Height of the ridge above Byblos**: ~1,800–2,200 m above sea level. Cedar stands in Lebanon today survive in enclaves above 1,300 m; historical stands reached lower.
- **The tree's growing time**: a cedar adds roughly **0.5–2 mm** radially per year. A trunk 90 cm in diameter is therefore a tree about **250–450 years** old.
- **Two years lying**: for comparison — a beech in a deciduous forest falls apart in 3–5 years, a spruce in 5–10. Cedar lasts decades, because its own resin protects it.
- **A crack two fingers wide**: ~**3 cm** across. That is the crucial number — a gap exactly that wide decides that the trunk cannot become a single keel, but two joined pieces.

## Shaving

This chapter claims that the tree was felled by a storm and that the carpenter chose it. Neither can be documented. What is documented is only that cedar is hard, fragrant and expensive — and that Jesus spoke of the rejected stone. Between those two lies this whole book, and I will not sweep it away: **the rest is image, and an image has no proof, because it does not need one — it needs a reader who knows it is an image.**

# I · The Keel

> **GROWTH RING I** · Nazareth in Galilee, a workshop, ~20 AD
> *The first ring. The one that can never be taken back.*

---

## Story

Into Nazareth wood is carried on the back.

That is the first thing I must say about that workshop, because otherwise you will picture it wrongly. Nazareth does not stand by the water. It does not even stand by a road along which a trunk could be dragged. It stands on a slope above the basin, in a place where water must be carried in clay jars and where every piece of decent wood is paid for twice: once to the dealer, and once with your own sweat on the way up.

When they set me down in the yard, it was an event. The neighbours came to look. Not at me — at the fact that the carpenter had dared to buy a trunk that was in two halves. The old potter, who always had an opinion before he had a question, asked whether wood on a roof also breaks in two. The young carpenter did not answer. He knelt on the ground, took a cord soaked in ochre and drew the first line on me.

I remember that line as the first thing that ever hurt me. The cord tightened, snapped, and a straight, bright line remained on the grey wood. Until that moment I had been a natural object. From that moment I was a drawing.

The workshop was one room with two windows that could be opened wide so that the shavings could go out. On the walls hung chisels, drawknives, saws, two mallets and one auger — an auger is the thing you make yourself, because nobody sells it. On the floor lay a layer of sweepings that nobody ever fully swept up, because sweepings are a sign that work is being done. And under the window stood a table on which there was a clay bowl of coarse salt and a loaf.

That bowl was the first thing anyone ever explained to me.

"Whoever enters," said the mother when an apprentice from the next village came to her, "receives bread and salt. Before he asks what anything costs." She took the loaf, broke it, salted it and handed it over. The apprentice took it with both hands, because he did not know what else to do with it. "That is how it is done," she said. "First the covenant, then the work."

She was Miriam. I will not tell more about her than what I heard from below and from close by: that she walked barefoot even in winter, that she spoke little and always to the point, that wrinkles appeared in her face only around the eyes, and that when her son looked at her he stopped talking about himself. Once, when I was almost finished, she laid her palm on me just as he had once done on me in the forest, and said: "It will be a fine boat." Not "useful", not "large". Fine. That word stayed with me.

From that moment I knew that in that workshop it was not only about work.

---

The work took four years.

To tell it as a single scene would be false, because a boat is not born in a flash. It is born in what repeats. It is born in the fact that every morning you come, look at the line you drew yesterday, and find it half a millimetre off. It is born in the fact that you make a plank, fit it, take it off, make it again. It is born in the fact that you do the same thing four times running and only the fifth time is it right — and "right" is not a feeling, it is a sound. When a plank seats, a tone sounds. Not metaphorically. When you lay a heated plank on a rib and it seats, there is a short, dry *clack*, and you know it holds.

I was the keel. That means: the spine.

First the trunk had to be sawn. The carpenter did it himself and it took him nine days, because he had no saw for that length and had to borrow one, and because cedar is not spruce — it is hard, it has pockets of resin that catch the saw like pitch. He sawed me into two longitudinal halves and then took the one with the crack and did the exact opposite of what anyone else would have done.

He did not cut the crack out.

He did not bridge it with a worthless plank.

**He put a wedge into it.**


He widened it, cleaned it, dried it with fire, and into the opening he set another wood — harder, from the olive that grows on dry stones and has a grain dense as bone. He fitted it in, wedged it, and then ran a band of hammered copper and nails along the whole length. When he had finished, the keel was not one piece. It was a piece that had decided to hold even though it is not one.

"Why not do it more simply?" asked the apprentice. "It takes three times as long."

"Because a boat does not sink where it is sound," said the carpenter. "It sinks where there is a fault. And a fault you hide sinks twice: once as water and once as a lie."

Then came what I awaited most: the curve.

A keel is not a beam. A beam is straight. A keel is a **line** that rises at both ends — more at the bow, less at the stern — and that line decides everything. It decides whether the boat rides on the water or into it; whether it bends at a wave or stabs into it; whether it is fast or safe; and because you cannot have both, it decides what that boat will be.

The carpenter did not make that curve by eye. In the yard he built a frame of planks, nailed cords to it and hung a plumb line between them, and then laid a thin strip of wood on me, bent it and looked. It was the first drawing I ever experienced. It took eleven days, and in that time he changed the curve six times. Every time he changed it he went down to the road, from where a piece of the lake could be seen, and looked at the water. I do not know what he saw there. I know that when he came back he always changed the bow once more.

In the end he carved the curve into the frame and nailed planks onto the frame so that nobody could shift it. Then he looked at me and said one sentence that I remember better than the whole sound of that *clack*:

**"A boat is not matter. A boat is a curve. Matter can be stolen; a curve has to be known."**

---

The neighbours came to look and shook their heads. That is the part every version of this book tells, and they tell it rightly, because it is the core. Nazareth in the hills. The lake a whole day's journey away. Who buys a boat in the middle of dry rock?

"Who will you sell it to, carpenter?"

And the carpenter said what he had to say, and it was not boasting, because in his voice there was nothing that wanted admiration:

**"I am not selling it. I am building it for those who will need it one day."**

The potter laughed and left. But one woman remained standing in the doorway and asked quietly, so quietly that I heard her only because I lay on the ground: "And how will you know?"

The carpenter was silent for a while. Then he said: "I will not. But when it is needed, it will be finished."

That is all. That is the only sentence about faith you need in this book. Faith is not the conviction that something will happen. Faith is working on a boat for people you do not know will come.

## From Scripture, Tradition and History

- **Mark 6:3** — "Is this not the **carpenter** (*tektōn*), the son of Mary…?" The only place in the Gospels where Jesus is named directly by his trade. *Tektōn* in Greek does not mean only a joiner — it means a craftsman working in hard material: wood and stone.
- **Matthew 13:55** — "Is this not the son of the carpenter?" The workshop was the family's; the trade passed from father to son.
- **Matthew 11:30** — "My yoke is kind and my burden light." A yoke is literally a carpenter's product — a piece of wood shaped so that it spreads the weight and does not cut. That sentence is a craftsman's sentence, not only a metaphor.
- **Sirach 38:24–34** — Jesus ben Sira (2nd century BC) praises craftsmen: the smith, the potter, the carver. He says their wisdom is *in the work of their hands* and that they do not sit at the head of judgment — and yet they "hold the world together". It is the closest ancient text that speaks of carpenters as seriously as of scholars. The Church reads these words about the carpenter of Nazareth too.
- **Luke 2:51–52** — "He was obedient to them… and Jesus grew in wisdom and stature." The Gospels are silent about the years between twelve and thirty. That silence is called the *hidden years*, and it is a real silence of the text, not a gap to be filled.
- **Bread and salt** — the covenant of hospitality is a documented practice of the ancient Mediterranean and Near East (salt as durability and obligation). In the corpus it is the centre of the book *The Salt of This Earth*; here it is a detail of the workshop, not dogma.
- **Plumb line and cord** — the basic tools of the ancient joiner and builder, documented iconographically and textually (Isaiah 44:13: "the carpenter… marks it with a cord, draws it with a compass").

## Measure

- **The cubit** (*ammāh*, Greek *pēchys*) = roughly **44.4–45.7 cm** (the common cubit); the Egyptian royal cubit 52.3 cm. A carpenter measures in cubits from elbow to middle finger — which is why measures differ between workshops, and why workshops kept *their own* cubit carved into the wall.
- **The length of the boat in this book: 18 cubits** = **8.1–8.2 m**. Beam **5 cubits** = **2.3 m**. Depth **3 cubits** = **1.3–1.4 m**.
- **Why not eight cubits:** eight cubits is **3.6 m**. Twelve people do not fit into such a boat, nor a cargo of fish, and on a stormy Kinneret it would go to the bottom. Another version of this book has eight cubits; it is the one number there that does not stand up, and this version therefore does not follow it. Anchor: the actual Galilean boat of 1986 measures **8.2 m**.
- **Crew:** the rowing configuration of the surviving boat corresponds to **four rowers in pairs** plus a helmsman — a crew of about **five men**, which fits exactly Simon and Andrew, James and John and the "hired men" (Mark 1:20). The twelve disciples fitted into the boat, but as passengers, not as rowers.
- **The keel**: cross-section roughly **12 × 15 cm** at its thickest, tapering at the ends. That is the spine that carries the whole hull — and at the same time the longest continuous piece of wood in the boat.

## Shaving

This chapter needed the boat to be eighteen cubits long, because otherwise a reader who has ever stood by such a boat would stop believing it. But eighteen cubits is a number I chose, not one documented for *this* boat — the documented one has no name, no workshop and no carpenter. What is documented is only that someone built such a boat. **The rest is image. And an image that pretends to measurement must admit that it, not the archive, holds the measure.**

# II · The Planking

> **GROWTH RING II** · the workshop in Nazareth, the second year
> *Skin first. Then bones.*

---

## Story

When people today look at old boats, they think a boat is a skeleton onto which planks are nailed.

It is not. And that old boat knew it before any of us.

The order is the opposite, and it is the most beautiful thing I learned in all those years in a hull: **first you make the skin, and only then the bones.** The carpenter nails planks edge to edge, one to the next, until they make a shell — a hull that holds together by itself, before it has a single rib. And when the shell holds, he crawls inside and fits the ribs: he bends them to the finished skin, not the other way round.

It is called *shell-first*. And it means something I would like everyone who reads this book to carry into their own life:

**The shape that holds is made from outside. The strength that carries it comes from within — but only afterwards.**

---

The planks could not simply be laid on. Each had to be different, because a hull is not a cylinder; it narrows towards bow and stern, and so every plank is wider at one end than the other and every one must be twisted differently.

For that the carpenter had a cauldron.

It was a large copper pot sunk into the hearth in the yard, big enough for a man to fit inside. When the water boiled he laid two crossbars over the mouth, a plank on them, and covered it with cloth so the steam would not escape. He left it there. Not an hour — **half a day.** The plank soaked, the fibres gave way, and wood that had been hard as bone began to be soft as leather. Then he carried it quickly into the workshop, pressed it against the rib frame and drew it in with conical wedges. He let it cool. And when he released the wedges, the wood stayed in its new shape.

This is called steam bending, and in that workshop they had done it this way for thousands of years before him.

But there was one thing in it that I never understood until I looked closely. Always, **always**, before bending a plank, the carpenter ran his hand along its whole length with his eyes closed and found one line in it. The one where the grain naturally yields. And then he did not bend it against that line.

He bent it along it.

When the apprentice tried it the first time, he took a plank and pressed it hot against the hull as the boat looked. The plank held. But the next day, when the wedges were released, a crack appeared in the seam as long as a palm. The apprentice went pale.

"Master, I will fix it. I will tear it off and do it again."

The carpenter knelt beside him and looked for a while.

"No," he said. "First look at why it cracked."

"Because I pulled it too hard."

"No. Because you pulled it *against*. Look at the grain." He took the apprentice's hand and led it along the wood, just as he had once led his own. "Wood always tells you which way it wants to go. You may ask it to go a little further right than it would on its own. But you must not force it left when all of it wants right. That comes back — and it comes back at night, while you sleep."

The apprentice stood there and stared.

"So I have to do the whole thing again."

"Yes."

"And how many planks will I destroy like this before I learn it?"

The carpenter straightened. "I do not know. But I know that every one you destroy will teach you. And every one I destroy for you will teach you nothing."

The apprentice did it again. And then again. The fourth plank seated. When that dry *clack* sounded, the carpenter said nothing; he only moved along and began teaching the next one.

---

Joining is the second thing I want taken from this chapter.

The planks of a boat are not nailed to each other merely so that they hold. They are joined so that they **can work.** A boat at sea moves constantly. It is not a rigid thing; it is a thing that twists, sags and stretches, and if it were rigid it would split at the first proper wave. The whole construction is an invention about how to be firm and still move.

It is done with three things.

The first is the **seam** — a narrow gap between two planks. If you make it zero, the planks press on each other and crush themselves. If you make it wide, the boat falls apart. The right seam is thin as a fingernail and it is *deliberate*.

The second is the **treenail** — a wooden peg that passes through both planks and keeps them from sliding over each other. Wooden, not iron, because wood works with wood in the same way. When the boat bends, the treenail bends with it; an iron one would stay straight and split the plank.

The third is the **nail** — iron, because in some joints you need to hold by force, not by give. And here a rule held that stayed with me as the first sentence about people I ever heard from wood:

"A treenail holds because it bends with you. A nail holds because it does not bend. Both are needed. But if you had a boat of nails only, it would fall apart at the first wave — because every nail would want to stay where it was, and everything else would move."

---

And then came the moment I had waited months for: the fishermen came.

They were men from Capernaum, two or three of them; they came to look, because a boat in the hills was being talked about. They entered the workshop and looked around with the gaze of people who know what they are talking about. One of them, older, with hands like leather, walked around the hull, knelt at the bow and laid his palm on me.

For a while he said nothing. Then he said:

"Who made this for you?"

"I did."

"Alone?"

"Alone."

The fisherman stood up and looked at the carpenter the way men look who have done one thing all their lives and meet someone who does it differently well.

"This is not a fishing boat," he said. "This is a boat meant to come back."

The carpenter looked at him. He did not understand.

"A fishing boat is built to carry nets," said the man. "Your boat is built to carry *people*. Look at the beam — too wide for nets, too heavy for oars. But when a wave lifts, this boat will not capsize, because it has a curve that holds it. And when water comes aboard, it has a bottom you can bail out from." He stood. "You have never done this before?"

"No."

"Then how do you know how?"

And the carpenter said the sentence I heard from below and that stayed with me all my life:

**"I do not know. But I did it for four years, and every time I asked the wood what it would bear."**

The fisherman laughed, shook his head and left. A week later he came back with another man. And that second man was called Simon.

## From Scripture, Tradition and History

- **Genesis 6:14** — God to Noah: "Make yourself an ark of **gofer wood** and make **rooms** in the ark; smear it with pitch inside and out." *Rooms* (Hebrew *qinním*) means compartments — the ark is a partitioned construction, not one piece. The old interpreters read this as an image of a community that holds together thanks to its partitions, not despite them.
- **Ephesians 4:16** — "From him the whole body, **joined and held together** by every joint, as each part is given, grows…" The image of a body as a construction that holds by *joining*, not by rigidity. Paul uses the same logic as a joiner: strength comes from joints, not from nails.
- **1 Corinthians 12:22–27** — "The members of the body that seem to be the weakest are the most necessary." A verse that in this book is about nodes and weak points, not about people in general.
- **Shell-first** — a documented construction technique of Mediterranean boats from the 1st century BC to the 1st century AD: planking is joined edge to edge with mortise-and-tenon joints and pegs, and the ribs are inserted only afterwards to stiffen the hull. The boat known as the "Jesus boat" is built this way — the technique is archaeologically documented, not a guess.
- **Steam bending** is a documented technology of antiquity and of the modern age; it is used to this day.

## Measure

- **Planking thickness**: **2.5–4 cm** for a boat of this size. Thinner cracks, thicker cannot be bent by steam any more.
- **The seam between planks**: **1–2 mm**. More = the boat leaks; zero = the planks chafe and destroy each other.
- **The treenail**: diameter **6–8 mm**, length through two planks, of hard wood (olive, oak).
- **The nail**: forged iron, length **6–9 cm**, with the end turned over (clinched) from inside.
- **Number of planks per side** for a boat of this length: **7–9 strakes**. Every strake is a different plank — a different shape, a different curve.
- **Time to bend one plank**: **4–8 hours** in steam + cooling overnight.
- **How many planks get destroyed**: for an apprentice, **3–5** before he understands the grain. That is normal and it is fine.

## Shaving

In other versions of this book the carpenter learns by succeeding. Here he learns by failing — and that is not a literary ornament. It is documented that the 1st-century Galilean boat was **repaired at least twice** and that its builders mixed wood from different trees, because they had no better. The craft this book celebrates was not pure. It was **repaired.** And that is perhaps more important than all the beauty: **a boat that can be repaired is better than a boat that cannot.**

# III · The Ribs

> **GROWTH RING III** · the workshop in Nazareth, the third year
> *The skeleton adapts to the skin. Not the other way round.*

---

## Story

I first saw the boat from inside at the moment when the shell was finished and the carpenter crawled in.

Until then I had known only the outside: planks, seams, nails. Now I found myself in a space that had the shape of a boat but as yet had nothing to hold it. It was dim in there. The air smelled of resin, sweat and wet wood. Sound behaved differently in that space — every word bent against the walls and came back from somewhere other than where you had sent it. And when the carpenter spoke, it sounded as though he were speaking inside a shell.

He worked sitting, with his legs folded, because you cannot stand in a hull. He took a plank he had left half a day in steam, laid it from inside against the skin, pressed it with his palm and looked where it did not seat. Where it did not seat, he made a cut. Then he took it off, enlarged the cut, laid it on again. And so on, round and round, until the plank clung to the planking so tightly that not even a leaf could pass between them.

That plank is called a **timber** — a rib.

I noticed one thing that stayed with me as the second image of this book. The carpenter did not make the rib according to any plan. He did not take a compass and draw the arc that ought to be right. **He laid the plank against the finished skin and let the skin decide what shape the bone should have.**

The skeleton of a boat is not made first. It is made **according to** what already exists.

Had he done it the other way — had he first made beautiful, regular ribs and then stretched planks over them — he need not have made a single mistake and the boat would still not have come into being, because it would have been a *different* boat from the one that grew out of the metres of wood that passed through his hands.

---

One day a man came whom I knew by sight. He carried water.

He walked with a wooden yoke across his shoulders and on both ends hung yokes with buckets. He carried twenty of them a day, perhaps thirty, from the spring below the village up to the slope. He was about forty and had one shoulder lower than the other — not much, but enough for anyone to see when he sat down.

He stood in the doorway and said: "I want a new yoke."

The carpenter looked at him. "Is the old one bad?"

"The old one is good," said the man. "But it chafes me."

The carpenter came to him, laid a hand on his shoulder and felt it — the way he felt every plank before bending it. Then he had him stand in the light and walked around him. The man endured it like a patient.

"How old are you?"

"Forty-two."

"And how long have you carried water?"

"Since I was twelve."

The carpenter went back to the table, took a charcoal and sketched two curves on a board. One was regular, symmetrical, beautiful — the kind of yoke made for oxen by someone who has never seen a particular ox. The second was crooked. It rose on the left, fell on the right, and had a hollow in the middle.

"This is what I will make you," he said, pointing at the second.

The man looked at it and hesitated. "But… this is crooked."

"Yes."

"I do not want a crooked yoke. I want a yoke like other people have."

The carpenter put down the charcoal.

"Look at me," he said. "Your shoulder is three fingers lower, because for thirty years you have carried a straight yoke. I can make you another straight one now, and in ten years your shoulder will be five fingers lower and you will not be able to lift your arm. Or I make you a yoke according to your shoulder — and you carry water for another twenty years."

The man was silent.

"And what will people think of me?"

"I do not know," said the carpenter. "But I know that you carry the yoke, not they."

The man took the yoke away. A month later he came back to pay — and he brought two other water carriers with him.

---

When the hull was stiffened, the last and hardest decision came.

On one of the planks, on the port side, a little below the level where the rower sits, there was a **knot**. Not large — a palm's width. It had formed a hundred years before, when a branch that no longer grew was swallowed by the trunk. The wood around it grew on and slowly enclosed it, until it remained inside as a hard black knot.

The apprentice noticed it. He took the plane and said: "I will cut this out, it will make an ugly place here."

The carpenter stopped him with a hand.

"No."

"But it shows."

"Yes. And it is **the hardest place in the whole boat.**" The carpenter tapped the knot. "If you plane it out, you make a hole and you must plug it with something else — and that something else will be softer than the knot, because a knot is twisted fibre, and twisted fibre does not crack. If you leave it, it will be the one place in the hull that never gives."

The apprentice looked at him. "So I am to leave it?"

"You will leave it. But you will hide it from the eyes, not from the water." He took his mallet and nailed the knot from inside, through the rib, the way a wedge is driven into a crack. "On the outside you will smear it with pitch and nobody will see it. Inside you will let it be seen — because there only someone looking closely will see it, and that one ought to know that here the boat is stronger, not weaker."

---

## From Scripture, Tradition and History

- **Isaiah 42:3** — "A bruised reed he will not break, and a smouldering wick he will not put out." The first of the four "Servant Songs" (Isaiah 42:1–9). An image the Church reads about Christ, but which is above all a rule for anyone working with fragile material — that is, with wood and with people.
- **Matthew 11:28–30** — "Come to me, all who labour… **my yoke is kind** and my burden light." A yoke is a joiner's product. The sentence says: *I will not give you a straight yoke for a crooked back.*
- **1 Corinthians 12:22–26** — "The members of the body that seem to be the weakest are the most necessary… and those we think less honourable we clothe with greater honour." A weak place is not a fault to be removed — it is the place where strength is decided.
- **2 Corinthians 12:9** — "My grace is enough for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness." Paul writes about what he did not want to have. Not about what he managed.
- **A knot in a plank** — botanically: a knot is the remnant of a branch, surrounded by wood that grew around it in other years. The fibre in a knot is twisted and dense; a knot is usually harder than the surrounding wood, but it is also the place where the wood begins to separate as it dries. A joiner therefore either removes a knot or reinforces it — never ignores it.
- **A rib fitted into a finished shell** is an archaeologically documented procedure on the 1st-century boat (the Ginosar boat) and on other Mediterranean vessels of antiquity.

## Measure

- **Number of ribs (timbers)** on an 8.2 m boat: **18–22**, spacing **40–50 cm**.
- **Thickness of a rib**: **3–5 cm**; reinforced at the points of greatest stress (by the keel and under the rowing bench).
- **How many planks are bent for one rib**: a rib is made from one piece bent along its whole length, or from two pieces joined at the keel. The point is not the number but that **no rib is the same as another** — each is made according to its place.
- **A made-to-measure yoke**: width **38–45 cm**, height **8–12 cm**, weight **1.5–2.5 kg**. A difference of three fingers (≈ **5–6 cm**) between shoulders is essential for such a yoke.
- **Knots**: on a hull of this size there are usually **1–3**. They are never planed away — they are reinforced.
- **Years**: three years of work on the hull before the water came.

## Shaving

I wrote that the carpenter made a yoke according to the shoulder, because that is beautiful. But there is a problem I do not want to sweep away: **I have no documentation anywhere that he did anything of the kind.** What is documented is that yokes in his time were made to measure for the draught animal — and that Jesus spoke about a yoke. The rest I assembled myself, because that sentence about a kind yoke needed a body on which one could see what it means. **When an image leans on documented practice, it is not a lie. When it leans only on itself, it is a lie. Here it leans — but the boundary is thin, and I leave it visible.**

# IV · The Pitch

> **GROWTH RING IV** · the workshop in Nazareth, the fourth year, autumn
> *The one layer that is not seen, and yet decides everything.*

---

## Story

In autumn a cauldron of tar came into the workshop, and with it a smell that cannot be forgotten.

It was pitch. Dark, thick, fragrant of resin and of something older than resin — of the earth it is drawn from. It was brought in clay vessels, sometimes from pines that grew high, and sometimes from places where black matter pushes itself out of the ground and where people collect it from the surface of a lake. The second was dearer and better, because nothing penetrates it.

It was warmed in an iron pan until it was fluid as honey, and then applied with a brush of goat hair. It must not be too hot — it would burn the wood. It must not be cold — it would not soak in. The difference was known by a bubble: when a bubble formed on the surface, burst and left a white ring behind, it was right.

With this the carpenter coated the hull outside for a whole week. He went plank by plank, seam by seam. Into every seam he first pressed hemp soaked in the same pitch — because a seam you only smear over opens at the first blow of a wave. The hemp holds inside, the pitch covers outside.

When he had finished, he straightened and said: "Now the same from inside."

The apprentice, who had carried it all, stopped.

"Inside? Master, the water does not get in there."

The carpenter looked at him, and in that moment I understood for the first time that this chapter would not be about water.

"Come here," he said. "Sit down in the hull."

The apprentice crawled in. It was the first time he had been in there alone. The carpenter handed him a brush and a bucket.

"Now look above you. What is there?"

"The deck. Or there will be."

"And above the deck?"

"The sky."

"And what falls from the sky?"

The apprentice was silent.

"Rain," said the carpenter. "And it rains into the boat. Not because the boat is bad, but because it is a boat. A boat on the water is always under the sky." He sat down on the gunwale. "And then there is the second water. The one the people bring into the boat, the ones who sit in it. Sweat. Tears. Blood, when someone cuts himself on a hook. Water from wet nets. Water from a bucket that tips over. Water from hair that does not dry for three days."

He paused.

"When you pitch only the outside, you make a boat that looks good from the harbour. It lasts one summer. But the water that is born **inside** is the water that finally sinks a boat — because nobody thinks about it. That is why we pitch from inside. Not for the sea. For the people."

The apprentice took the brush and began. He coated wood that nobody would ever see — the inside of the planking, the lower edges of the ribs, the corners under the rowing bench, places where neither light nor glance arrives. It took him nine days.

When he finished, his hands were black all over, and it never came fully off the skin.

---

And then came the test that everyone who has ever built a boat knows — and which I want to tell slowly in this book, because it is the only moment when a boat on dry land does the opposite of what it is made for.

**They filled me with water.**

They brought buckets and began pouring water inside, into the hull that stood on trestles in the yard. The neighbours came to watch, because it looked like madness: four years of work, and now they pour water into it.

The water rose. It went through the seams between the planks, filled the space between the ribs, reached corners I had until then known only as darkness. It was the first time I **held** anything. All my life I had been wood that is bent, cut and nailed; now I was a vessel.

Beneath the trestles three drops appeared.

Three drops an hour. The apprentice went pale and ran for pitch. The carpenter caught him by the shoulder.

"Wait."

"But it is leaking!"

"It is leaking. Look where."

He pointed to one place by the stern where a plank met the keel. There, where the wood dried most.

"This is one seam. Today. In a year there will be five. In five years, twenty." He released him. "A boat is not made watertight once. A boat is made watertight **every year**."

"So I fix it and it will be good?"

"You fix it and it will be good until next spring. Then you check it again. And then again." The carpenter sat on the gunwale and for the first time in four years looked tired. "Listen to me, because this is more important than everything I have taught you about wood. **A boat someone cares for sails. A boat that is forgotten sinks — even if it was built perfectly.** You are not making a boat that will last. You are making a boat that can be repaired. That is the whole difference between pride and work."

---

In the evening, when everyone had gone and I still stood filled with water, the mother came into the workshop.

She carried a lamp. She set it on the table, walked around me and laid her palm on the hull — on the place where I had that seam under the rowing bench pitched from inside.

For a long time she said nothing.

Then she said, quietly, more to herself than to me:

**"What covers people is what carries them over the water."**

And she left. The water in me did not even stir.

---

## From Scripture, Tradition and History

- **Genesis 6:14** — "…and smear it with **pitch** inside and out." In Hebrew *ve-khafarta* — and the verb is from the root **k-p-r**. The same root carries *kófer* (pitch, ransom), *kappóret* (the cover of the mercy seat above the ark) and *yom kippúr* (the Day of Atonement). In Czech one might render it "you will pitch it and thereby **cover** it".
- **Leviticus 16** — the Day of Atonement: the high priest enters behind the curtain to the *kappóret*, to the Cover that **covers** the ark of the covenant. The word for cover and the word for pitch are the same family. Pitch, which keeps water out, and covering, which keeps guilt out, are one image in Hebrew.
- **Psalm 32:1** — "Blessed is the one whose transgression is **forgiven**, whose sin is **covered**." Literally: *covered*.
- **Proverbs 10:12** — "Hatred stirs up strife, but **love covers** all offences."
- **1 Peter 4:8** — "…love, which **covers** a multitude of sins." Peter quotes Proverbs and turns covering into ethics, not bookkeeping.
- **Pitch in boatbuilding** — documented practice: wooden hulls were pitched with natural resin and with bitumen. Asphalt from the Dead Sea region was a commodity in antiquity and was used precisely for sealing boats and baskets. Seams were additionally caulked with hemp or jute soaked in pitch.
- **Testing for leaks on dry land** is a documented and still-used method — the hull is filled with water and the seepage watched before the boat goes on the water.

## Measure

- **Amount of pitch** for a hull of this size: **40–60 kg** of dry matter, applied in **two to three coats**.
- **Thickness of a coat**: **1–2 mm** on surfaces, **3–5 mm** in seams (a seam is first raked out, filled with hemp and smeared over).
- **Drying time**: **3–7 days** between coats depending on the weather; the last coat hardens in **2–3 weeks**.
- **Added weight**: 40–60 kg of pitch is negligible for a boat of several tonnes displacement — but not for speed. Pitch is the tax for keeping the boat above water.
- **Three drops an hour**: **~15 ml/h** from one seam. Negligible on the water and catastrophic on dry land — because on dry land it means the seam is open and will open further.
- **Nine days** inside a hull where you cannot stand.

## Shaving

The most beautiful sentence of this chapter — *"what covers people is what carries them over the water"* — is mine. It is not from Scripture, not from tradition, not from any rabbinic commentary I could document. It arose from the root k-p-r, because that root really is there and because Hebrew really does use the same word for pitch and for covering. But **the connection, that pitch from inside is an image of care for what people carry within, is the image of this book.** I let it stand — and I let it be seen beside it that I made it myself. Had I kept silent about that, it would be a fine sentence and a bad book.

# V · The Oar

> **GROWTH RING V** · the road from Nazareth to the Kinneret and the first summer on the water, ~28 AD
> *The one part of the boat that needs a person.*

---

## Story

A boat standing in a yard is not a boat. It is an object.

Only when you move it do you find out what you have made.

They took me off the trestles at dawn, when the ground was still hard with frost and the road not yet muddy. They slid three rollers of olive wood under the keel, tied me with ropes and began to haul. There were twenty of us for the job. We walked all day and part of the night.

Nazareth lies at about three hundred and fifty metres. The Kinneret lies **two hundred and twelve metres below the level of the world ocean.** Between them there are five hundred and sixty metres of height and thirty kilometres of road, which slopes gently at first and falls at the end.

All that way I was upside down against myself. The ropes cut in, the rollers creaked, once the edge of the road gave way under one side and I tilted so far that I would have gone over had the men not held me. I heard one of them shout at another, and I heard the other not argue but simply brace himself and pull. And I heard the carpenter, who walked at the back the whole time with his hand on the stern, and who said not a word — only once, when it sagged, he said: "Slowly. We do not have to be on time. We have to get it there."

We reached the water at dawn.

And then came the moment every joiner remembers until he dies: **the launching.**

It was not a glory. There were tired men, wet ropes, two fishermen's boats already on the water, and a patch of sand. The carpenter stood knee-deep in the water holding me by the bow. Someone asked whether it would float. Someone else laughed.

And then he let go.

The water took me as nothing had ever taken me. It was not an impact — it was a **change of clothes**. Something opened beneath me and I laid myself into it along my whole length. In that moment I stopped being wood on dry land and became a thing that has water above it and beneath it, and whose only question is: *how much of it will I let in?*

I let in a little. The first day I soaked — the wood swells, the seams draw tight, the nails seat. Had I been of iron, nothing would have escaped and I would have burst. As it was, I leaked a little for three days and then stopped. It is said that a boat *drinks*. It is the only moment in a boat's life when it is weak — and it is right that it should be, because if it began at its strongest it would have nowhere to grow.

---

The first summer I sailed with the fishermen.

I learned what it means to be a working boat. It is not romance. It is the smell of fish that cannot be washed out. It is silence, because fish are afraid of noise. It is boredom and then sudden fear, when the net catches on the bottom and the boat heels. It is rhythm: stroke, stroke, ease, stroke.

From rowing I learned what I had not known until then: **a boat on its own can do nothing.**

The keel holds. The planking covers. The ribs carry. The pitch seals. But all of that is only the **possibility** of sailing. When a boat is empty and without oars, it is a bowl rocking in place until the wind throws it on the shore.

The oar is different. The oar is not part of the boat — it can be pulled out, set aside, lent, broken and replaced. And yet without it the boat does not make a single metre against the wind.

It is the only part the boat **does not own**. When a stroke sounds, it is not because the boat is good. It is because someone decided to pull.

---

In the spring of the second year it happened.

A crowd came down to the water. There were so many that they pressed on the shore, some stood knee-deep in the water and others had nowhere to go. And he — the one who built me — came, stepped into me, and asked Simon to push off a little from the shore. Not to sail away. To have distance from the wall.

Then he sat down in the stern and began to speak.

What happened next I explain by physics, because physics explains it well and I have no reason to invent a miracle where the shape of the shore will do. The Kinneret lies in a bowl. When you speak above the surface from a boat a little way from the shore, the sound travels over the water and returns from the opposite slope. It is not magic. It is **a theatre made by the landscape** — and the old fishermen knew it long before, because anyone who has worked on this lake has found that you hear further from the water than from the shore, and that a man does not have to shout for those at the back to hear him.

People stood and listened. And for the first time in my life I was not a boat that carries — I was a boat from which something is **said**.

---

When he had finished, he turned to Simon and told him to put out into the deep and let down the nets.

Simon was a man who had caught nothing all night. He was a man with calluses from ropes on his hands, who knew that if nothing was caught at night, nothing would be caught by day. He said so aloud — and then he said the second sentence, which I think is the most important sentence in this whole chapter:

**"But at your word I will let them down."**

Not that he believed. Not that he knew. That he did it.

The nets filled until they tore, and they had to call the second boat, and both sank low. Simon let go, fell on his knees in the wet silver glory of fish and said: "Depart from me, Lord, for I am a sinful man."

And he said to him the thing that stayed with me as a question for the rest of my life:

**"Do not be afraid. From now on you will be catching people."**

---

The men hauled the boats up on the shore, left the nets and went.

I stayed on the water.

I was confused, because a boat does not like unresolved things: when the fishermen leave and abandon the boat, does it mean they will come back, or that they have left it? For three days I rocked by the shore and waited for whoever would come for me.

Simon came. He took me by the rope and towed me to the place where he tied me. He said nothing to me. But from that moment I noticed he looked at me differently: not as a tool, but as a **place**.

And so I became a boat from which people spoke, and a boat to which people ran when the storm rose from the wrong side. The two are not the same. But both are permitted to a boat.

---

## From Scripture, Tradition and History

- **Luke 5:1–11** — "He got into one of the boats, which belonged to Simon, and asked him to put out a little from the shore; then he sat down and **taught the crowds from the boat**." Then the miraculous catch and the calling. Luke is the only one who develops the scene in detail.
- **Mark 1:16–20** — the calling of Simon and Andrew, James and John by the nets; "Come after me and I will make you **fishers of people**."
- **Matthew 4:19** — the same word. The image that recurs in the Gospels is not about a boat but about a **change of trade**: those who knew how to take from the water learn to take people out of the water.
- **The acoustics of the Kinneret** — the lake lies in a tectonic basin surrounded by slopes; sound spreads over the water and returns from the opposite slope. On the north-western shore there is a natural amphitheatre (the site known as *heptapégon*, "seven springs"), where even a quiet voice can be heard. Preaching from a boat is therefore not only a literary image — it is the **functionally best place**.
- **The Kinneret — the numbers** — the lowest freshwater lake on Earth, surface about **−212 m** below world ocean level; length ~**21 km**, width ~**13 km**, depth to ~**40 m**. It lies in a rift valley, which is why sudden short storms arise there, when air falls from the slopes into the basin.
- **The "drinking" of a new wooden boat** — wood swells at first contact with water and the seams draw tight. New hulls therefore leak slightly for the first days. It is a documented property of the material, not a defect.

## Shaving

That sentence about physics — that preaching from the boat worked because the shore is an amphitheatre — is true and yet dangerous. Had I written it without the rest, I would have explained the gospel by acoustics and taken from it what people look for in it. **I am not explaining what happened. I am explaining only why it worked even without a miracle.** Those are two different sentences, and the difference between them is the whole difference between honesty and arrogance.
