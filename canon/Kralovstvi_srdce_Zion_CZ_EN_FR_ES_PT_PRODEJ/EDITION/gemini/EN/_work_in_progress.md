## A Word Before We Sail

This book is a story. It tells of the carpenter of Nazareth who, in his father's workshop, did not only plane yokes for oxen, tables for bread and doors against the wind, but in the back corner under a cloth built a **boat** that nobody had ordered — and it follows how far that cedar wood, the little clay cup of salt and the empty alabaster jar travelled once the Sea of Galilee became the sea of ages and the sea of ages became the shore of the New Earth.

**It is not a biblical text, nor secret history.** The Gospels do not tell of a boat built by Jesus' own hands; they do know his trade (*tektōn*), they know the preaching from the deck of Simon's boat, they know the sleep on a cushion in the stern in the middle of a storm, they know the empty tomb in the garden and the fire on the shore. This book places into the silence of the hidden years in Nazareth the image of a single boat — in the way Jewish midrash and Christian legend have done since antiquity: not to rewrite Scripture, but so that one may hear how its images speak to one another.

And because this book stands at the very centre of the **ZION TerraNova** corpus, it does not end on the shore of the Sea of Galilee. In the second, third and fourth parts the cedar keel of Nazareth meets the city of Bezechuti and the five towers of Babylon (*The Salt of This Earth*, *Maitreya*), the child Lumi and her lantern on the ark by Mount Ararat (*Nirvana*, *The Book of the Lantern*), Miriam's empty jar and the speaking Rose by the river (*Miriam*, *The Story of the Rose*), the six gardens of the Earth and the seven seats around the round table (*Bodhi Gaia*, *The Circle of the Goddesses*), the window of an orbital station (*Issobella*), and finally the sails of light on the three hulls of *Tres Marías*, with the scribe Lumen's scroll, the father's letter to his son Surta, and the fourth vessel — the rescue *Ark*, which returns for those who remained in the water (*María del Camino*, *On the Way*, *Surta of the Heart*, *The Ark*).

## Prologue · At the Gate of Two Lights

At the edge of the New Earth, where the path from the harbour ends and the garden beneath the mountains begins, there stood a gate.

It was not vaulted in granite nor bound with iron. It was made of two lights: on the left a low, warm fire in a stone hearth, smelling of dry olive wood and resin; on the right a tall, quiet flame in a lantern carved from old cedar, hung on a post at the height of the eyes.

By the fire sat a woman in a dress the colour of the evening sky, a dot of sandal dust on her forehead and a bowl of coarse salt and a jug of forest honey in her lap. Her name was **Radha**. By the post with the lantern stood a second woman, bareheaded, in a cloak of unbleached linen. Her name was **Elizabeth**.

Radha welcomed everyone who came out of the mist, gave them a pinch of salt on bread and a drop of honey on the tongue so that they would remember the road has a taste, and she never asked where they came from. Elizabeth asked one thing only. Her lantern shone for those who would come in a hundred years, and from everyone who wished to cross the threshold she wanted to hear the answer:

*"What will remain of you when you move on?"*

That evening, in the hour between the third and the fourth night watch, when the darkness before dawn is thickest, two figures stepped out of the mist. A man in a cloak dusted by seven shores — in the corpus they simply call him **the Pilgrim** — and beside him a barefoot girl of barely six, with light curly hair and a pink sash, whom they had begun to call **Lumi** on the deck of the ark. The child had not spoken the whole way; in one hand she carried a small cedar lantern, exactly like the one on the post by the gate, and in the other an empty alabaster jar with an old crack near the bottom.

The Pilgrim stopped before the threshold. In his bag he had only a clay cup with the last of the salt from a distant lake and a bundle of written pages.

*"What will remain of you when you move on?"* Elizabeth asked.

The Pilgrim was silent for a long time. He could have spoken of the bridges he had helped to arch over chasms, of the numbers in ledgers, of the plans of ships whose keels nobody had yet laid. But in the light of that lantern every boast looked like chaff in the wind.

*"A story will remain of me,"* he said at last, quietly. *"It is not mine. It began two thousand years ago in the hills above Galilee, in a workshop where a carpenter built a boat for twelve people and one storm. That wood from his keel now holds up the ceiling of your house, the lantern in this little girl's hand is carved from its offcut, and his name is cut into the bottom of the ark we sailed in on."*

Elizabeth did not look away. She looked at the small girl at the Pilgrim's side — calmly, without surprise, the way a grown person looks into the surface of a well at their own childhood face.

*"There is often deep water between a story and the truth,"* Elizabeth said. *"Many have drowned in it, because they passed off a dream as stone and a wish as a finished boat."*

*"I know,"* the Pilgrim nodded. *"That is why at every plank I will say where it comes from. What is the stone of old documents, what is the clay of human memory, what already truly stands on this earth, and what is still only a drawing on a table."*

Radha smiled, took a pinch of coarse salt, placed it in the Pilgrim's palm and handed him a piece of bread.

*"Whoever is to speak until dawn must not speak of hunger,"* she said. *"Sit by the fire, pilgrim. And tell it from the first plank."*

The Pilgrim sat down on a basalt stone, Lumi laid her head against his knee, the two lanterns — the one in the child's hand and the one on the gatepost — quietly settled into a single rhythm, and he began.

> *Two lights at one gate:*
> *the fire warms the hands,*
> *the lantern weighs the words.*

## Chapter 1 · The Workshop in Nazareth and the Boat Nobody Ordered

### Story

In Nazareth, on a stony slope high above the lake basin, there stood a workshop that the whole village knew by its smell. Before you had even turned past the dry limestone wall you caught the bitter resin of Lebanese cedar, the sweetish dust of olive wood, and the warm oil with which the edges of the chisels were wiped after work. On wooden pegs along the wall hung drawknives, saws and mallets whose handles had been smoothed by the palms of three generations.

The carpenter grew up in that workshop. From the moment he could hold a mallet he listened to how wood answers a knock: a knotty oak that grew on a windy ridge sounds different from a straight cypress from the valley.

He made the things the village needed to live. He carved yokes for oxen — and before he handed a yoke to a farmer he went into the byre and laid his palms on the animal's neck so that no sharp edge remained anywhere to chafe it raw. He built oak tables that did not wobble even on an earthen floor, and heavy doors with pivots that kept the winter gale outside and the warmth of the hearth within. The people of Nazareth were not in the habit of praising, but about his work they said one thing: *what the carpenter joins does not come apart.*

Every evening, when he had swept up the shavings and the last neighbour had gone, his mother Miriam set on the table a clay bowl of coarse salt from the Dead Sea and a loaf of barley bread. Such was the law of their threshold: whoever entered received bread and salt before anyone asked them their name or about their debts. From that bowl the carpenter always tipped a pinch into a small fired cup that he carried in a leather bag at his belt.

But when the village fell quiet and only a narrow flame was left in the oil lamp, the carpenter drew back the coarse cloth in the back corner of the workshop. There, on wooden trestles, a **boat** was slowly growing.

It was not large — eight cubits long, just enough for twelve oarsmen and one place at the steering oar in the stern. He had cut its keel from a single trunk of old cedar that a storm had felled years before on the northern slopes, and he bent the ribs over a cauldron of boiling water as patiently as fingers are bent around a child's palm.

The neighbours, when they occasionally caught sight of the corner of the drawn-back cloth, shook their heads.

*"A boat, Yeshua? Here in the hills?"* the old potter laughed. *"It is a whole day's journey to Magdala and the Kinneret with an ox team. And the fishermen by the lake build their own boats in Capernaum. Who would buy a boat from you in the middle of dry rock?"*

The carpenter ran the plane along the gunwale, checked the line with his eye and smiled.

*"Nobody will buy it,"* he answered calmly. *"It is not for sale. I am building it for those who will need it one day."*

*"And when will they need it?"*

*"When the wind comes that their boats cannot handle,"* said the carpenter. *"A boat meant to carry people through a storm is not built at the moment the waves rise. It is built in the quiet years, while there is calm — every plank laid three times, every peg wedged twice — so that it holds when the calm is over."*

One thing, however, was missing from that boat, though the hull was almost finished: it had no mast with a sail. When the boy from next door, who came to hold the planks for him, asked about it, the carpenter shook his head:

*"The hull and the keel I must make myself, because they carry the weight. But a sail I will not sew. The sail must be brought by those who come aboard."*

One night, shortly before he was to leave the workshop for ever, his mother came in. For a long time she looked at the finished hull. Then she stepped to the keel, bent down and pressed her open palm against the cedar exactly where a boat has its centre of gravity — the way mothers lay a palm on a child's forehead when they want to know whether there is fever. The wood beneath her hand darkened with warmth and with a drop of oil from her fingers. She said not a word; she knew that this boat would sail further than the lake.

> *A keel from a single trunk,*
> *built in the years of silence,*
> *waits for a wind still to come.*

## Chapter 2 · Gofer and Kofer — Wood with a Scar, and the Pitch of Atonement

### Story

In choosing wood the carpenter was strict, but not in the way the dealers from Tiberias were. The dealers looked for planks straight as a ruler and threw every piece with a knot or a scar onto the firewood pile. The carpenter walked around the heaps behind the saw, took the discarded pieces in his hands and ran his fingertips over them for a long while.

The apprentice from the neighbouring yard, who helped him carry the boards, could not bear it one afternoon:

*"Master, why take exactly this oak rib? It is warped, and it has a grown-over wound from lightning. Straight wood planes up better."*

The carpenter laid his palm on the scarred grain of the wood, as if stroking the shoulder of an old soldier.

*"Straight wood grew in the lee,"* he said. *"It does not know the pull of the wind. When the first beam sea strikes it in the middle of the lake, it splits along its rings, because it never had to resist. But look at this piece: it grew on rock, it bent in storms, and where the lightning wounded it, it grew wood twice as dense around the wound. A crack in a life you must not fill with clay so that it stops showing. You must go around it with a peg and let it hold the hull at the point of greatest pressure."*

He paused, then added more quietly:

*"It is the same with people as with wood. Each grew in a different wind and each is bent to a different side. A bad builder cuts them all to one template until nothing is left but splinters. A good boat does not remake them. It sets their curves against one another so that one braces the other — and it carries them all."*

When the planking was fitted on pegs of hard acacia, the carpenter heated a cauldron of black pitch over the fire. The thick resin bubbled and smelled of pine, earth and smoke. He took it up on a wooden spoon and began to pour it carefully into every seam — first from outside along the whole belly of the boat, and then from the inside, under the benches and around the keel.

*"Why do you smear the inside too, master?"* the boy wondered. *"The water presses from outside, from the lake."*

*"From outside the lake presses,"* the carpenter agreed. *"But inside the boat sit people. And out of people runs sweat when they row against the wind, out of them run tears when they lose the shore, and rain falls on them from the clouds they carry in their heads. The pitch outside keeps the flood of the world outside. The pitch inside holds the crew together, so that their own pain does not rot away under their feet."*

He took the tip of a knife and showed the boy the thin black line between two cedar planks.

*"Do you know what the pitch is called, in the language of our fathers, with which the forefather Noah sealed the ark inside and out?"*

*"Kofer,"* answered the boy, who went to the synagogue.

*"Kofer,"* repeated the carpenter. *"And from the same root comes the word kapóret — the mercy seat above the ark of the covenant — and kipur, atonement. That which covers the seam between two crooked pieces of wood so that no evil water flows between them is called in the language of Scripture by the same name as forgiveness. Remember that when you build on your own one day: wood gives a boat its shape, but only what covers its seams carries it across the deep."*

> *Crooked wood from a windy cliff,*
> *joined with the pitch of atonement:*
> *what you cover will not leak.*

## Chapter 3 · Kinneret — a Voice Carried by Water and Nets in the Deep

### Story

Spring came when the carpenter put aside his apron, washed his hands in rainwater and took the cloth off the boat.

They brought it down from the hills of Nazareth at daybreak on a two-wheeled cart drawn by oxen. The carpenter walked the whole way beside the cart with his palm resting on the cedar side, the way one leads a young horse that hears the rumble of wheels for the first time. When the pass at Arbel opened before them, the surface of the lake of Kinneret blazed below in the basin — blue, drawn out like the harp it was named after.

By the shore near Capernaum it was busy, but without joy. The fishermen were just hauling two boats up onto the pebbles and, in the silence that comes only after a ruined night's labour, washing green weed and fine gravel out of the mesh of their nets. All night they had thrown their nets into the dark and had not pulled out so much as a fin.

The carpenter left his new boat lying on wooden rollers a little apart. Its hour was to come only in the evening.

While the sun climbed, people from Capernaum and the surrounding hamlets gathered by the shore — day labourers, women with baskets, tax collectors, and those who were not allowed into the front rows of the synagogue. They pressed right down to the water. The carpenter looked around, stepped into one of the hauled-up boats, which belonged to a fisherman named Simon, and asked him to push off a few cubits from the shore.

He sat down in the bow, facing the crowd on the pebbles, and began to speak.

He did not speak from the scholars' platform nor from the stone seat in the synagogue. He spoke from swaying wood above the water — and the surface of the lake beneath him worked like the soundboard of a harp: it carried every word of his clearly and far, so that even the last ones, standing behind the fig trees on the slope, heard him.

When he had finished and the people were dispersing in silence, the carpenter turned to Simon, who sat at the oars with his eyes red from sleeplessness.

*"Put out into the deep,"* he said, *"and let down your nets for a catch."*

Simon looked at his palms, torn by wet ropes, and then at the sun, already high — at the hour when the fish have long gone down to the bottom and no sensible fisherman on the Kinneret casts a net.

*"Master,"* he breathed wearily, *"we have toiled all night and caught nothing at all. But at your word I will let down the nets."*

They rowed out into the deep water and threw the net over the side. The ropes tautened at once like bowstrings. So many silver bodies flashed in the deep that the mesh began to tear. Simon waved to his partners in the second boat — James and John, the sons of Zebedee — and when they came alongside and transferred the catch into both boats, both sank so low that the water reached the edge of the gunwale.

Simon dropped to his knees among the fish.

*"Depart from me, Lord,"* he whispered, his face in his hands, *"for I am a sinful man."*

The carpenter bent to him and laid a hand on his shoulder — the same firm hand with which he had measured the necks of oxen in Nazareth.

*"Do not be afraid,"* he said. *"From now on you will be catching people."*

When they came to the shore, the fishermen hauled both boats up onto dry land, left the nets and the silver heap of fish to those who remained, and followed him.

Only when it had grown dark and the shore had fallen quiet did the carpenter lead Simon, Andrew, James and John to the cedar boat they had brought down from the hills that morning. Together they knocked out the wedges and lowered it on the rollers into the lake. It slipped into the water without a single splash, like a word returning to the mouth that spoke it.

When the carpenter later called all twelve into it on the mountain — Simon called Peter, Andrew, the sons of thunder James and John, Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew the tax collector who counted everything, doubting Thomas, James son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, Simon the Zealot and Judas Iscariot, who carried the common purse — they found that the boat had exactly twelve planed benches. Nobody had to crowd and nobody was left over. And right at the back, by the steering oar, lay a cushion of coarse linen stuffed with shavings from the Nazareth workshop.

*"Twelve benches,"* counted Simon Peter, and looked at the carpenter. *"Did you measure us out already in Nazareth, before you met us?"*

*"I was building benches for twelve tempers that hold together only when they row in one boat,"* answered the carpenter. *"And that cushion in the stern is the thirteenth place — for the one who can sleep calmly, because he knows how the keel is joined."*

> *A voice from the water reaches the last ones,*
> *the net in the deep bursts with fullness,*
> *twelve benches had waited since the first cut.*

## Chapter 4 · Magdala — the Salt of the Earth and the Woman from the Tower

### Story

The fish from that morning's catch could not be eaten in a single day. Anyone who lives by the Kinneret knows the merciless law of heat: what you do not treat with salt by evening rots in the sun by the third day and feeds nobody.

And so they headed south along the western shore, to the town the Jews called **Magdala** — after the stone watchtower above the harbour — and the Greeks **Tarichea**, that is, *the town of salted fish*.

Salt could be smelled in the air there before you saw the first roofs. Caravans of pack animals brought it in sacks from the south, from the Salt Sea, and in the paved yards by the harbour stood long rows of stone and wooden vats. Women in aprons, their hands cracked and white from brine, laid gutted fish layer upon layer and interspersed them with coarse crystals so that the fish would survive the journey as far as Jerusalem, into Syria and beyond the sea.

At one of the vats stood a woman with dark hair bound in a scarf. They called her **Miriam of Magdala**. She had once been ill so deeply that people said seven foreign voices were pulling at one another inside her; from the moment the carpenter had given her back her calm and her own name in a single sentence, she had walked with his company and bought flour, oil and bread out of her own means for all who followed him.

The carpenter stopped at her vat and watched how her fingers scattered the white crystals evenly among the fish.

*"Why do you look at it like that, Rabboni?"* Miriam asked, wiping her forehead with her forearm. *"There is nothing noble in it. Only salt and fish."*

*"The whole kingdom is in it, Miriam,"* said the carpenter quietly. *"If there were no salt in your hands, the miracle of this morning would stink on the shore by the day after tomorrow. The world thinks a miracle is a full net. But it takes only a handful of salt to keep a full net alive."*

Miriam reached into the clean linen pouch at her waist, took a handful of the purest rock salt and, without a word, poured it into the carpenter's clay cup, next to the pinch he carried from his mother's table in Nazareth.

When the disciples, the fishermen and the women from the vats had gathered around them on the harbour wall, the carpenter raised the cup and said the sentence they then repeated all over Galilee:

*"You are the salt of the earth."*

He looked around at their calloused, salt-eaten hands. None of them was a scribe of the temple council, none wore a purple hem.

*"Notice how salt works,"* he went on. *"Salt does not shout that it is the main thing on the table. If it wanted to be the whole dish, nobody would swallow it. Salt does one thing: it dissolves in the bread and in the fish, it vanishes from sight — and precisely by that it gives them taste and keeps them from ruin. But if salt loses its saltiness, what will you salt with it? Then it is fit neither for the soil nor for the dung heap; it is thrown out onto the road and people trample it. Have salt in yourselves — and live at peace with one another."*

In the evening, as the boat sailed along the shore back towards Capernaum and the air smelled of smoke from the fishermen's fires, Simon Peter asked from the oar:

*"Master, to be salt — is that a task we must fulfil, or a state we must not lose?"*

The carpenter laid his hand on the cup in his bag.

*"Salt does not invent what it will be, Simon,"* he said. *"Salt simply is. And as long as it does not forget what it is, it holds every covenant together."*

> *The miracle hauls up a full net,*
> *but only the salt that dissolves*
> *keeps the gift from going mouldy.*

## Chapter 5 · The Storm on the Lake and the Builder's Sleep

### Story

The lake of Kinneret lies deep below sea level, squeezed between the Golan Heights and the hills of Galilee. Anyone who knows it only in the morning calm thinks it is a tame surface. But towards evening, when cold air from the mountains plunges down the narrow gorges into the warm basin as into a funnel, the lake can turn black within a quarter of an hour and raise waves as tall as a man.

That day the carpenter taught by the shore until dusk. When the sun sank behind the ridge, he said to the disciples a sentence that sounded ordinary, but in which the whole journey of this book was hidden:

*"Let us cross over to the other shore."*

They untied the rope and set out. The carpenter was so tired after the whole day that he went to the stern, laid his head on the linen cushion stuffed with shavings from Nazareth, rested his palm against the cedar keel and fell asleep at once.

In the middle of the lake the funnel above the mountains opened.

The squall struck the side without warning. Waves broke over the gunwale, salt foam sprayed into faces and the bottom of the boat began to fill with black water. Simon, Andrew, James and John had been fishermen since childhood — they knew every bay and had outlasted dozens of storms — but now the oars jumped from their tholes and the wooden buckets could not bail fast enough.

And the carpenter in the stern slept.

He slept calmly and deeply, not because he could not hear the thunder, but because his ear rested directly above the cedar keel. He felt how the oak ribs — those crooked ribs from the windy cliffs that he himself had chosen in Nazareth — flexed under the weight of the waves and came back. He felt that the *kofer* pitch held in every seam. He knew his boat from the inside, and therefore he knew what it could bear.

*"Master!"* Simon Peter shouted over the roar of the wind and shook him by the shoulder. *"Do you not care that we are perishing?"*

The carpenter opened his eyes and sat up. He did not look first at the waves; he looked at the blanched faces of his twelve. Then he stood, set his bare feet on the wet deck and looked into the dark from which the wind was pouring — calmly, matter-of-factly, the way a craftsman looks at a piece of work in progress that has jammed under the chisel.

*"Be silent,"* he said to the wind. And to the water he said: *"Be still."*

The squall broke in the middle of a breath. The crests of the waves fell and such immense quiet settled on the lake that only the slow dripping of water from the oars and the beating of thirteen hearts could be heard.

The carpenter turned to the disciples:

*"Why are you so afraid? Do you still have no faith?"*

They did not answer. They looked at the smooth, mirror-like dark around the boat and a fear greater than in the middle of the squall took hold of them. They whispered to one another among the benches:

*"Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?"*

And old Matthew, in the corner of his bench, remembered the words of the hundred and seventh psalm he used to hear in the synagogue: *of those who go down to the sea in ships, who do their work on the great waters, who cry out in trouble — and He stills the storm to a gentle breeze, so that the waves fall silent, and brings them to the harbour they long for.*

> *Whoever did not build the boat shouts at the waves.*
> *Whoever knows every rib and every seam*
> *sleeps with his head in the stern — and the silence obeys him.*

## Chapter 6 · Five Loaves, Twelve Baskets and a Step Upon the Water

### Story

When the Twelve returned from their first journey through the villages, their mouths were full of stories and their hands were empty. But so many crowds pressed around the carpenter that they had no time even to eat bread.

*"Come away by yourselves to a lonely place,"* the carpenter said to them, *"and rest a little."*

They got into the boat and headed for the eastern shore. But the people on the shore recognized the carpenter's boat by the shape of its bow, ran on foot out of every little town along the lake, outran the bow as it laboured against the headwind, and when the keel settled into the sand of a lonely bay, five thousand men were already waiting there, not counting women and children.

The carpenter was not angry with them. He saw that they were like sheep without a shepherd, and he taught them until late afternoon. When the sun began to lean towards the western mountains, the disciples came to him:

*"This place is lonely and the hour is already late. Send them away, so that they can go to the surrounding farms and villages and buy something to eat."*

The carpenter looked at them and said a sentence that overturned the bookkeeping of the whole shore:

*"You give them something to eat."*

Matthew, who still had numbers in his blood from the customs house, calculated the cost at once: *"If we went and bought bread for two hundred denarii — half a year's wages for a labourer — not even a mouthful would reach everyone!"*

*"Do not go and buy,"* said the carpenter. *"Go and look how many loaves you have with you in the boat."*

Andrew came back leading a small boy in a coarse smock by the hand.

*"There is a boy here,"* he said, somewhat embarrassed. *"He has five barley cakes in a bundle — the bread of the poor — and two dried fish salted in Magdala. But what is that for so many people?"*

The boy, however, was not afraid. He simply handed the bundle to the carpenter, without conditions and without a form — the way one gives what one has brought from home.

The carpenter told the people to sit down in groups of a hundred and of fifty on the green grass. He took the five loaves and the two fish, looked up to heaven, gave thanks, broke the loaves and handed them to the Twelve to carry from circle to circle. And something happened that Matthew's counting board could not record: as long as the bread went from hand to hand and nobody hid it under a cloak for tomorrow, the bread did not run out. Everyone ate until they were satisfied. And when they gathered the broken pieces so that nothing should be lost, they filled **twelve baskets** with them — exactly one full basket into the hands of each of the twelve doubting oarsmen.

Immediately after, when he saw that the crowd wanted to proclaim him king in its enthusiasm and set up a new throne above the shore, the carpenter made the disciples get into the boat themselves and sail ahead to Bethsaida. He himself dismissed the people and went up into the dark on the mountain, to be in silence with the Father.

In the night a sharp headwind rose over the lake again.

The boat was in the middle of the waves, many furlongs from either shore, and this time the carpenter was not in it. The cushion in the stern lay empty. Twelve men strained at the oars, sweat running into their eyes — and yet in the middle of that labour they noticed one thing: the cedar keel beneath them did not twist and the oak ribs did not give. **The boat held in the storm even when the builder was not on board.**

Between the third and the fourth night watch, in that darkest hour before dawn, they saw on the crests of the waves a figure walking on the water towards them. They cried out in terror, because they thought they were seeing a ghost.

But out of the wind came a calm voice they knew:

*"Take heart. It is I. Do not be afraid."*

Simon Peter caught hold of the gunwale and called into the dark: *"Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you on the water!"*

*"Come,"* said the carpenter.

Simon swung his leg over the cedar side, let go of the wood and stood on the surface. He took the first step. The second. The third. As long as he looked into the eyes of the one who had said *Come*, the water carried him as firmly as basalt rock. But then he lowered his gaze to the black deep beneath his bare feet, felt the blow of the squall, calculated in his head that a person cannot walk on water — and in that moment he began to sink.

*"Lord, save me!"*

The carpenter reached out, caught him firmly by the wrist and pulled him up out of the foam: *"You of little faith, why did you doubt?"*

When they stepped together over the side into the boat, the wind lay down at once. And those who sat on the twelve benches did not say this time *Who is this?* — they bowed before him and confessed: *"You are truly the Son of God."*

> *As long as the bread is broken and passed on, it is enough for five thousand.*
> *The boat holds in the storm even without the builder in the stern —*
> *and the water carries the one who looks at the caller, not into the abyss.*

## Chapter 7 · The Kingdom Is Within You — the Name on the Keel

### Story

On the way back to Capernaum the Twelve argued in the boat.

They spoke under their breath so that it would not be heard in the stern, but water and a cedar hull carry every whisper. They quarrelled about the benches: who sits nearest the steering oar, who carried the heavier oar in the storm, and who will sit at his right and at his left when the carpenter finally proclaims his kingdom. Even twelve justly divided benches can be turned into a ladder by human pride.

When they had moored the boat at Capernaum, the Pharisees came to them — men who measured the coming of the Messiah by signs in the sky and by scrolls with dates. They stood above the boat and asked the carpenter outright:

*"When will the kingdom of God come?"*

The carpenter looked at their scrolls, then at the twelve oarsmen who a moment before had been competing for the front places, and finally at the quiet surface of the lake.

*"The kingdom of God does not come in a way that can be watched for from towers,"* he answered them. *"Nobody will say, 'Look, here it is!' or 'Look, there it is!' For behold — **the kingdom of God is within you.** And it is among you every time you sit together in one boat and nobody wants to be above another."*

The Pharisees shrugged and left; whoever looks for the kingdom in calendars and boundary stones goes away empty when he is sent into his own heart.

But a small child remained standing on the shore — a barefoot girl from a fisherman's hut who had been playing with a piece of wood and listening. The carpenter sat down on the gunwale of the boat, called the Twelve around him, took the child by the hand and set her right in the middle of the hull, on the coiled rope between the benches, where there was neither a first nor a last place.

*"If anyone wants to be first,"* he said to the Twelve, *"let him be last of all and servant of all. Whoever receives one such child in my name receives me."*

Matthew bowed his head and stopped counting merits. James and John moved along the bench to make room for the child.

Then Simon Peter went to the bow, knelt by the wood and asked the thing that nobody had dared to ask aloud since they left Nazareth:

*"Master… every boat on the Kinneret has a name burned or carved into its side, after its owner or its town. This one is still smooth. What is it actually called?"*

The carpenter stood up, lifted the floorboard in the middle of the boat — exactly where the child was standing — and pointed to the upper edge of the cedar keel.

In the dimness under the planks, protected by a layer of clear resin, two words were carved there with a deep chisel:

> **KINGDOM OF THE HEART**

And directly beneath them, on the lower edge of the keel facing the water, a smooth, empty surface remained, without a single letter.

*"It has had its name since the first day in Nazareth,"* said the carpenter. *"I carved it inside, under your feet, not on the side for boasting in the harbour. Because this boat was not built only for the Kinneret. The Kinneret is the first practice water. This boat sails inward — there where fear and compassion are born in a person — and it sails across the ages. It is called Kingdom, because nobody ever sails in it alone. And it is called Heart, because there is the only helm that does not fail when the stars go out."*

*"And that empty place down under the keel?"* asked John.

*"That is the place for a signature,"* answered the carpenter. *"It will be signed there by the one who comes when the world has forgotten the way — and after him by everyone who understands that a boat is not owned, but handed on."*

> *The name is not on the side for harbour officials.*
> *It is carved inside, beneath the child's feet:*
> *Kingdom of the Heart — within you and among you.*

## Chapter 8 · The Alabaster Jar and the Wood of the Cross

### Story

The last spring came, and the carpenter turned his steps away from the lake and up towards Jerusalem. The boat remained moored in the bay by Capernaum, covered with cloth, but the air around the company grew heavy as before a storm such as the Kinneret had not yet known. Three times on the way he told them what awaited him in the city, and three times they did not understand him; when he told them of the prophet Jonah, who was three days and three nights in the belly of a great fish and so too would the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth, they thought he was speaking in riddles.

Two days before the feast of unleavened bread they were sitting at table in Bethany, in the house of Simon the Leper.

A woman entered the room carrying in her palms an **alabaster jar** full of pure nard — translucent stone with veins like scars, sealed with wax, worth three hundred denarii, a labourer's yearly wage. She brought no ledger and asked for no receipt. She came to the carpenter, **broke the neck of the jar**, so that it could never again be stoppered or sold, and poured all the oil over his head and his feet. The whole house was filled with the scent of nard, stronger than the fear of death.

An irritated grumbling rose at the table. Judas Iscariot, who held the purse, and with him the others, counted aloud:

*"Why this waste? This oil could have been sold for more than three hundred denarii and the money given to the poor!"*

The carpenter raised his hand and stopped them:

*"Leave her alone. Why do you trouble her? She has done a good thing for me. You always have the poor with you, and whenever you wish you can do them good; but you do not always have me. She has done what she could: she has anointed my body beforehand for burial. Amen, I tell you, wherever the gospel is proclaimed in the whole world, what she has done will also be told — in remembrance of her."*

That night Miriam of Magdala hid the empty alabaster jar, cracked near the bottom, under her cloak. She did not yet know that inside, in the thin crack of the stone where the last drop of nard had run, a single tiny seed of a wild rose from the garden in Bethany had caught. She knew only what she would say years later in a cave above the sea to the girl Sarah: *"A full jar can be stolen from you by a king or a thief. An emptied jar nobody will ever take from you again — because what has been surrendered cannot be confiscated."*

Then came the night in the garden of Gethsemane, where the carpenter sweated blood like a helmsman who feels the blow of a wave in his own chest before it reaches the hull. There came the kiss at the olive press, the ropes and the courts. Simon Peter, who had walked on water on the lake, swore three times by the fire in the courtyard that he did not know the man — and when the cock crowed and the carpenter looked at him quietly across the courtyard, Simon went out and wept bitterly.

And in the morning came the wood that no carpenter prepares for life.

It was a rough, unworked beam of a cross. The carpenter who had spent his life planing yokes so that they would not press on the neck carried on his broken shoulders the timber up the skull-shaped hill of Golgotha. There hands that could sense every grain of cedar were nailed with iron spikes to the wood.

The men of the twelve benches scattered like fish when the net breaks; of the disciples only the young John remained beneath the cross. But **the women did not run**. Three Marys stood there in the grey wind — the carpenter's mother Miriam, who had once laid her palm on the keel in Nazareth; his aunt Mary of Clopas; and Miriam of Magdala with the empty alabaster jar under her cloak. They said nothing. They knew that to run away means to leave the seed without a witness — and they were resolved to remain witnesses to the end.

Between the sixth and the ninth hour darkness fell over the whole land. The carpenter cried out in a mighty voice: *"It is finished"* — and bowed his head.

Before sunset, before the sabbath began, Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus took the body down from the wood, wrapped it with myrrh and aloes in clean linen, laid it in a new tomb cut in the rock in the middle of a garden, and rolled a great stone to the entrance. Miriam of Magdala and the other Mary sat opposite the tomb and watched where he was laid.

Down in Capernaum the carpenter's boat lay hauled up on the dry shore. The twelve benches were empty, the wind turned the linen cushion in the stern, and it seemed that the voyage had ended at the bottom.

> *A jar broken out of love can no longer be stolen.*
> *The carpenter nailed to rough wood sank for three days into the deep,*
> *and the women remembered the place where the seed was laid.*

## Chapter 9 · The Gardener in the Dark and the Fire on the Shore

### Story

On the first day after the sabbath, while it was still deep dark, Miriam of Magdala rose and went with the other Mary to the garden beneath the walls.

They found the stone rolled away from the tomb. ## Chapter 10 · Navicella and the Boat Without Sails

### Story

When the carpenter vanished from sight, the boat on the Kinneret did not stay tied to the jetty.

On the day of Pentecost, when a roar like a violent wind rose over Jerusalem and people of a dozen tongues understood one another for the first time without an interpreter, three thousand souls stepped onto its deck at once. The fisherman Simon, called the Rock, stood at the steering oar — and the boat sailed out of the lake onto the wide sea of the centuries.

People gave it different names: in Greek they called it *barka*, in Latin **navicella** — *little ship* — and they built the main spaces of their temples with a vault turned upside down like the wooden ribs of a hull, and called them the *nave*.

It sailed through the ages when emperors wanted to sink it with fire and iron, and it did not go down. But then a harder trial came: the emperors stopped sinking it, they seated helmsmen in purple, bound its sides with gold and built tall towers on the deck. The ship grew so heavy that it barely moved. The crew in the hold was divided by bulkheads, the individual decks waged war on one another, and every partition claimed that it alone was the whole ship. Human pride leaked into the hold.

And yet it did not sink.

It did not sink because its anchor was not dropped down into Roman or Byzantine mud. Its anchor was thrown **upward** — behind the curtain of the invisible world, as the writer of the Letter to the Hebrews put it: *hope as an anchor for the soul, firm and secure*. And whenever the helmsmen in purple forgot whose ship they were steering, someone simple emerged from the hold — a poor man of Assisi who gave away his cloak and spoke with birds, a barefoot woman tending lepers, a village priest standing before the barrels of tanks, or a grandmother who behind locked shutters taught her grandchildren to break bread with salt — and knocked his palm on the cedar bottom beneath the floorboards and reminded the crew of the name carved in the keel: **Kingdom of the Heart.**

At the same time as the great *Navicella*, however, another, far quieter boat was sailing the sea.

In Provence, in the south of Gaul, people told a story about it for centuries which the Book of the Rose calls **clay** — a shape kneaded by a thousand human hands. When the first great persecution broke out in Jerusalem, the enemies of the carpenter's friends — Miriam of Magdala, Mary of James, Mary Salome and with them a dark girl named **Sarah** — seated them in an old skiff **without sails, without oars and without a rudder** and pushed them off the shore onto the wide Mediterranean, to perish without a grave.

The carpenter in Nazareth had once said to the boy at the workbench: *"I will not sew the sail. The sail must be brought by those who come aboard."*

The women in the boat without sails knew what a sail is sewn from when both cloth and oars are taken from a person. It is sewn from **faithfulness**, which did not run from the cross, and from **witness**, which remembers where the seed was laid. Wind and sea current carried their defenceless hull for whole weeks, until the keel slid softly onto a sandbank in the Rhône delta — at the place people still call *Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer*, the Holy Marys of the Sea.

Old Miriam then, according to the same legend, climbed to a limestone cave in the mountain of Sainte-Baume, while young Sarah — whom the Roma call *Sara e Kali*, Sarah the Black — brought her bread and water and listened to her telling, carefully divided into **stone** (what she really saw by the lake and at the tomb), **clay** (what people added along the way) and **dream** (what the powerful dreamed up about royal blood and golden chalices, because they could not understand that a true lineage has calluses on its palms from a carpenter's chisel).

And when the last sentence died away in the cave, Miriam placed in Sarah's hands the only thing she had brought from Galilee: the **empty alabaster jar** with a crack near the bottom.

*"You must not fill it with gold,"* Miriam told her. *"You carry it on through the centuries to an open gate, where a child with a lantern of Nazareth cedar is waiting by a river."*

> *The great ship sails through the storms of ages with its anchor thrown upward,*
> *the little boat without sails crosses the sea on mere faithfulness,*
> *and in the empty alabaster a single seed travels through time.*

## Chapter 11 · The City of Bezechuti and the Five Towers of Babylon

### Story

Time flowed like a river that sometimes disappears into the sandstone and a hundred miles further bursts again from the rock.

At the end of one of those long ages there stood in a broad plain a city that nobody wanted to call by its true name, and yet in the end everyone named it so: **Bezechuti**.

It was neither a poor city nor a ruined one. On the contrary — above its walls **five enormous towers of Babylon** rose into the clouds, so high that the people below already took their shadow for the natural colour of the sky:

1. **The Tower of Money** — the oldest of all. At the top, in the last storey, a machine rattled day and night printing paper consents, while down at its foot debt was being born. Every loaf of bread in the marketplace carried within it a fee for permission to sell and interest on a consent that nobody remembered having borrowed.
2. **The Tower of Data** — the tallest and the smoothest. Above its gate shone the inscription *"Entry free"* and beneath it in small print: *"Sign in, so that you may exist."* People brought it their faces, their letters and their fears, so that in its mirrors they could see that they were real, and the tower built storeys out of their faces and rented them to buyers of attention.
3. **The Tower of Energy** — the only one that blazed at night. It drew current from the sky through a thick cable and sent light into every house through a socket, together with an invoice, so that people would forget that sun and wind fall on roofs for nothing.
4. **The Tower of Meaning** — full of heralds in shining robes who told the city every evening what it should fear by morning, whom it should envy and what the word "success" means. Their single commandment ran: *"Believe us, you have no time to count it yourself."*
5. **The Tower of the Throne** — the finest and the most dangerous. It did not stand in the square of stone; it stood in the head of everyone who was given a key, and it whispered a single word: *"Exception. Decide it yourself this once, without the others, and you will save time."*

He sat down under an old fig tree at the edge of the marketplace, where those sat for whom none of the five towers had a free storey: a water carrier, a soldier without pay with a scarred palm, a market woman who had baked bread on credit for twenty years, a smith whose forge had burned down, and a woman who had buried three children and still carried buckets to the well for the neighbours' children.

The carpenter set the clay cup on the ground in the middle of the circle.

*"What are we to do with that?"* the water carrier smirked. *"That is ordinary salt. We are hungry."*

*"Try your bread with it,"* said the carpenter.

The old soldier broke off a piece of hard flatbread, pressed it against the coarse crystals from the cup and bit into it. For a moment he did not move.

*"It tastes… different,"* he said then, slowly, like a man who after twenty years of darkness has recognized his mother's voice. *"It has taste."*

*"You are the salt of the earth,"* the carpenter told them quietly. *"The towers think they hold the city, but you hold it. And towers do not fall because you throw a torch at them — whoever fights a tower with fire builds a new tower on its ashes. Towers fall on the day you stop going to them for consent."*

Only the fifth tower remained — **the Tower of the Throne**. But for that one to fall too, a boat first had to be finished in the middle of the square.

*"The thirteenth place,"* he said. *"On the lake it was for the one who slept because he trusted the keel. On the ark it belongs also to the one who sings in the middle of the dark."*

The next morning, while dew still lay on the keel, the smith found on the underside of the cedar — exactly in that empty place the carpenter had left by the Kinneret for times to come — a freshly carved signature:

> *Maitreya Buddha, 2025*

And the smith's son, who read the *Book of Genesis* by lantern light at night, discovered that the same signature stands beneath the proclamation of the **Game of the Golden Egg** — of one hundred and eight steps and three keys (copper for persistent work, jasper for patience with the insoluble, and crystal for truth spoken even against one's own advantage).

*"Whose is that egg, father?"* asked the boy. *"His who finds it — or mine, whose name you wrote into the first block of the world?"*

The smith took his son into the workshop and showed him the parchment with the first message of the chain (*"For Sarah Issobel, Maitreya Buddha, Radha & Sita, Meriam… ZION is yours"*) and beside it a letter wrapped in pitch-soaked cloth with five rules for Generation Z.

Because at the egg the ego must die, so that the Golden Age may be born."*

*"And what then do I inherit, when you have signed my name beneath the game?"*

The smith led him inside the hull of the Ark. There, below the deck, stood **four seats**:

*"You do not inherit the treasure from the egg,"* the father told his son. *"You inherit the seat of a steward. ZION is not yours — it belongs to all. You are stewards, not owners. Five per cent of every block goes to the children and to the earth — that is the breath of the boat, never change it. And if I were to disappear on the way and not be heard from for five years, I have built into the helm a safeguard that will hand you the key by itself."*
