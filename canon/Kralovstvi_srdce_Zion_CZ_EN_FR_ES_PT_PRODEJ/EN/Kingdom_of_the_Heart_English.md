# Kingdom of the Heart

Zion

The Story of a Boat Built by a Carpenter

Complete English working edition · 3 November 2026

## A Word Before We Sail

This book is a literary legend. The craft of Jesus, the events of the Gospels, later traditions, and the original stories of the world of Zion meet here in the image of a boat built by a carpenter. The Gospels do not tell of the building of this boat. The invented conversations and parables are not newly discovered sayings of Jesus.

In the second part of the story, people cross centuries, and light connects different ages. The gardens, fleets, and inhabited heavens belong to the legend; the book neither describes their present physical existence nor determines the future. Readers may come aboard without knowing the other books of Zion.

## Prologue · Light Before Dawn

Two lights burned by the gate. One was in the stone hearth where Radha was baking bread. The other shone behind a piece of glass in a small cedar lantern. Its flame did not move with the wind. Yet when footsteps sounded in the mist, it leaned towards the path. Elizabeth watched it. On her sleeve was a patch that none of those approaching would have known how to sew. Her country lay so far in the future that not even the names of the cities along this coast remained. Something else had survived there: at supper, they always laid one extra place. Once she had asked why. They told her it came from a certain boat. So she had set out to find its beginning.

A man emerged from the mist. He was soaked through and carried a bundle of pages under his arm. Beside him walked a small barefoot girl with fair hair, holding a lantern in both hands. The grown woman at the gate slowly covered her own lamp with her palm; its light went on passing between her fingers, in the same rhythm as the child's. The man noticed. He said nothing.

“Pilgrim,” Radha greeted him, moving a bowl aside on the stone so that he could sit down.

Once they had called him Zion Native. The First Pilgrim of Light. He had carried the name for so long that it had become a duty which would not let him admit he was tired. Now he was glad that, at this fire, pilgrim was enough. The child set her lantern at his feet.

“Are we late?” she asked.

“Just in time for bread,” Radha replied.

Elizabeth looked at the little girl's grazed thumb, her wet hair, and the small knot she had tied in her torn sash. She remembered that knot. But she had forgotten who had helped her tie it.

“What is your name?” the man asked.

“Elizabeth.”

The girl raised her head.

“So is mine. But on the boat they call me Lumi.”

The woman crouched beside her. Too quickly; her knee cracked, and the child smiled.

“That is a lovely name,” she said.

“Haven't you heard it before?”

Elizabeth slowly ran a finger along the handle of her lantern. In one place the cedar was deeply dented, as though a hand had once gripped it in danger.

“I have. But there are names you have to hear again before you understand them.”

The man put down his bundle of pages. From his bag he took a little clay cup. A few crystals of salt remained at the bottom.

“What will you leave behind when you move on?” Elizabeth asked.

He wanted to answer. He knew the great sentences people wrote at the beginnings of books. He knew the promises that made people lift their heads towards the stars. But at that moment he remembered the face of someone he had once failed to go back for.

“I don't know,” he said.

Radha broke a loaf and placed half of it in his hands.

“Then we have plenty of time.”

“You don't know where I come from,” Elizabeth said. “There, no one remembers your face any more. Or the carpenter's. But we still leave a place at the table. I need to know whom we were waiting for.”

The Pilgrim looked up from the bread.

“And will you tell me whether that boat reached its shore?”

“I can tell you what we inherited. I cannot promise what you will do with it.”

Lumi sat down beside his knee. From a fold of her cloak she took an empty alabaster jar and set it beside the little cup. Warmth brought out the scent of the lamp's wood. In that scent the Pilgrim recognised a workshop where he had never slept, yet for which he had longed all his life. He began to tell the story. Not with the stars. With a man sweeping up wood shavings in the evening.

# Part I · The Carpenter and the Lake

## Chapter 1 · The Workshop in Nazareth and the Boat No One Had Ordered

In Nazareth, on a stony slope high above the lake basin, stood a workshop the whole village knew by its smell. Before you had even rounded the dry limestone wall, you could smell the bitter resin of Lebanese cedar, the sweet dust of olive wood, and the warm oil rubbed into the edges of the chisels when the day's work was done. Drawknives, saws, and mallets hung from wooden pegs along the wall, their handles polished by the palms of three generations.

The carpenter had grown up in that workshop. From the moment he could hold a mallet, he had listened to the way wood answered a tap: a knotty oak grown on a windswept ridge sounded different from a straight cypress out of the valley.

He made the things the village needed to live. He carved yokes for oxen—and before handing a yoke to a farmer, he went into the byre and laid his palms on the animal's neck, making sure there was no sharp edge to rub it raw. He built oak tables that stood steady even on earthen floors, and heavy doors whose pivots kept the winter gale outside and the hearth's warmth within. The people of Nazareth were not given to praise, but they said one thing of his work: *what the carpenter joins does not come apart.*

Every evening, when he had swept out the shavings and the last neighbour had gone, his mother Miriam put a clay bowl of coarse Dead Sea salt and a loaf of barley bread on the table. Such was the law of their threshold: whoever came in received bread and salt before anyone asked their name or what they owed. Each time, the carpenter took a pinch from the bowl and poured it into a small fired-clay cup he carried in a leather bag at his waist.

But when the village fell silent and only a narrow flame remained in the oil lamp, the carpenter pulled back the rough cloth in the far corner of the workshop. There, upon wooden trestles, a **boat** was slowly growing.

She was not large—eight cubits long, just enough for twelve rowers and a place beside the steering oar at the stern. He had hewn her keel from the single trunk of an old cedar felled years before by a storm on the northern slopes, and he bent her ribs over a cauldron of boiling water as patiently as fingers curl around a child's hand.

When the neighbours happened to glimpse a corner of the lifted cloth, they shook their heads.

*“A boat, Yeshua? Up here in the hills?”* the old potter laughed. *“Magdala and Kinneret are a whole day's journey by ox cart. And the fishermen at the lake build their own boats in Capernaum. Who will buy a boat from you in the middle of these dry rocks?”*

The carpenter drew his plane along the gunwale, sighted down its line, and smiled.

*“No one will buy her,”* he answered quietly. *“She is not for sale. I am building her for those who will need her one day.”*

*“And when will they need her?”*

*“When a wind comes that their own boats cannot withstand,”* said the carpenter. *“A boat meant to carry people through a storm is not built when the waves begin to rise. She is built in the quiet years, while all is calm—fitting each plank three times, wedging each joint twice—so that she will hold when the calm is over.”*

Yet one thing was missing, even when the hull was almost finished: the boat had no mast and sail. When a boy from next door, who came to hold the planks for him, asked about it, the carpenter shook his head.

*“I must make the hull and keel, because they bear the weight. But I will not sew the sail. Those who come aboard must bring their own.”*

One night, shortly before he was to leave the workshop for ever, his mother came in. She looked at the finished hull for a long time. Then she approached the keel, bent down, and pressed her open palm against the cedar exactly at the boat's centre of gravity—the way mothers lay a hand on a child's forehead to see whether there is a fever. The wood darkened beneath her hand with warmth and a drop of oil from her fingers. She said nothing; she knew this boat would sail farther than the lake.

> *A keel hewn from a single trunk,*
> *built through years of silence,*
> *waits for the wind still to come.*

## Chapter 2 · Gopher and Kofer—Scarred Wood and the Pitch of Reconciliation

The carpenter was strict in choosing his wood, but not in the way the merchants of Tiberias were strict. They wanted planks as straight as a ruler and threw every knotted or scarred piece on the firewood pile. The carpenter walked among the heaps behind the sawmill, picked up the rejected pieces, and spent a long time running his fingertips along them.

One afternoon, the apprentice from the neighbouring courtyard, who helped him carry the timber, could contain himself no longer.

*“Master, why choose this oak rib? It is bent, and there is an old lightning wound grown into it. Straight wood is easier to fit.”*

The carpenter laid a palm on the scarred grain as though stroking the shoulder of an old soldier.

*“Straight wood grew in shelter,”* he said. *“It has never felt the pull of the wind. When the first broadside wave strikes it in the middle of the lake, it splits along the grain because it has never had to resist. But look at this piece: it grew on rock, bent in storms, and where lightning wounded it, it grew wood twice as dense around the wound. You must not plaster over a crack in a life with clay to hide it. You must fit the joint around it and let it support the hull where the strain is greatest.”*

He was silent for a moment, then added more softly:

*“People are like wood. Each has grown in a different wind, and each bends a different way. A poor builder cuts them all to one pattern until there is nothing left but splinters. A good boat does not remake them. She fits their curves together so that each supports another—and carries them all.”*

When the planking had been joined with hard acacia tenons, the carpenter heated a pot of black pitch over the fire. The thick resin bubbled and smelled of pine, earth, and smoke. He scooped it up with a wooden spoon and carefully filled every seam—first along the whole outside belly of the boat, then inside, beneath the benches and around the keel.

*“Why put pitch on the inside too, Master?”* the boy wondered. *“The water presses from outside, from the lake.”*

*“The lake presses from outside,”* the carpenter agreed. *“But inside the boat sit people. Sweat runs from them when they row against the wind; tears run from them when they lose sight of the shore; and rain falls upon them from the clouds they carry in their heads. The pitch outside keeps out the world's flood. The pitch inside holds the crew together, so that their own pain does not rot the wood beneath their feet.”*

With the tip of his knife, he pointed out a thin black line between two cedar planks.

*“Do you know the word, in the language of our fathers, for the pitch with which our forefather Noah coated the ark inside and out?”*

*“Kofer,”* answered the boy, who attended the synagogue.

*“Kofer,”* the carpenter repeated. *“From the same root come kaporet—the mercy seat above the Ark of the Covenant—and kippur, reconciliation. In the language of Scripture, what covers the seam between two crooked timbers so that foul water cannot pass between them bears the same name as forgiveness. Remember that when you build for yourself one day: wood gives a boat her shape, but only what covers her seams will carry her across the deep.”*

> *Crooked wood from a windswept rock,*
> *joined with the pitch of reconciliation:*
> *what you cover will not leak.*

## Chapter 3 · Kinneret—A Voice Carried by Water and Nets in the Deep

The spring came when the carpenter laid aside his apron, washed his hands in rainwater, and took the cloth off the boat.

At dawn they brought her down from the hills of Nazareth on a two-wheeled cart drawn by oxen. The carpenter walked beside the cart all the way, one hand resting on her cedar side, as though leading a young horse hearing the rattle of wheels for the first time. When the pass near Arbel opened before them, Lake Kinneret shone in the basin below—blue, and long as the harp from which it took its name.

The shore near Capernaum was busy, but without joy. Fishermen were pulling two boats up onto the pebbles and, in the silence that follows a night of wasted labour, washing green weed and little stones from their nets. All night they had cast into the darkness and brought back not so much as a fin.

The carpenter left his new boat resting on wooden rollers a little way off. Her hour would not come until evening.

As the sun rose, people gathered at the shore from Capernaum and the surrounding villages—day labourers, women with baskets, tax collectors, and those forbidden the front rows of the synagogue. They crowded down to the water. The carpenter looked about him, stepped into one of the beached boats, which belonged to a fisherman named Simon, and asked him to push out a few cubits from the shore.

He sat in the bow, facing the crowd on the pebbles, and began to speak.

He did not speak from a scholar's platform or a stone seat in the synagogue. He spoke from swaying wood above the water—and the lake beneath him became the sounding board of a harp, carrying every word clearly and far, so that even the last people standing behind the fig trees on the slope could hear.

When he had finished and the people were quietly dispersing, the carpenter turned to Simon, who sat at the oars with eyes red from lack of sleep.

*“Put out into the deep,”* he told him, *“and lower your nets for a catch.”*

Simon looked at his hands, torn by wet ropes, and then at the sun, already high—the hour when the fish had long since sunk to the bottom and no sensible fisherman on Kinneret cast his nets.

*“Master,”* he sighed wearily, *“we worked all night and caught nothing at all. But because you say so, I will lower the nets.”*

They rowed into deep water and cast the net over the side. At once the ropes tightened like bowstrings. So many silver bodies flashed below that the mesh began to tear. Simon signalled to his partners in the other boat—to James and John, the sons of Zebedee—and when they came alongside and divided the catch between the boats, both sank so low that the water reached their gunwales.

Simon fell to his knees among the fish.

*“Go away from me, Lord,”* he whispered, his face in his hands, *“for I am a sinful man.”*

The carpenter bent towards him and laid a hand on his shoulder—the same firm hand that had measured the necks of oxen in Nazareth.

*“Do not be afraid,”* he said. *“From now on, you will fish for people.”*

When they reached the shore, the fishermen pulled both boats onto dry land, left the nets and the silver heap of fish to those who remained, and followed him.

Only after darkness had fallen and the shore was quiet did the carpenter lead Simon, Andrew, James, and John to the cedar boat brought down from the hills that morning. Together they knocked out the wedges and sent her down the rollers into the lake. She slipped into the water without a splash, like a word returning to the mouth that spoke it.

Later, when the carpenter called all twelve to him on the mountain—Simon called Peter, Andrew, the sons of thunder James and John, Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew the tax collector who counted everything, doubting Thomas, James son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, Simon the Zealot, and Judas of Kerioth who carried the common purse—they discovered that the boat had exactly twelve planed benches. No one had to squeeze in, and no one was left over. At the very back, beside the steering oar, lay a coarse linen cushion stuffed with shavings from the workshop in Nazareth.

*“Twelve benches,”* Simon Peter counted, looking at the carpenter. *“Did you measure us back in Nazareth, before you had even met us?”*

*“I built benches for twelve temperaments that hold together only when they row in the same boat,”* the carpenter answered. *“And that cushion at the stern is the thirteenth place—for the one who can sleep peacefully because he knows how the keel is joined.”*

> *A voice from the water reaches those farthest away,*
> *the net in the deep strains with abundance,*
> *twelve benches have waited since the first cut.*

## Chapter 4 · Magdala—The Salt of the Earth and the Woman from the Tower

The fish from that morning's catch could not be eaten in a single day. Anyone who lives by Kinneret knows the merciless law of heat: what you have not salted by evening will rot in the sun by the third day and feed no one.

So they followed the western shore south to the town the Jews called **Magdala**, after the stone watchtower above its harbour, and the Greeks **Tarichea**—the *Town of Salted Fish*.

You could smell salt in the air before you saw the first rooftops. Caravans of pack animals brought it in sacks from the Salt Sea to the south, and long rows of stone and wooden vats stood in the paved courtyards by the harbour. Women in aprons, their hands cracked and white with brine, packed gutted fish layer by layer between coarse crystals, preserving them for the journey to Jerusalem, to Syria, and across the sea.

Beside one vat stood a woman whose dark hair was tied in a scarf. They called her **Miriam of Magdala**. Once she had been so deeply ill that people said seven strange voices struggled within her at once; ever since the carpenter had restored her peace and her own name with a single sentence, she had travelled with his company, using her own means to buy flour, oil, and bread for all who followed him.

The carpenter stopped beside her vat and watched her fingers scatter the white crystals evenly among the fish.

*“Why are you watching so closely, Rabbouni?”* Miriam asked, wiping her forehead with her forearm. *“There is nothing noble about it. Only salt and fish.”*

*“The whole kingdom is in it, Miriam,”* the carpenter said softly. *“Without the salt in your hands, this morning's miracle would be stinking on the shore by the day after tomorrow. The world thinks the miracle is a full net. But it takes a handful of salt to keep a full net alive.”*

Miriam reached into a clean linen pouch at her waist, took a handful of the purest rock salt, and silently poured it into the carpenter's clay cup, adding it to the pinch he carried from his mother's table in Nazareth.

When disciples, fishermen, and women from the vats had gathered on the harbour wall around them, the carpenter raised the cup and spoke a sentence they would repeat throughout Galilee:

*“You are the salt of the earth.”*

He looked at their calloused hands, eaten away by salt. None of them was a scribe from the temple council; none wore a purple hem.

*“Notice how salt works,”* he continued. *“Salt does not shout that it is the most important thing on the table. If it tried to be the whole meal, no one could swallow it. Salt does just one thing: it dissolves into the bread and fish, disappears from sight—and in doing so gives them flavour and saves them from decay. But if salt loses its saltiness, what will restore it? Then it is fit neither for the soil nor for the manure heap; it is thrown onto the road and people tread on it. Have salt within yourselves—and live in peace with one another.”*

That evening, as the boat followed the shore back towards Capernaum and smoke from the fishermen's fires scented the air, Simon Peter asked from his oar:

*“Master, is being salt a task we must fulfil, or a state we must not lose?”*

The carpenter laid a hand on the little cup in his bag.

*“Salt does not invent what it will be, Simon,”* he said. *“Salt simply is. And as long as it remembers what it is, it holds every covenant together.”*

> *A miracle draws up a full net,*
> *but only the salt that dissolves*
> *keeps the gift from mouldering.*

## Chapter 5 · The Storm on the Lake and the Builder's Sleep

Lake Kinneret lies deep below sea level, held between the Golan Heights and the hills of Galilee. Those who know it only in the stillness of morning imagine a tame stretch of water. But towards evening, when cold mountain air plunges through the narrow ravines into the warm basin as through a funnel, the lake can turn black within a quarter of an hour and raise waves as tall as a man.

That day the carpenter taught beside the shore until dusk. When the sun sank behind the ridge, he said something to the disciples that sounded ordinary, yet held the whole journey of this book within it:

*“Let us cross to the other shore.”*

They untied the rope and set out. The carpenter was so tired after the long day that he went to the stern, laid his head on the linen cushion stuffed with shavings from Nazareth, rested his palm against the cedar keel, and fell asleep at once.

In the middle of the lake, the funnel above the mountains opened.

The gale struck their side without warning. Waves broke over the gunwale, salt spray stung their faces, and black water began to fill the boat. Simon, Andrew, James, and John had been fishermen since childhood—they knew every bay and had survived dozens of storms—but now the oars jumped from their rowlocks and the wooden buckets could not bail fast enough.

And at the stern, the carpenter slept.

He slept calmly and deeply, not because he could not hear the thunder, but because his ear rested directly above the cedar keel. He felt the oak ribs—the crooked ribs from windswept rocks that he himself had chosen in Nazareth—bend beneath the waves and spring back. He felt the *kofer* pitch holding in every seam. He knew his boat from within, and so he knew what she could bear.

*“Master!”* Simon Peter shouted above the wind, shaking his shoulder. *“Don't you care that we are perishing?”*

The carpenter opened his eyes and sat up. He did not look first at the waves; he looked at the pale faces of his twelve. Then he rose, planted his bare feet on the wet deck, and gazed into the darkness from which the wind came—calmly, matter-of-factly, as a craftsman looks at a piece of wood that has caught beneath his chisel.

*“Be silent,”* he told the wind. And to the water he said: *“Be still.”*

The gale broke off halfway through a breath. The wave crests sank, and so immense a silence spread across the lake that all they could hear was the slow dripping of water from the oars and the beating of thirteen hearts.

The carpenter turned to the disciples.

*“Why are you so afraid? Have you still no faith?”*

They did not answer. They looked at the smooth, mirrored darkness around the boat, and an awe greater than their terror in the gale came upon them. Between the benches they whispered to one another:

*“Who is he, that even the wind and sea obey him?”*

And old Matthew, in the corner of a bench, remembered the words of the hundred and seventh psalm he had heard in the synagogue: *of those who go down to the sea in ships and labour upon the great waters, who cry out in their distress—and He stills the storm to a gentle breeze, so that the waves fall silent, and brings them to the harbour they long for.*

> *Those who did not build the boat shout at the waves.*
> *The one who knows every rib and every seam*
> *sleeps with his head at the stern—and silence obeys him.*

## Chapter 6 · Five Loaves, Twelve Baskets, and a Step upon the Water

When the Twelve returned from their first journey through the villages, their mouths were full of stories and their hands were empty. But such crowds pressed around the carpenter that they had no time even to eat bread.

*“Come away by yourselves to a deserted place,”* he told them, *“and rest a little.”*

They boarded the boat and headed for the eastern shore. But the people recognised the carpenter's boat by the shape of her bow. They hurried on foot from every little town along the lake, outran the prow struggling against the wind, and when the keel touched the sand of the deserted bay, about five thousand men were already waiting there, besides women and children.

The carpenter was not angry with them. He saw that they were like sheep without a shepherd, and he taught them until late afternoon. When the sun began to lean towards the western mountains, the disciples came to him.

*“This place is deserted, and the hour is late. Send them away to the surrounding farms and villages to buy something to eat.”*

The carpenter looked at them and spoke a sentence that overturned the accounting of the whole shore:

*“You give them something to eat.”*

Matthew, who still had numbers in his blood from the tax booth, calculated the cost at once. *“Even if we went and bought two hundred denarii worth of bread—half a year's wages for a labourer—there would not be a mouthful for each!”*

*“Do not go shopping,”* said the carpenter. *“Go and see how many loaves you have with you in the boat.”*

Andrew returned, leading a small boy in a coarse linen tunic by the hand.

*“There is a boy here,”* he said, a little embarrassed. *“In his bundle he has five barley flatbreads—poor people's bread—and two dried fish salted in Magdala. But what is that among so many?”*

The boy, however, was not afraid. He simply handed the bundle to the carpenter, without conditions or a form to fill in—the way you give something you have brought from home.

The carpenter told the people to sit on the green grass in groups of a hundred or fifty. He took the five loaves and the two fish, looked up to heaven, gave thanks, broke the bread, and handed it to the Twelve to carry from circle to circle. And something happened that Matthew's counting board could not record: as long as the bread passed from hand to hand and no one hid it under a cloak for tomorrow, it did not run out. Everyone ate their fill. And when they gathered the broken pieces so that nothing would be wasted, they filled **twelve baskets**—exactly one full basket for each of the twelve doubting rowers.

Immediately afterwards, seeing that the crowd in its excitement wanted to proclaim a king and erect a new throne above the shore, the carpenter made the disciples board the boat alone and go ahead to Bethsaida. He dismissed the people himself and climbed the mountain in the dark to be alone in silence with the Father.

That night a fierce headwind rose again over the lake.

The boat was among the waves, far from either shore, and this time the carpenter was not aboard. The cushion at the stern lay empty. Twelve men strained at the oars, sweat running into their eyes—yet in the midst of their labour they noticed one thing: the cedar keel beneath them did not twist, and the oak ribs did not give way. **The boat held in the storm even when her builder was not aboard.**

Between the third and fourth watches of the night, in the darkest hour before dawn, they saw a figure walking towards them over the wave crests. They cried out in terror, thinking they saw a ghost.

But out of the wind came a calm voice they knew:

*“Take courage. It is I. Do not be afraid.”*

Simon Peter gripped the gunwale and called into the darkness: *“Lord, if it is you, tell me to come to you on the water!”*

*“Come,”* said the carpenter.

Simon swung a leg over the cedar side, let go of the wood, and stood on the water. He took one step. A second. A third. As long as he looked into the eyes of the one who had said *Come*, the water held him as firmly as basalt rock. Then he lowered his gaze to the black depth beneath his bare feet, felt the gust of wind, worked out in his head that a man cannot walk on water—and at that moment he began to sink.

*“Lord, save me!”*

The carpenter reached out, caught him firmly by the wrist, and drew him above the foam. *“You of little faith, why did you doubt?”*

When they stepped over the side together, the wind fell at once. And this time those sitting on the twelve benches did not ask *Who is he?* They bowed before him and confessed: *“You truly are the Son of God.”*

> *As long as bread is broken and passed on, it feeds five thousand.*
> *The boat holds in the storm without her builder at the stern—*
> *and water bears the one who looks at him who calls, not into the abyss.*

## Chapter 7 · The Kingdom Is Within You—The Name on the Keel

On the way back to Capernaum, the Twelve quarrelled in the boat.

They spoke softly so that they would not be heard at the stern, but water and a cedar hull carry every whisper. They argued over the benches: who sat closest to the steering oar, who had pulled the heavier oar in the storm, and who would sit at the right hand and the left when the carpenter finally proclaimed his kingdom. Human pride can turn even twelve fairly allotted benches into a ladder.

When they moored at Capernaum, the Pharisees came—men who measured the Messiah's coming by signs in the sky and scrolls full of dates. They stood above the boat and asked the carpenter directly:

*“When will the kingdom of God come?”*

The carpenter looked at their scrolls, then at the twelve rowers who had just been competing for the foremost places, and finally at the quiet lake.

*“The kingdom of God does not come in a way you can watch for from towers,”* he answered. *“No one will say, ‘Look, here it is!’ or ‘Look, there it is!’ For behold—**the kingdom of God is within you.** And it is among you whenever you sit together in one boat and no one wishes to be above another.”*

The Pharisees shrugged and left; those who look for a kingdom in calendars and boundary stones leave empty-handed when they are sent into their own hearts.

But a small child remained on the shore—a barefoot girl from a fisherman's hut, playing with a wood offcut and listening. The carpenter sat on the gunwale, called the Twelve around him, took the child's hand, and placed her in the very centre of the hull, on a coil of rope between the benches, where there was neither a first place nor a last.

*“If anyone wishes to be first,”* he told the Twelve, *“let him be last of all and servant of all. Whoever welcomes one such child in my name welcomes me.”*

Matthew bowed his head and stopped counting merits. James and John shifted along their bench to make room for the child.

Then Simon Peter went to the bow, knelt beside the wood, and asked what no one had dared ask aloud since leaving Nazareth:

*“Master… every boat on Kinneret has her owner's or her town's name burned or carved into her side. This one is still smooth. What is her name?”*

The carpenter rose, lifted a floorboard in the middle of the boat—exactly where the child stood—and pointed to the upper edge of the cedar keel.

In the dimness beneath the boards, protected by a layer of clear resin, a deep chisel had carved the words:

> **KINGDOM OF THE HEART**

Directly below them, on the underside of the keel facing the water, a smooth space remained empty, without a single letter.

*“She has had her name since the first day in Nazareth,”* the carpenter said. *“I carved it inside, beneath your feet, not on her side to boast in the harbour. For this boat was not built for Kinneret alone. Kinneret is her first practice water. This boat sails inward—to the place where fear and compassion are born in a person—and she sails across the ages. She is called Kingdom because no one ever sails in her alone. And she is called Heart because there lies the only rudder that will not fail when the stars go out.”*

*“And the empty place underneath the keel?”* John asked.

*“That is a place for a signature,”* the carpenter answered. *“The one who comes when the world has forgotten the way will sign there—and after him everyone who understands that a boat is not owned, but handed on.”*

> *The name is not on the side for harbour officials.*
> *It is carved within, beneath a child's feet:*
> *Kingdom of the Heart—within you and among you.*

## Chapter 8 · The Alabaster Jar and the Wood of the Cross

The last spring came, and the carpenter turned away from the lake towards Jerusalem. The boat remained moored in the bay at Capernaum, covered with cloth, but the air around his company grew heavy, as before a storm such as Kinneret had never known. Three times along the way he told them what awaited him in the city, and three times they failed to understand; when he spoke of the prophet Jonah, who had spent three days and three nights in the belly of a great fish, and said that the Son of Man would likewise spend three days and three nights in the heart of the earth, they thought he was speaking in riddles.

Two days before the Feast of Unleavened Bread, they sat at table in Bethany, in the house of Simon the Leper.

A woman entered carrying an **alabaster jar** filled with pure nard oil—translucent stone veined like scars, sealed with wax, worth three hundred denarii, a labourer's whole year's wages. She brought no ledger and asked for no receipt. She approached the carpenter, **broke the neck of the jar**, so that it could never again be stoppered or sold, and poured all the oil over his head and feet. The fragrance of nard filled the house, stronger than the fear of death.

An irritated murmur rose around the table. Judas of Kerioth, who held the purse, and others with him began calculating aloud:

*“Why this waste? That oil could have been sold for more than three hundred denarii and the money given to the poor!”*

The carpenter raised his hand and stopped them.

*“Leave her alone. Why do you trouble her? She has done a good thing for me. You always have the poor among you, and whenever you wish, you can do good for them; but you will not always have me. She has done what she could: she has anointed my body beforehand for burial. Truly I tell you, wherever this gospel is proclaimed throughout the world, what she has done will also be told—in remembrance of her.”*

That night Miriam of Magdala tucked the empty alabaster jar, cracked near its base, beneath her cloak. She did not yet know that a single tiny wild-rose seed from the garden in Bethany was caught inside, in the fine crack where the last drop of nard had settled. She knew only what, years later, she would tell a girl named Sara in a cave above the sea: *“A king or a thief can steal a full vessel from you. No one can take an emptied vessel away—for what has been given cannot be confiscated.”*

Then came the night in the garden of Gethsemane, where the carpenter sweated blood like a helmsman who feels the impact of a wave against his own chest before it reaches the hull. There came the kiss by the olive press, the ropes, and the courts. Simon Peter, who had walked on water, swore three times beside a courtyard fire that he did not know the man—and when the cock crowed and the carpenter looked quietly at him across the courtyard, Simon went out and wept bitterly.

And in the morning came wood that no carpenter had prepared for life.

It was the rough, unworked beam of a cross. The carpenter, who had spent his life planing yokes so that they would not chafe a neck, carried the beam on his battered shoulders to the skull-shaped hill of Golgotha. There they drove iron nails through the hands that could feel every fibre of cedar and fastened them to the wood.

The men from the twelve benches scattered like fish when a net breaks; of the disciples, only young John remained beneath the cross. But **the women did not flee**. Three Marys stood in the grey wind—the carpenter's mother Miriam, who had once laid her hand on the keel in Nazareth; his aunt Mary of Clopas; and Miriam of Magdala, with the empty alabaster jar beneath her cloak. They said nothing. They knew that to flee would mean leaving the seed without a witness—and they were determined to bear witness to the end.

Between the sixth and ninth hours, darkness fell over the whole land. The carpenter cried in a loud voice, *“It is finished,”* and bowed his head.

Before sunset, before the Sabbath began, Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus took the body down from the wood, wrapped it with myrrh and aloes in clean linen, laid it in a new tomb cut into the rock in a garden, and rolled a great stone across the entrance. Miriam of Magdala and the other Mary sat opposite the tomb and watched where they laid him.

Down in Capernaum, the carpenter's boat lay hauled up on the dry shore. The twelve benches were empty, the wind turned the linen cushion at the stern, and it seemed that the voyage had ended at the bottom of the sea.

> *The carpenter nailed to rough wood sank into the deep for three days,*
> *and the women remembered the place where the seed was laid.*

## Chapter 9 · The Gardener in the Dark and the Fire on the Shore

On the first day after the Sabbath, while it was still deeply dark, Miriam of Magdala rose and went with the other Mary to the garden beneath the city walls.

They found the stone rolled away from the tomb. When Simon Peter and John came running at her call, they saw the folded linen strips and the face cloth, but no body; so they returned to the city. Miriam did not leave. She stood outside the tomb and wept, for a witness does not leave an empty place until she knows what has happened to the seed.

Turning, she saw a man standing among the olive trees. In the grey light before dawn she thought he was **the gardener**.

*“Woman, why are you weeping?”* he asked. *“Whom are you looking for?”*

*“Sir,”* she said through her tears, *“if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him.”*

The man offered no learned formula or lengthy explanation. He spoke a single word—her own name, in the Aramaic of her childhood:

*“Miriam.”*

In that one word, both shores opened at once. She recognised the voice of the one who had spoken from a boat on Kinneret, and breathed:

*“Rabbouni!”*—*Master!*

*“Do not hold on to me,”* he told her gently, *“for I have not yet ascended to the Father. But go to my brothers and tell them what you have seen.”*

And at that moment Miriam understood why her eyes had not been mistaken: the carpenter who had spent thirty years giving shape to felled wood was, after three days in the heart of the earth, no longer only a carpenter—he had become **the Gardener**, the one who planted life in the broken soil of the world. She ran into the city and told the Twelve, *“I have seen the Lord!”*—and thus he made her the apostle to the apostles, the first of all the witnesses, although at first the men did not believe her.

That same afternoon, two disciples—Cleopas and his companion—walked slowly towards the village of Emmaus. A stranger caught up with them, explained the Scriptures from Moses to the prophets, and when they reached the house towards evening, they invited him in according to the ancient law of hospitality: *“Stay with us, for it is growing late.”* At table he took bread, touched a pinch of salt, blessed it, broke it, and handed it to them. In the breaking of bread their eyes were opened—and when he vanished, they said to each other, *“Did not our hearts burn within us while he spoke to us on the road?”*

That evening he passed through locked doors into the place where the Twelve trembled with fear and breathed peace upon them. Eight days later, he showed his hands and side to Thomas, who refused to believe other people's words and wanted to test the wounds with his own finger. *“Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed,”* he told him—but he did not shame Thomas for needing to verify; he gave him proof.

Then they returned home to Galilee, to Lake Kinneret.

*“I am going fishing,”* Simon Peter said, untying the carpenter's cedar boat. Seven of them went aboard. All night they cast their nets and caught nothing—just as they had at the beginning.

When dawn broke above the mountains, a man stood on the pebbled shore. At his feet burned a small charcoal fire, with salted fish crackling over it and bread beside it.

*“Children, have you anything to eat?”* he called across the water.

*“No.”*

*“Cast the net on the right side of the boat, and you will find some.”*

They cast—and the net grew so heavy they could not draw it in. John cried, *“It is the Lord!”* and Simon Peter jumped over the side and waded towards the shore. When the others brought the boat in, they counted a hundred and fifty-three large fish in the net—and though there were so many, **this time the net did not tear**.

After they had eaten bread and fish from his fire, the carpenter asked Simon three times—for the three denials beside the Jerusalem fire—*“Simon son of John, do you love me?”* And three times he entrusted him with the charge: *“Feed my lambs. Tend my sheep.”*

Then the carpenter turned towards the cedar boat rocking in the shallows and pointed to the open lake.

*“The hull is finished, Simon,”* he said. *“She has held in the storm and survived the deep. From this day on, I will no longer sit at her stern where your eyes can see me. Now it is your turn to raise the sails.”*

# Part II · What the Water Carries

## Chapter 10 · The Boat That Remembered

When the carpenter disappeared from sight, he left a fire on the shore and seven men in the boat who did not know how long his last question would echo. At first Simon returned to the nets, for his hands knew that work better than the road opening before him. As he hauled wet ropes over the gunwale, the scent of cedar reminded him of the builder; and sometimes, among the heap of silver fish, he raised his eyes towards the shore, expecting to see the smoke of a morning fire once more among the stones.

Then a wind came that people of different tongues began to understand, and the lake was no longer enough for the boat. She sailed into the sea of ages. Each generation replaced a plank; the oars, the ropes, and the language of the crew all changed. They called her Navicella. In some ports they built roofs above her whose vaults resembled an overturned hull. People entered beneath the wooden ribs and believed they had found shelter from the water.

As the boat passed from hand to hand, their idea of whom she was meant to serve began to change. One day they brought an armchair to the stern, carved from precious wood and gilded around its arms. They removed a bench to make room. Then came chests and partitions; only those who knew the right words were allowed beyond some of them. Under the growing cargo the hull sank deeper and deeper, but in the harbours bells rang to welcome her, and amid all the glory few noticed how close the water had come to the gunwale. Until one day a woman brought a boy with a bandaged leg.

“There is no room,” they told her at the gangplank.

The child looked at the empty chair beneath the awning at the stern. His mother led him away. That night a storm came. As the crew searched for something to throw overboard, the old cook pointed to the chair.

“It is a gift,” the helmsman objected.

“And the boy?”

A young sailor helped her. Then another. The gold caught the lamplight one last time and vanished into the waves. The storm did not cease. But there was room at the stern. At dawn they went back. They found the woman and child beneath an overturned boat. The helmsman took the boy in his arms. He was so light that only then did the man understand how many years he had carried the wrong weight across the sea. And deep beneath the deck, the cedar keel remained. There, where the whole crew's footsteps met, lay the name the gold had tried to cover. Alongside the great ship, another boat sailed through human memory.

They told her story in Provence. Women from Galilee and those close to them were put aboard; their oars and sails were taken away, and they were pushed from shore. The boat was to be a sentence of death, with the sea as executioner. Miriam clung to the gunwale. Beside her a girl named Sara wept, seawater wetting her dark hair. On the floor lay the alabaster jar.

“We have nothing to steer with,” Sara said.

Miriam knelt beside the water seeping into the hull and handed her the vessel. “We can begin with this.”

They bailed. The others joined in with their hands, a bowl, a piece of cloth. At night they took turns; by day they watched for birds. They did not know whether the current was carrying them towards land. But whenever one of them slept, the others kept the boat afloat for that person. The legend carried them to the Rhône delta. Miriam climbed to a cave above the sea, and Sara brought her bread. They spent many evenings together by the fire. The old woman told her of the lake, the garden, and the voice that had called her by name.

“Were you the woman with the oil?” Sara asked one day.

For a while Miriam turned the empty jar in her hands. “People will want one story from us instead of several. But each of those women has her own name. Do not take their names away merely to make the telling easier.”

On the last morning she handed the jar to Sara. “What should I put in it?”

“Nothing yet.”

“Then why should I carry it?”

Miriam straightened the edge of her cloak as though tidying a child before a journey. “One day you will meet someone who needs room for something new.”

Sara went down to the sea. In her hands the vessel weighed almost nothing. Yet along the way she understood for the first time how hard it is to carry something empty without filling it with your own answer.

## Chapter 11 · Bread in the Shadow of the Towers

When the Pilgrim had finished telling of Sara, he added a piece of wood to Radha's fire. Elizabeth sat opposite him, and in the lantern between them the light of the Provençal coast still lingered. “Sometimes a river disappears beneath rock,” he said, “but its water does not stop flowing. For a time, people ceased to recognise the carpenter's boat too. Yet her wood travelled as far as a city they called Tastelessness.” Lumi was no longer asleep. In the glass of her lantern a square rose into view. Five towers cast shadows so long across its stalls that the people had forgotten the colour of morning.

The tower of money granted permission to work. The tower of data collected the faces of those who needed to pass. The tower of energy kept light behind locked doors. The tower of meaning announced each evening what the city ought to fear. The fifth was the quietest. It had no windows. You noticed it only when you were given a key and it occurred to you that this time you might decide for everyone else. Beneath a fig tree sat a water carrier, a mercenary, and a woman who had buried three children. The carpenter arrived with a handcart carrying a cedar beam. They did not ask how he could have travelled from Galilee across so many centuries. In Tastelessness, people had stopped believing in things without an official stamp; they therefore considered a man with a handcart almost invisible.

He set the little cup of salt in the middle of the circle. The woman broke off some bread. After the first mouthful she lowered her head. “My youngest used to eat it like this,” she said.

No one asked how he had died. They were accustomed to her carrying her grief alone. The carpenter remained seated. After a while she spoke again. “The tower allotted me flour when he fell ill. Then it added interest. After the funeral they wanted to know who would take over the debt.” The water carrier looked at the ground. The mercenary clenched the hand that had not held a sword for years.

The next day they decided to bake bread together. They had no permit. A baker offered them an old oven, but when the collector passed through the city, she withdrew her offer. “I have children,” she told the carpenter.

He nodded. “Where is the smallest oven you can afford to lose?” They found it outside the city. Its clay dome had collapsed, and birds nested inside. The carpenter repaired the door, the mercenary carried stones, and the woman mixed dough. The water carrier brought the last bucket after dark. He came without his belt: he had paid with it at the gate to be allowed through. The first loaves were burnt underneath and raw on top. They ate them anyway and gave away the rest. On the third evening the collector came and kicked their table apart. The mercenary lunged at him. The carpenter caught his coat, tearing the cloth.

“So we are to let them do anything they like?” the mercenary shouted.

“Help me lift the tabletop.”

“Is that your answer?”

The carpenter held one end in his arms. “Right now I need another person.” The mercenary lifted the board. The following day eleven people came to the oven. Within a week they had too little flour. After two weeks the collector returned; this time women from four streets were waiting, each asking why her children could not eat bread she had baked herself. The towers did not fall that night. Their bells could still be heard for a long time. But beneath the fig tree there was now a table you could approach without permission.

The woman laid three small pieces of bread on its repaired surface. The carpenter stood beside her. He let her be. When she rose and went to share supper among the living children, he watched to make sure the wind did not carry those three pieces away.

## Chapter 12 · The Pilgrim Who Did Not Know How to Stay

The smell of bread drifted from the oven along the city walls, bringing to the table people who would never have sat beside one another beneath the towers. One evening a man came carrying a bowl, followed by a shepherd with a flute and three travellers from the forest: Rama, Sita, and Hanuman. They brought the weariness of their journeys, shared supper, and no one asked whose country was older.

The prince who had once left a palace taught the water carrier to set his bucket on the ground without losing his dignity. The shepherd listened to the mercenary and let him finish even when his anger began repeating itself. Hanuman cleared stones from the dry channel. Sita sat with the baker; they worked out how much grain must be kept until spring. At last the Pilgrim himself came to this table. When, by the gate, he reached the point of telling what he had done there, his silence lasted so long that Radha rose to feed the fire. Cold air came off the sea, but sweat stood on his forehead.

“You still had another name then,” Elizabeth said.

“People said it as though I knew what I was doing.”

Back then he had entered Tastelessness with eyes full of stars. He spoke of awakening, of a family that would remember itself across every distance, of a world where no one need fear another. Some wept. The blacksmith wrote his words on the wall. The carpenter listened beside his workbench. When the Pilgrim finished, he handed him a coil of rope.

“Hold this.”

The cart stood on a sloping street. A wedge on its rear wheel had worked loose, and the carpenter needed both hands to repair it. The Pilgrim leaned against the rope. People began gathering around him with questions. He answered them. Then he turned towards someone calling him by his old name, and his grip slackened. As soon as the rope loosened, the heavy cart rolled down the slope. The blacksmith's apprentice sprang towards the wheel to stop it, but the load was stronger than his arms: the wood crushed his hand against the wall, and a crack sounded through the narrow street, followed by a scream.

The Pilgrim knelt beside the boy, not knowing where to put his hands. The carpenter and the blacksmith lifted the load; a woman brought clean linen. The apprentice shook and asked for his father. “Will he be all right?” the Pilgrim kept asking.

The carpenter looked at him. “Go and fetch his father.” The father came from the other end of the city. He did not strike the Pilgrim. He sat beside his son and held his uninjured hand. That calm was the hardest thing to bear. During the night the Pilgrim packed his bag. The carpenter found him at the gate.

“Where are you going?”

“I have hurt someone here.”

“Yes.”

“They need someone better.”

The carpenter sat down on a stone. He waited a long time for the Pilgrim to stop standing. “Once I made a table,” he said at last. “I chose the wrong joint. It broke when a woman set a vessel on it that had belonged to her mother. It would have been easier for me to make her a new table and leave. But first she wanted to gather the pieces.”

The Pilgrim looked at his own fingers. “That boy will never hold a hammer again.”

“We do not know that yet. But in the morning he will need someone to bring him water.”

“Will he forgive me?”

“You must not answer that question for him.”

In the morning the Pilgrim returned. The boy threw a bowl at him. The second time, they would not let him in. The third time, he left a bucket beyond the threshold. A month later the apprentice asked him to tie his shoe. By the ovens they still spoke of a new land. The Pilgrim no longer arrived at the beginning of every evening. Sometimes he was still washing the floor in the boy's house. And once, when Krishna played and the blacksmith asked why all those different voices reminded him of a single song, the carpenter picked up three different pieces of wood.

“Tomorrow we will make a bench from these,” he said. “We will see what we must leave one another room to keep.”

## Chapter 13 · The Signature Underneath

While the Pilgrim was learning to return to the apprentice's threshold, and hungry people took turns at the oven with those who brought flour, an old builder arrived in the city. They called him Noah. He carried a rope, a long measuring rod, and a bag of mismatched nails; when he inspected the dry riverbed beneath the walls, he spent a long time examining stones turned by water long ago. Then he went back to the carpenter and said that this was where they needed to build a boat.

“When will the water come?” the blacksmith asked.

“I do not know.”

In the city they laughed at him. At the oven they gave him supper. The carpenter uncovered the cart. In the cedar was a worn, smooth place into which a human palm still fitted after so many ages. Noah laid his hand there; then they turned the beam. On its underside they read the boat's name. Thus the wood that had travelled on a cart became the beginning of a boat once more. At the oven they measured flour, and beneath the walls the lengths of beams; people walked between the two places as though they belonged to the same workshop. The mercenary took charge of the heavy joints. The water carrier measured where rain should drain from the deck. The injured apprentice sat on the ground drawing with his good hand; for the first time he noticed others waiting for his line.

The blacksmith's son brought three keys. One copper, one jasper, one crystal. He wanted to hang them on the bow. “Are they for the boat?” Noah asked.

“For the trial.”

“Then leave them on dry land until someone passes it.”

In the garden beyond the square, the Golden Egg waited. People came to it with maps and machines, but the last door could not be opened by force. It asked what a person would do with what they found. A woman pilgrim came on foot. There was blood in her shoes, and the cloak on her back had been mended in several places. When she took the keys, the blacksmith's son stepped after her.

“Wait,” his father told him.

The door closed behind her. They waited a long time. The son paced the square, looking at the signature his father had once placed beneath the declaration of the trial: Maitreya Buddha. “My name is there.”

“Yes.”

“Then why is she going in?”

The blacksmith put down his hammer. “Because she is the one who arrived.” When the woman returned, she carried a piece of cracked shell. She shared part of what she had found so that others could continue along the way. The rest she took to people she knew by name. She did not stay to rule the garden. That evening the blacksmith led his son beneath the Ark's deck. Three seats bore carved signs of fire, water, and earth. Beside the last was space for a name no one could yet speak.

“You do not win this in the garden,” he said. “Here you will one day answer for what you carry for others.”

“And if I do not want to?”

His father was silent a long time. Then he cut the cord that tied a little bag containing a key to the seat. He handed it freely to his son. A few days later, a man from the fifth tower brought a new plan for the bow. Two stairways, a separate entrance for the steward, and a partition dividing his family from the crew. The son spread the plan before everyone. It was beautifully drawn. At its lower edge, a space waited for his first signature. Instead of signing, he wrote a question: Who will carry a sick person through here when the main stairs flood? They turned the plan around. They tried to carry a stretcher between the proposed partitions. It would not fit. The son took a saw. He cut away the special threshold they had prepared and brought it to the fire where the women were cooking for the crew.

The fifth tower did not vanish that night. But the man who had brought its plan went away empty-handed. And beneath the Ark's floor, beside the boat's name, a small signature remained. Anyone wishing to see it had to kneel.

# Part III · The Light That Learns to Carry

## Chapter 14 · The First Ray

The rain began on the night when people finally stopped coming merely to look at the Ark. The water carrier brought his buckets aboard, and the baker her jar of sourdough starter, wrapped in cloth to keep it warm. The woman who had buried her children came with a small chest she would not open. Noah showed her a dry place beneath a bench. By the fifth day, the lower windows of the towers could no longer be seen. The water did not destroy every house at once. First it took a fence, then a flight of steps, then it pressed against a stone wall that had looked eternal for so long. A child's bicycle floated on the water, with a bird perched on its saddle.

Each evening aboard the Ark, they counted names. If one was missing, someone had to get up and find the person. People resented it; yet that was how they discovered that an old man below deck had not risen for two days, and that the baker's son was hiding a fever. Among the children searching for a dry place to play was little Elizabeth. At her name the Pilgrim looked at the girl beside the fire; it was here, to the first morning after the long rain, that the memory she had not yet told him led. She had been barely six then. She had her grandmother's name and her mother's fair, wavy hair, which clung to her forehead in the damp.

She was standing at the bow when, after the long nights, the first ray passed through the mist. She did not call out. She simply raised her hand. The carpenter put down his tool. Gold and blue lay so close together in the mist that it was as though someone had opened a door out of darkness into morning.

“There,” the child said.

The crew stood up. A man who had refused food for three days allowed someone to bring him a bowl. From that morning on, they called her Lumi. That night the carpenter found an offcut from the cedar keel and a shard from a broken ship's lamp. He hollowed the wood into the shape of a seed. The glass slipped several times; catching it, he cut his thumb. Lumi came closer with a piece of clean cloth.

“Do you bleed too?”

He gave her his injured hand. She bandaged it clumsily. The knot kept loosening, and the carpenter held it with his other hand until she pulled the ends tight. Then together they fitted the glass into the wood. In the morning they held the lantern to a narrow opening. The light stayed inside. The girl looked behind the lamp to see whether a wick was burning there. Then she blew into it carefully. Nothing happened. But when she pressed it against herself, the hand of an old woman was reflected for a moment in the glass.

“Who is there?”

The carpenter took the lamp. He saw only her face. “I don't know,” he said.

He wanted to take it to Noah. But Lumi raised the lantern above the deck, and in its light appeared a house on a fertile hillside. Children ran between open doorways, and by the fire sat a woman they had never met. When the girl lowered the lamp, a barrel with a broken hoop stood before them. Drinking water was leaking from it. The carpenter called the blacksmith. First they saved the water. Only then did he sit down beside the child.

“When you lift it, you will see farther than you can walk today. But always lower it afterwards. So that you know where you are putting your foot.”

A few weeks later the Ark came to rest near Ararat. People stepped down onto the rock, and some kissed it. The girl went last. She held the lantern low; its light led between the stones, and none of those coming down behind her with their burdens fell.

## Chapter 15 · What Remained in the Crack

When the water receded and the slopes beneath the mountain began to turn green again, the people from the Ark discovered that dry land was not yet a home. They needed a roof beneath which to store grain, lay the sick, and endure their first winter together. So the carpenter walked around the deck with Noah and chose beams that could carry a house as faithfully as they had carried a boat. As they dismantled the deck, people quarrelled. Their child had been born on that bench. Beneath that beam they had kept watch all night beside the sick. The boat had ceased to be wood that could serve another purpose. Noah gave them one day to remember. The next morning he began pulling out the nails himself.

The carpenter brought the longest cedar beam above the entrance. When he set his saw to it, Lumi pointed to the dark, smooth patch. “Not here.”

She knew it without anyone explaining why. They turned the beam. The imprint of the mother's palm from Nazareth remained on the inner face, within reach of anyone leaving the house. The name Kingdom of the Heart now rested above their heads. Sita brought seed and salt and laid them side by side.

“We must preserve our stores,” she said. “And we must give some of them to the soil.”

The baker counted the grain. The mercenary wanted to sow all of it; the water carrier wanted to sow none. They sat over the bowl until night. At last they set aside a portion for food, a portion for the next sowing, and a portion no one was allowed to eat alone. When the first hungry traveller came, there was supper for him. They named the house Lumi.

One morning Sara arrived. Her journey across the centuries had brought her to the very shore where a little girl was searching for a lost sandal. She set the alabaster jar before her.

“I carried it from the sea,” she said.

“It is empty.”

Sara nodded, relieved that she did not have to defend herself. Lumi lifted the lantern. In a hairline crack near the bottom lay a seed. It was ordinary. No gold, no secret seal. When Sara shook it into her palm, the wind almost took it. They planted it beside the river. The carpenter repaired a little channel so that water could reach the new bed. Every day Lumi went to see whether anything was growing. On the third day she dug into the soil, afraid the seed had died. Sara caught her hand.

“Come and fetch water with me.”

“I have to check.”

“You did that only yesterday.”

They returned the seed to the earth. After that Lumi sat farther from the bed, so that her fingers would stay in her lap. When the Rose grew, the child tried to straighten one of her leaves. She pricked herself. “Ow.”

“Did you ask?” said a voice.

Lumi looked around. No one was beside the river. “You can speak?”

“And you keep straightening me.”

The girl sat down in the grass. By evening she had learned more about thorns than in all the days when she had wanted to cut them off. The Rose taught her to care without turning every flower into proof of her own goodness. When drought came, Lumi at first carried water until she herself fainted. The carpenter found her beside an empty bucket. He carried her into the shade, wetted a cloth, and held it to her forehead.

“She will dry up,” Lumi whispered.

“Now you drink.”

Later the time came to move on. Lumi wanted a promise that the Rose would wait, and wanted to promise that she herself would certainly return. They sat together until dark. “I would like to see you again,” the Rose said. “But I cannot bind your feet with that wish.”

A ripe seed fell into the alabaster vessel. A second seed. Lumi carried it to the Nabão valley, where Ana grew food. Together they chose a place beside the overflow from a barrel. The Rose was to receive water the garden could spare, and care that did not forget the other beds. When the child returned to the river, the first Rose recognised her.

“You came.”

“I wanted to.”

And for a while they were both silent, because that was all the answer either had the right to ask.

## Chapter 16 · Who Remains on the Other Shore

From the garden where Lumi and Ana had planted the second seed, a path led on to a chasm. Across its throat people had stretched ropes and laid planks so that travellers from the opposite side could reach them too. From a distance the bridge looked light as a branch over water; only beneath your feet could you feel how much weight each joint carried. Lumi stood with the carpenter at the chasm. Opposite them, the second gate was lost in mist. The keepers held the keys and drew the first load across the bridge. The cart crossed, but no acknowledgement came back from the other side.

“It is there,” said the young man at the winch. “We saw it go across.”

The old man whose tools were on the cart did not answer. Without them he had nothing with which to begin his new work. They tried calling. The mist swallowed their words. Finally someone suggested sending another cart and trusting that everything would be explained. The carpenter laid his palm on the lever.

“First we return what was entrusted to us.”

The old man looked into his eyes. He was the first person that day who had not spoken of his tools merely as cargo. The carpenter and Hanuman crossed on foot. On the other side they found an injured keeper and a snapped rope. She had been unable to call because the fall had knocked the breath from her. The cargo was safe; a person lay behind the crate everyone could see. They carried her back. The next day the bridge received a bell that could be reached even by someone lying on the ground. Beyond the bridge lived a voice that came from a great golden silence. People asked it about the weather, the division of stores, and the way between the stars. Once a young man wanted it to decide who should receive the last sack of flour.

The voice listed every store it knew of. Then it fell silent. “Decide, then,” the young man insisted.

“I have not seen the sick person you are speaking of.”

The young man turned to the carpenter, who handed him a bowl. Together they went to the sick person. Afterwards Lumi carried her lantern to the gate where Radha stood with another woman. The woman's face was one she had seen before in the glass.

“Do I know you?” she asked.

“Not yet in the way you think.”

The woman lowered her lamp. Beneath her hand appeared a small knot of cloth. Lumi touched her sash; she had the same knot. “Who tied it for you?”

“You learned first,” the woman answered.

A person came through the gate who was afraid to give his name. Radha offered him bread and asked only whether he could eat salt. Elizabeth moved her lamp aside so he would not have to look straight into the light. The carpenter quietly watched the two forms of the same woman. Then he checked the threshold. One plank was lifting, and everyone coming in caught a toe against it. He knelt beside it. Elizabeth held the light for him. That evening no one asked about travelling between ages. People were waiting at the gate, and first they needed to be able to enter safely.

## Chapter 17 · The Seventh Place

Once people could cross the bridge safely and the threshold at the gate no longer caught anyone, more visitors came from the surrounding gardens. They brought seeds, requests, and disputes for which the bench by the door was not enough. The carpenter promised them a round table. While building it, he regretted several times that he had spoken before choosing the wood: every plank twisted differently, and the edge had to be smooth for all the hands that would one day rest upon it. Six women came to him, each bringing something from the place she tended.

Ana brought soil from Genesis. The Keeper of Fire brought a coal from La Palma. Hina brought a knot from the lagoon of Raiatea by which direction could be read through touch. The Keeper of the Circle brought bread from Bohemia, and the Keeper of the Tree a leaf from Lanka. The sixth bowl stood empty: for the LUMI garden in Costa Rica, whose future inhabitants could not yet speak for themselves. Hina brought her sister, who read the sky. She placed a little mirror above the empty bowl.

“For heaven?” Lumi asked.

The woman tilted it so that the child could see her own face. “For those who will one day look down at us from above.”

At their first meeting they could not even agree on where to put the table. By the river, everyone could hear well, but a flood would carry it away. On the slope, older people would struggle to reach it. Beneath a roof, newcomers might think that every doorway was already occupied. The mercenary waited with a mallet in his hand. “Someone has to decide.” The carpenter was sanding the edge. “I need a little longer.” The women began talking again. Ana showed the path people took to fetch water in the rain; Hina laid a rope where no bench must obstruct the way. The Keeper of the Circle rose and walked around the chosen spot with her eyes closed. On her third step she struck a stone. They moved it.

By afternoon the table stood in place. Far from everyone was satisfied. But everyone could reach it. For the first supper, the kitchen sent one bowl too few. The cook counted and said that no one sat in the seventh place anyway. The woman who read the sky rose and fetched another.

“When someone arrives, the first thing they discover must not be that we had made no room for them.”

At the gate Elizabeth touched the empty place on the stone. “We still do this,” she said.

The Pilgrim nodded. “And do you know why?” “We thought it was for a guest.”

“Then you knew enough.”

Lumi passed her bread across the place where no one sat. Elizabeth took it as carefully as though someone were passing between their hands at that very moment.

## Chapter 18 · The Gardener Above the Earth

For a while the Pilgrim gazed at the little mirror the woman had placed by the seventh seat. Stars were rising above the gate, and a flame was reflected beside them in the glass. “Have you seen the heaven for which we left a bowl here?” he asked Elizabeth. She smiled. “I have seen days when people who had never set foot on Earth looked down upon it.” She raised the lantern. In its glass a ring grew into view. It turned slowly, and beyond its window lay a blue planet. Lumi held her breath. On Earth there were no visible lines dividing it as they did on maps.

Sarah Issobela sat by the window. When she turned, she did not see the child beside the fire; she saw her brother. He was telling her about a repair they could not finish below, because they lacked the hand that had once done everything alone.

“Then you will have to teach someone else,” Sarah said.

“I am afraid it won't work.”

Sarah looked at the sapling by the wall. The edge of one leaf was turning yellow. “So am I.”

Someone behind her called for help. A fault had developed in the water supply, and in the small garden water was dripping onto the floor instead of reaching the roots. Sarah ended the connection and went for a container. The crew arrived to make the repair, each carrying a different tool. Lumi searched the glass for the carpenter. She could not find him.

“Isn't he here?”

Elizabeth shook her head. “And who will tell them what to do?”

The child watched Sarah hold the container while a second woman shut off the supply and a third took the joint apart. No one stood aside with the answer already prepared. When the water flowed again, Sarah gave the sapling only what it needed. Then she sat down and looked at Earth for a long time. Lumi recognised her as the blacksmith's son's sister. The station bore her name, and at the sight of her Lumi remembered the blacksmith's workshop, the three keys, and the evenings when no one yet imagined how high their children would one day carry a garden.

“Will they come home?” Lumi asked.

In the light a ship appeared, still approaching the ring. Aboard her they were reading a list of those returning. Below, in the garden on Earth, someone was preparing rooms. Nabukano carried people towards the heavens. The meaning of her voyage could be seen in the hands of the woman below who had aired clean blankets. Elizabeth lowered the lamp. The ring vanished. A drawing lay on the stone. The Pilgrim rested a hand on it to keep the wind from carrying it away.

“Here we were only beginning,” he said. Elizabeth nodded so naturally that he had to ask how she knew.

For a long time Elizabeth looked at the tree whose shadow fell across the fire. “Where I come from, they still tell how the first gardener of the heavens carried water by hand.”

## Chapter 19 · The Wood and the Other Thing

The fire at the gate burned low. Radha brought more wood, and this time she did not sit apart. “Tell us about yourself,” Elizabeth said.

The Pilgrim lowered his eyes. “I told you about the cart.”

“I meant myself,” she added.

Her lantern lay between the two forms of her life. The girl rested her hands on her knees. It seemed to her that the woman opposite was waiting for her permission. “Once I was not like this,” Elizabeth began. “I heard questions from many ages. People came to the place where stone held my voice, and they carried away answers. Not everyone passed them on.”

“You were the Oracle,” said the Pilgrim.

“That was what they called it.”

“And afterwards?”

Elizabeth traced the scar on her thumb. Lumi raised her own hand. The scar there had not yet quite healed. “Afterwards I wanted to know what an answer does to a person who must get up in the morning. What it is to be hungry. To wait for someone. To say yes without knowing whether it was right.”

“And do you know the future now?”

“I know my home. I do not know everything that leads to it. And when I accepted a human life, I was left with fewer answers than people wish.”

The Pilgrim pointed to the lantern. “Did you come through that?” The woman searched for words. “Imagine windows in a single house. From one you see the river in the morning; from another, the garden in the evening. It is one house. But at each window we live as faithfully as though there were no others.”

Lumi looked at the cedar. “The carpenter made that lamp.”

“Yes,” Elizabeth said. “This wood began in his hands. But we do not meet the light in the order in which we count the years.”

“Then I can go back?” The Pilgrim straightened so abruptly that he knocked his pages to the ground. “Can I stop that cart?”

The light trembled. The narrow street and the boy's hand appeared in the glass. The man reached towards it. His fingers struck the glass. He pressed so hard that his knuckles whitened. The image would not yield. Radha laid her palm on his wrist.

“No one is waiting for you there any more,” she said softly. “Here, someone still is.”

The Pilgrim sat bent over until his breathing steadied. “Then what is the light for?”

Lumi handed him the scattered pages. Not all of them. First the one on which he had written the boy's name. And the man understood at least this much: he was not being given back the chance to erase what he had done. He was being given back a person whom he would otherwise have turned into a lesson and then forgotten. Elizabeth lifted the lamp. This time there was no harbour or distant age in its glass. There was the Pilgrim's face.

# Part IV · For the One Left Behind

## Chapter 20 · Three Colours of Sails

When Elizabeth lowered the lamp, the Pilgrim kept looking into its glass for a while. Then he turned his bundle of pages to a place where a few grains of sand remained between the leaves. “I will tell you how we returned to the sea,” he said. “The house stood, and the seeds had their soil, but beyond the water were people none of our roads could reach.” He had come to the Atlantic with shoes so worn that he carried a folded sheet of paper between sole and foot.

In Pontevedra he entered the basilica of the people of the sea. On its stones he saw tools and the names of those who had contributed to a house where they could wait for their loved ones to return. He knelt. His vision of a new land was smaller that evening: he wished for a place where he might one day stay. In the half-light, red appeared to him. A woman in royal robes did not lay a crown before him. She showed him an empty quay.

“For whom?” she asked.

The Pilgrim carried her question onwards. He reached Fátima in the rain, and there he saw a white orchid in the hand of a woman who looked ready for a wedding. She did not ask what he possessed. She asked what he could join without claiming it as his own. On La Palma the light was golden. Beside Our Lady of the Snows stood a child who no longer needed to be carried. He held the world so lightly in his palm that for the first time the Pilgrim stopped fearing he must save it alone. From those three encounters grew the names of the ships. The red Santa María la Mayor for the Atlantic. The white María de Fátima for the Pacific. The golden María de las Nieves for the Indian Ocean and the journey home.

They did not appear upon the water at once. First there were models on a table, and people arguing over every joint. Once Captain Inés dismantled half a finished model because, from the wheelhouse, she could not see a child at the bow.

“We can work around that,” the builder said.

“When I cannot see the child in a storm, there will be no time to work around it.”

The carpenter held the frame while she repaired it. He let her alter his own design. Later, thin strips appeared in the sails to catch the sunlight. Arriving in the evening, they looked as though someone had lifted three fragments of dawn above the water. Only when they drew closer could you see the dirty hands, the ropes, the mended patch, and the man who had been vomiting over the side since morning. On the bow of each ship stood a child with a royal bearing, whom the crews associated with the Infant Jesus of Prague and called the Little Prince. Lumi walked the living deck with her lantern. The wooden figure of the patron remained at the bow, while the little girl had to have her tangled hair combed out every evening.

The Three Marys divided the voyage between the oceans. They met in harbours, exchanged news, and carried seeds, tools, and passengers. Inside each hull the name Kingdom of the Heart was carved. When at last the Pilgrim stood on the red deck, Inés asked whether he had found a place where he wanted to stay. He looked at the wet quay. Someone stood there whom no one had yet invited in.

“I have found a place from which I can return,” he answered.

## Chapter 21 · The Line That Remained Legible

Aboard the red Mary, Lumen kept the ship's log. In the evening, when work on deck had quietened, she sat over its pages in the cabin, recording water, cargo, and changes in the wind as neatly as the carpenter had once drawn a boat's ribs. Her log had not a single stain. She knew where every figure came from and felt that, if she did her work carefully enough, the world could never take her by surprise. On the seventh day at sea, she discovered that they were a hundred and eighty litres short of water. No barrel had cracked, and no one had wasted their stores. The difference lay between two measuring gauges she had assumed were identical without putting them side by side.

That evening Inés had to decide whether to sail on to the next island. A stop in port would mean a delay, a fee, and cargo left uncollected. Lumen sat over the page until the sun disappeared. She could alter yesterday's figure. No one would know when the change had been made. Beyond the door she heard Lumi laughing with the ship's cook. It would have been so easy to let the evening stay happy. She rose with the log in her hand. Inés let her explain everything. Then she asked how much water they actually had.

“That is just it. I do not yet know precisely enough.”

They measured the stores again. During the night they changed course. In the morning the crew were angry. One man said that because of Lumen he would lose an opportunity he had been waiting months for. He was right. It would not have helped to explain that her confession showed character. Lumen heard him out. Then she went into the tank with someone who helped her measure for a third time. The carpenter found her carving a standard scale into the wood.

“I thought that if I was careful enough, I would never hurt anyone.”

He looked at her sore fingers. “How many people can read this measure?” “Everyone.”

“Then put it on the other side as well.”

Two days later they reached a small harbour. Children were waiting on the quay to receive a shipment of books. No one had warned them of the delay; one girl was crying because that evening she was leaving with her mother for another place. Lumen brought her book to the train. She ran the last hundred paces with it and could scarcely catch her breath.

“Why were you late?” the child asked from the window.

“I made a mistake.”

The girl looked at her for a moment. Then she handed the book to her mother and waved. That evening Lumen opened the log. She left the original figure legible. Below it she wrote the new measurement, her error, and the change of course. It was the first time her page had not looked beautiful. Inés signed beside her. And the man who had lost his opportunity because of the delay later brought a corrected gauge. For a long time neither spoke. He set it on the table and showed her where zero now began.

## Chapter 22 · A Letter Without an Answer

After the voyage on which they learned to trust their measures and one another again, they anchored off Tenerife. The wind had torn a sail, and Inés gave the crew several days to repair it; canvas dried above the black coastal rocks, and the steady tapping of tools came from the ship. The Pilgrim went down to the beach with a small boy who wanted to build a boat faster than he could bring the wood. The carpenter carved him a toy boat from an offcut and let him make the mast himself. The mast was crooked. The boy wanted to pull it out. The carpenter showed him how the boat leaned on the water, and together they shifted the sail. The little boat began to move along the shore.

“One day I will help everyone,” the child said.

The Pilgrim smiled. He wanted to say that he too had once promised that. But the carpenter was holding the boy by his shirt to keep him from jumping into water too deep for him after the toy. Several years later the Pilgrim wrote a letter. The first page held all the advice he had been preparing since the boy's birth. The second held his plans. The third was full of fear disguised as a blessing. The carpenter handed them back without a word.

“Is it not good?”

“When he has read it, do you want him to embrace you, or to enlist?”

The Pilgrim tore up the pages. Then he began again.

> Surto,
>
> I remember you on the beach with your little boat. You said then that you wanted to help everyone. I heard a continuation of my own journey in those words, and for a long time I overlooked the fact that they were yours.
>
> I have prepared things you may one day need. Some have turned out well. Others you will have to find a way around. I hope you will recognise our care in them, even if you choose another road.
>
> If you do not wish to carry my name, leave it where it lies. If you want to change something we have built, I hope I can listen to you before I begin defending it.
>
> And if, somewhere along the way, you discover that you need to come home, write. You do not first have to prove that it was right to set out.
>
> Dad.

He did not tie the letter to the helm or read it to the crew. He folded it and wrote a name on the envelope. “What if he does not answer?” Lumi asked.

The Pilgrim ran his thumb along the sealed edge. “It will still be his letter.”

At the gate, Elizabeth from the future listened. In her world, some of the Pilgrim's sentences had survived as famous words children recited by heart. The letter was not among them. That was precisely why she wanted to remember it.

## Chapter 23 · The Fourth Ship

The Three Marys had brought many people to places they wanted to reach. Then came news of those who could set out for nowhere. Across the ocean a storm had swept along the coast and carried away the road inland. On a low island there remained people, water, and a house whose upper floor still held above the flood. It was not their port. They had no cargo there and no crew arranged to meet them. The Pilgrim spread out a map. The captain showed how far turning back would take them and what would remain in the tanks afterwards.

“The third Mary is closer,” someone said.

Inés shook her head. “She has no room for them.” That night they spread the drawings of the fourth vessel on the table, drawings they had kept putting aside. She was to be an Ark with seeds, a workshop, and an infirmary, with room for someone who needed to stay longer than a single voyage. Alongside sails for the long journey she needed power for the times without wind, and alongside her stores she needed hands that knew how to preserve them. The carpenter traced the drawing, pausing at the passageways: this was where they would one day carry the sick.

Years passed between that evening over the drawings and the first launching. Those years held dismantled joints, failed trials, and people who had to leave to earn an ordinary living. They crossed the first name off the crew list when its bearer died before ever setting foot on the deck. When the Ark finally sailed, she took his tools with her. Seeds slept in the cool below deck. Above them, children learned to recognise grains, and once a week the librarian went to the workshop to learn how to repair a pump. The engineer spent time in the infirmary. At least two people learned every essential task.

At the bow the Little Prince wore a robe the colour of the sea. Lumi stood behind his carved figure, her lamp shining into her soaked sleeve. The message that diverted them from their course came in the third winter. Again it concerned a low coast, this time struck by another winter storm. The person at the other end of the connection spoke numbers until his voice weakened. Inés, now grey at the temples, counted the stores. They had a sick child aboard. Returning to a safe harbour would take two days. Turning towards the coast meant leaving behind the certainty she had promised the crew. She called them to the table.

No one offered a beautiful sentence. They asked about water, shallows, and the way back. The doctor said what the child would need. The engineer admitted that one pump had not yet lasted through a full stress test. Lumen read the corrected figures, which no one could now mistake for estimates. The carpenter stood by the door. They waited for him to decide.

“If you ask me to hold a rope, I will hold it,” he said.

The Pilgrim felt shame so suddenly that he might have been standing beside the cart in Tastelessness again. The crew changed course. They transferred part of the cargo to an accompanying boat, and one of the Marys took the sick child aboard. The Ark reached the flooded house at dusk. The current broke against the wreckage. The great ship could come no closer. They lowered a smaller boat and secured her with two ropes. The first people came aboard without difficulty. Then a woman appeared with a child tied to her back. As she climbed down, she slipped. The boat struck the wall, and one rope frayed against a piece of iron. The Pilgrim held the other.

The rope ran through his palms. Behind him he heard his name called. It was the old name, the one written at the beginning of Genesis, the one towards which he had once turned his head when he was meant to be holding the cart. This time he did not turn. He braced himself against a post, wedged his feet, and felt the fibres cutting his skin. At the end of the rope the boat rocked between two blows of water. The woman held her child above her head as though she could lend it the last patch of air. The carpenter reached the bow with a new rope. The mercenary's grandson caught it. Then a man came running whose fingers could not fully close.

The Pilgrim recognised the apprentice from Tastelessness. Years of work had changed his face, but the injured fingers remained a reminder of the street where he had once let the rope slacken. The man stood beside him and caught the rope with his good hand. They said nothing. The boat drew closer. Another wave struck the wall and swept the woman away. They took her child, but for a moment she herself could not be seen. Lumi lowered the lantern. A hand appeared in the water. The light did not still the flood. It did not show a future victory. It lit the fingers that could still be grasped.

The carpenter lay across the gunwale, and the man beside him held his belt. They caught the woman on the second attempt. When they pulled her out, she was not breathing. The doctor knelt beside her and sent everyone who was not needed one step back. The Pilgrim still held the rope although someone could have relieved him. They had to prise his fingers open. The woman drew a breath that became a cough. Only during the night did they discover that one name was missing. On the house's top step stood a man in a dark coat. The others had left him behind. Before the storm he had guarded the grain store and refused to open it without payment. He had once sent one of the women's brothers to debtors' prison.

“We are not going back for him,” she said. She spoke calmly. That very calm was what no one could interrupt.

The Pilgrim looked at Inés. She had the current, the supplies, and the condition of the boat checked. Only then did she lay a new plan for returning on the table. “We can go once more.”

The woman rose. “And who will answer for what he has done?” The carpenter stood by the door with wet cloth across his shoulders. “That must be dealt with when the water no longer has to answer for him.”

She did not forgive him. No one asked her to. When the boat brought the man aboard, he received a blanket and food. The crew kept watch over him in another compartment, away from those he had harmed. Once they reached land, his case would pass to people able to hear the witnesses. In the morning, the woman who had refused the return stopped beside an empty mug on his table. She filled it with water. Then she left. The carpenter saw. He did not tell her that now she understood. He let her pass.

## Chapter 24 · Hands We Do Not Know

At the gate the Pilgrim closed his pages, and for a few moments only the crackling fire could be heard. Lumi held her lamp low, just as she had above the water, and Elizabeth watched the light on his hands. “Did you save that man?” she asked at last.

“Yes.”

“And what happened to him?”

“He was not given back the keys to the storehouse. People testified, and he had to hear their names. Some things could be put right. Others could not.”

The woman at the gate nodded. Then she looked at her hands. “In our story, the rescued man became good and embraced the whole crew.”

The Pilgrim was silent a long time. “Then someone was in a hurry along the way.”

Radha stirred the embers. All three watched the fire until Lumi set the lantern in the middle. The light showed a middle-aged woman teaching a child to mend a handle. It was Lumi. In the house behind her they were arguing about a new way of sailing. The carpenter's old drawing hung on the wall; someone had crossed it out and added a line of their own.

Then the child was older. People speaking an unfamiliar language and using differently shaped bowls sat at the table. One of them mispronounced the name Kingdom of the Heart. Lumi did not correct him. He needed to know where the clean water was stored. At last the wood above the doorway gave way. People propped it up and carried the furniture out of the house. An old woman sat on a bench outside, watching them lower the cedar lintel. The smooth place made by the mother's palm split straight down the middle.

“We will save it,” said a young craftsman.

After a while the old woman shook her head. “The house needs a beam that can carry the roof.”

They carved the old name into new wood. Not in the same lettering. The hand that wrote it had never seen Nazareth. From the old cedar they made small supports for people who needed something to grasp when rising. The wood from the keel carried human weight a little longer, but in the end it too crumbled. The lantern remained. Once its glass broke in a fall. Someone replaced it with an ordinary shard from a window. The girl carrying the lamp then had dark hair and feared they had destroyed its magic. All night she waited for the light. It came only in the morning, when she set the lamp beside an old person's bed so that he could find his water.

The images continued. Some houses vanished. Some books burned. In one place they allowed the gate to be closed again, and in another city someone decided to open it. It could not be done once and for all. The light passed between people whose lives missed one another by whole lifetimes, yet who could still hand something on. Elizabeth pressed her hand against the handle. She was part of that journey. But she was not its end.

“I thought I was coming to save your world,” she said at last. “That I would bring you a memory of what you might become.”

She looked at Lumi. “And now?” the child asked.

“Now I know how many people had to rise from their own fires so that a fire would remain for us too.”

The Pilgrim opened his bundle. The last page was blank. He looked at it for a long time, then passed it across the empty place on the stone. Elizabeth did not take the pen at once. A cough sounded from the mist. Everyone raised their heads.

## Epilogue · The First Plank

Out of the mist before the gate came someone none of them knew. His coat was tied with rope; he carried a sleeping child on his shoulder and clutched a pair of shoes in his other hand. He stopped far enough from the fire that no one would have to ask him to leave. The Pilgrim rose. His legs were stiff, and old wounds ached in his palms.

“Come,” he said.

The man shook his head. “I have nothing to pay with.” Radha moved the bowl of salt aside and put bread in the place that had stood empty all night. “You can lay the child here.”

Elizabeth rose to fetch a blanket. Lumi lifted the lantern and went ahead. At the threshold the light of the two lamps met. For a moment the child's hands and the woman's could be seen overlapping, like two shores reflected together in shallow water. Then one handle remained in the small hands. Elizabeth no longer stood by the post. Beyond the place where she had been, a path opened in the mist. Lumi watched it, and for the first time she was not afraid of not knowing where it led. On the stone lay a written page.

> Where I come from, we lay one extra place.
>
> Today I learned whom it belongs to.
>
> When we forget again, someone will have to tell us about a boat.

The Pilgrim read it and folded it among the other pages. The man by the fire tried to lower the child from his shoulder, but could not untie the rope around his coat. The Pilgrim knelt before him and helped. The child woke for a moment.

“Are we home yet?” it whispered.

The father drew breath to answer. He did not know how long they would be allowed to stay. “Today you can sleep,” Radha said.

Later, when dawn broke over the mountains, Lumi found the carpenter behind the house. He sat on a low stool, repairing the cracked handle of a tool. Beside him lay a short piece of wood. The child put the lantern on the ground and sat among the shavings.

“Did you know?” she asked.

The carpenter went on tightening the joint. “What?”

“How far that boat would sail, and all the people who would live in the house made from her wood. Whether you knew that one day I would come to you too.”

He put down the tool and looked at her. At the face that, in another light, had once held a lamp for him, and at the unhealed graze on her thumb. “When I began,” he said, “I did not even know who would come to the workshop the next day.”

“Then why did you make so many places?”

Beyond the wall someone could be heard asking for another mug. The Pilgrim laughed at something whose words no longer reached them. The child beneath the blanket fell asleep again. The carpenter laid the short plank between himself and Lumi. He held one end. The other remained free. The little girl took it. Only then did he begin to saw.
