# Episode 2 — Dissolution

*Salt in a pile just lies there. Salt in water gives taste to the whole river.*

> *"As a river flows toward the ocean, let your mind flow too — without clinging, without resistance."*
> — paraphrase, inspired by the Buddhist teaching of *anattā* (non-attachment)

---

## Story

A second pilgrim arrived in Tasteless a week later. He carried no bow, no sword, nothing that could serve as a weapon or as a proud proof of the road traveled. He had only a begging bowl, and he walked slowly, as though the journey never mattered to him more than the step he was taking.

The water carrier, the mercenary, and the woman — who still carried a handful of the carpenter's salt in their pockets — met him at the well.

"Who are you?" asked the mercenary, who trusted no one without a weapon — because in a city where taste had vanished, everyone survived by their own means, and unarmed men tended to be either saints or dead within a week.

"I was a prince," the pilgrim answered. "I had a palace, a wife, a son, endless stores of rice, and slaves who lit incense before I slept. And I was more wretched than anyone else in my kingdom, because beyond the walls I had seen old age, sickness, and death, and I could not unsee them."

"And now?" asked the woman.

"Now I have a bowl," he said, and lifted it. "And I know it is enough."

They sat with him on the edge of the well. He was a pilgrim in no hurry to get anywhere, and that alone was strange in Tasteless — everyone else rushed after the taste they had lost, as though it could be caught by walking faster.

"The carpenter gave you salt," said the pilgrim, seeing the white grain on the mercenary's palm. "Salt is a good thing. But I will ask you a different question: what will you do when you have too much of it?"

No one answered right away.

"If you had an entire warehouse of salt," he went on, "it would cease to taste. Salt that does not dissolve is only mineral dust. Salt in a pile preserves nothing — it just lies there, waiting to become property that people will fight over."

"So what are we supposed to do with it?" asked the water carrier.

"Dissolve it," said the pilgrim. "Not store it. Not own it. Dissolve it — into water, into bread, into the soup you give your neighbor without expecting it returned. Salt you clench in your fist will only cut your hand. Salt you release into the river gives taste to the whole village downstream."

The mercenary frowned. His whole life he had believed that survival meant hoarding — food, weapons, scars as proof that he had endured more than the rest. "That sounds like weakness," he said.

"I tried the strength of the palace," the pilgrim replied calmly. "I had an army. I had gold. And still I woke at night with a question no army could answer: *what is it all for, if it ends one day?* The middle way does not mean giving up everything, nor clinging to everything. It means acting — and letting go. Dissolving the salt and letting it do its work, without watching to see whether the river is stealing it from you."

No one in Tasteless grew rich that night, and no one grew poorer. But the woman who had buried three children, for the first time in a long while, took the handful of salt from her pocket and gave it to a neighbor she knew only by this: that the neighbor had a hungry child. She expected nothing back. And yet, when she returned home, her own supper tasted different — not because she had more, but because she had stopped counting what she had left.

---

## What it means

Siddhartha Gautama left the palace and did not seek a new throne. He sought the **middle way** — between indulgence and austerity, between clinging and indifference, between hoarding and emptiness.

His wisdom can be distilled into three gestures:

- **Recognize** — suffering exists, and denying it does not diminish it.
- **Release** — its root is clinging, not the world around you.
- **Walk the middle** — act with full commitment, without grasping at the result.

Buddha's salt does not accumulate. **Salt dissolves.** Cast into water, it ceases to be "salt that we own" and becomes the taste of the water that belongs to everyone who drinks. It does not own the water. It demands no share. And yet without it, the water is only dead liquid.

That is the question Buddha would put to any network that calls itself salt: **Does it accumulate, or does it dissolve?**
