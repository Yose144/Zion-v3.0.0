# Chapter 4 — Three Keys, Three Seats

*Year 2035 in the game calendar. Ten years after the first block, the Game of the Golden Egg begins — and in a city that has forgotten taste, the children learn that keys are not bought.*

---

When the Proclamation of the Egg was announced on the tower inscriptions, Bezechut split into two kinds of people: those who wanted to win — and those who wanted to find.

The Whale arrived first. It sailed into the harbour behind the market on a machine bigger than the ship itself — a stack of metal that cast a shadow over half the squares and hummed like a swarm. "I will buy a hundred and eight seekers," it said. "And their machines. And time. I will find the keys first — because keys always belong to the one who pays more."

The Guild of Cartographers came second — a hundred and eight maps, each folded from the hands of hundreds of seekers, none belonging to a single person. "We share the clues," said their foreman. "The key is found by the one who assembles the map more honestly than the others." And it was true — they assembled honestly, until the moment two of them stopped assembling the same piece of the map twice, because each wanted to be the one who assembled it better.

And then there was the smith's son.

He had no stack of machines, no guild, no map. He had a compass — five points carved on a slip of wood his father had given him: truth, freedom, unity, love, service. "When you get lost," his father had once told him, "don't look for the way back to Babylon. Look for the compass. Babylon taught you to seek the way to the throne — the compass will teach you to seek the way to truth."

---

The hundred and eight clues were scattered across the whole chain — in block headers, in address labels, in old inscriptions left on the walls when the city was repainted. The Whale passed the first three clues in a day — its machines solved riddles at a speed that hurried the city clock by a minute. The Guild of Cartographers passed five in a week — every clue read a hundred times, every map redrawn.

The son passed one in a month.

Not because he was slow. Because every clue he found taught him something the Whale's machine could not — it taught him that a clue is not an answer but a question about oneself. "Who are you when you seek?" asked the eighteenth clue. "What are you seeking — a key, or a reason?" asked the forty-seventh. The Whale answered quickly and moved on. The son answered slowly — and stayed with the question until it told him something about himself.

The first key — copper — he received not for a riddle but for constancy: seventy days of returning to the same clue, even though it told him he would find nothing. The key appeared on the day he returned for the third time — because a key is not an answer to a question but proof that you did not forget the question. "Work," said the smith when his son brought the key home. "Copper is forged slowly — and it holds."

The jasper key he received for patience with the unsolvable. Three months at a clue that made no sense — and he did not tear himself away. The saddler told him it meant "to endure even when it makes no sense" — and he endured. The key came quietly, the way a stone gives way in water nobody heard: not because something changed, but because he stayed long enough to hear it.

The crystal key was the hardest — for truth even against his own advantage. The clue offered him a shortcut: tell a lie and the key comes faster — or tell the truth and lose the key. The son told the truth. And the key that was meant not to appear, appeared — because crystal does not break upon a lie.

---

The Whale meanwhile gathered keys differently — it hired seekers who treated the keys as a task. They brought it two pieces of metal that looked like keys — and were not. "A key cannot be bought," said the carpenter when she showed them to him. "You can only buy a piece of metal that looks like a key. A real key is not a thing — it is proof that something endured. And proof cannot be stolen or sold — only carried."

The Guild of Cartographers found one key — jasper — and then fell apart. Not because the maps overlapped, but because each wanted to be the one to hand it in. "You will find the key," the carpenter told them when they came asking for an arbiter, "when you stop competing over who holds it. A key is not property. A key is proof — and proof is not about the owner, but about what endured." The guild fell apart — and the key they had held as a trophy lay on the arbiter's table. The carpenter handed it to the son — not because it belonged to him, but because the son was the only one who did not ask what he would get for it.

---

In the square where the ark stood, the three keys came together for the first time — the son carried all three, the way one carries what one holds, not owns: the copper he had earned himself; the jasper the carpenter had handed him from the arbiter's table; the crystal that came of itself when the son told the truth against his own advantage. And in the light the ark returned to the city, the three keys lit up like three doors waiting for the one who would pass through them.

"Three keys," said the carpenter, who came quietly, as always. "Copper — work. Jasper — patience. Crystal — truth. Do you know what is strange about them?"

"That I have them?" said the son.

"That they are not yours," said the carpenter. "The keys unlock the door of the game — and the door is for the one who is to come. The winner of OASIS is the one who walks the path and finds the egg — not the one who holds the keys. You hold them as a guardian — you hold them for the one who will use them."

"And how will I know the one who is to use them?"

"By the fact that they come on foot," said the carpenter. "And they don't take the keys — they take the path."

---

In the evening the son sat down beside his father and laid the three keys on the table. Copper, jasper, crystal — three small proofs that patience, work and truth can be won, but not bought.

"Dad," he said. "The Whale bought pieces of metal that look like keys — and aren't. The Guild found one — and they quarrelled over it until the carpenter gave it to me. I have three — and I don't know what to do with them. I am not the one meant to use them — I can feel it."

"You feel it rightly," said the father. "You don't hold the keys for yourself — you hold them for the one who will walk the path and find the egg. The winner of OASIS is the one who arrives — not the one who carries the keys. Your work is to hold the key for the one who comes. Not to open."

"And what about me — am I not the one who is to come?"

The father looked at him — long, the way one looks at iron that is not finished yet, but already holds its shape.

"You will come differently," he said. "The game of the egg is for everyone — the winner is the one who walks the path. You were born into a different story: the signature beneath the game bears your name — and with that name goes not the egg, but the seat. You win the game by the path — you receive the seat by surrender. The game rewards the one who seeks. The seat waits for the one who grows up."

---

That night the son received the last testimony — not about keys, but about seats. On the workshop wall, beside the leaf with the genesis message, hung the list of guardians — seven addresses, three of which bore the inscription:

*Guardian 1 — Rama → Maitreya Buddha*
*Guardian 2 — Sita → Sarah Issobela*
*Guardian 3 — Hanuman → Elizabeth*

Three seats, three names — and behind each name a "→", like an arrow showing whom the seat belongs to once they grow up.

"Seats are not won," said the father, who came quietly, as always. "The game of the egg has its winner — the one who finds it. The seats have their stewards — those who grow up. Rama holds the fire — for the one who understands the protocol. Sita holds the water — for your sister, whose name the station bears. Hanuman holds the earth — for Elizabeth, who has not come yet. And the air — the air is held by the space in which they all sit. The one who holds the space does not hold a throne — they hold what everyone breathes."

"The chain holds the seats," the son repeated quietly — and for the first time understood that the keys were never meant to open his door. The keys were for the pilgrims. The seats were for the children — stewards, not owners.
