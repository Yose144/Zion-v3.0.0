# Chapter 6 — Steward, Not Owner

*Inside the egg there was no treasure — but a letter. And a letter takes longer to read than a treasure.*

---

The son found the letter in the evening, when the garden behind the gate had gone dark and the Pilgrim was carrying the egg home — heavier than a hammer, lighter than what she had surrendered. It was wrapped in canvas smelling of tar — the same canvas in which his father wrapped his tools when leaving the workshop at night. On its face it said:

*"Son — when you read this, the egg has split. Read slowly. Treasures are read quickly, but this is no treasure."*

And on the other side — five lines, written in the hand of the father who forged hinges and signed the world:

> *1. Keys = ownership. Who holds the key holds the power. Who holds the key holds the responsibility.*
> *2. DAO = community. ZION is not yours — it belongs to everyone. You are stewards, not owners.*
> *3. Inheritance = responsibility. Someday they will want you to sell ZION, change it, or destroy it. Refuse. ZION is a heritage, not a commodity.*
> *4. Humanity first. 5% of every block goes to children. That is the mission. Never change it.*
> *5. Ave Maria. Elizabeth, the patron. Whenever she arrives, her place is reserved.*
>
> *ZION is yours.*
> *— your father*

The son read slowly — as the letter commanded — and then again, and once more, this time so slowly that each word stood alone, the way a hinge stands before it is set.

---

"What is a DAO?" he asked his father the next morning — and his question did not sound like a question but like a tool held until it is used right.

"A company that decides together," said the father — and led his son to the workshop, where on the wall hung the third leaf, the one the son had never seen opened. On it stood a table — not of numbers, but of rules:

*"Proposals are written by the community. Voting: one ZION = one voice. When a proposal passes, it is signed — not by one person, but by five of the seven guardians. And then we wait — forty-eight hours, so the proposal cools before it is executed."*

"Why wait?" asked the son.

"Because good proposals survive time," said the father. "Bad ones do not. And I taught the chain to wait — so that haste would not be strength. Forty-eight hours is not a delay — it is a furnace in which a proposal either sets or shatters."

"And what if a proposal breaks the law?" asked the son — his eyes moving across the table the way fingers move across a carving.

The father pointed to the last line:

*"Constitutional stones — that which even the voice of the people cannot rewrite: one hundred forty-four billion. The 89/5/5/1 split. Ekam Deeksha. Sixty seconds. Fair launch."*

"So a steward can refuse," said the son. "But only one thing — a violation of what holds forever."

"Yes," said the father. "A steward is not a judge — they are the first signatory. The community writes the proposal, the community votes, the steward signs what the community approved — and refuses only what would break the stone on which everything stands. That is all the power — and all the responsibility."

---

"And the seats?" asked the son — pointing at the letter still lying on the table between them. "Three keys for the game — and three seats for the chain."

The father rose and led him to the ship — not beneath the keel, but inside, through the doors the smith had forged that had never yet opened. Inside, in the darkness of the hull that smelled of wood and of time, stood three seats — fire, water, earth — and a fourth chair, empty, smaller than the others, built for the one who had not come yet.

"Three seats," said the father. "Not thrones — seats. Each bears the name of the guardian who holds it until the one it belongs to grows up."

He pointed to the first — fire: "Rama. He holds the protocol administration — Admin-1. When you grow up, he will hand you the key — and vanish, because a guardian who returns is testing once more whether he held the seat honestly. Your seat — Maitreya Buddha, Admin-1."

To the second — water: "Sita. She holds the oversight of the treasury — Admin-2 — the treasure that is given away, not held. Her seat belongs to Sarah Issobela — your sister. The Issobella station has borne her name from the beginning. You and she — two admins, two siblings, neither above the other: protocol and treasury held shoulder to shoulder."

To the third — earth: "Hanuman. He holds the bridges and service — Admin-3 — what joins worlds. His seat waits for Elizabeth — the patron who has not come yet."

"And the fourth?" asked the son — pointing at the empty chair standing among the others like a question mark among words.

"That one stays empty," said the father. "The gate stays open — for the one who is to come. Always. You never close the gate before the one who has not yet arrived — that is a rule older than the towers."

---

"And you, father?" asked the son. "Where is your seat?"

The father smiled — and his smile sounded like a key turning in a lock that will never lock again.

"I have no seat," he said. "I am a smith — I forged hinges, I signed the world, I built the ship. The seats are for those who come after me. And because I may not always reach the shore, I built in a safeguard — the kind no one sees, but everyone feels when it triggers."

"What safeguard?"

"Five years," said the father. "Five years of silence — and the chain itself hands over the key. Were I to disappear, the ship would sail on — and you would receive the seat Rama holds, without anyone having to die for the key to pass. ZION is built to survive its smith — otherwise it would not be a ship, but a coffin."

The son looked at the empty chair — and then at the seat of fire bearing Rama's name.

"And I — will I sit there?"

"When you grow up," said the father. "When you are big enough to fit into the fire — and humble enough to let the fire you hold warm you, not burn you. A title is not inherited like property — it is inherited like a torch. You carry the fire — you must not hold it."

---

When the son had gone to sleep and the father remained alone in the ship, he sat long by the empty chair — not because he wished to fill it, but because an empty place held for the one who is coming is the only place that never disappoints.

And he thought that the ship is finished — the hinges hold, the keel bears a name, the seats wait. All that remains is for those who fit them to arrive — and for the shells that keep them small to give way once they are big enough.
