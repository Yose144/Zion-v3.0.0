## Chapter 12 — Hiranyagarbha: the primordial womb

*The Rigveda, the golden egg and the archetype of creation*

---

### A hymn from the dawn of ages

In the **Rigveda** — the oldest of the four Vedas, dated approximately to 1700–1100 BCE — in the Tenth Book (Mandala X), more precisely in **Hymn 121** (ṚV X.121), there is one of the deepest cosmological texts of humanity. The hymn has ten stanzas; the fifth says:

> *Hiranyagarbhas samavartata agre*
> *bhūtasya jātaḥ patireka āsīt.*
> *Sa dādhara pṛthivīm dyāmutemāṃ*
> *kasmai devāya haviṣā vidhema.*

*("In the beginning there was the golden germ — Hiranyagarbha. When it was born, it was the sole Lord of all that exists. It upheld this Earth and the heavens. To which of the gods should we bring offering?")

The word **hiranya** in Sanskrit means *gold* or *golden*. The word **garbha** means *womb*, *germ*, *embryo* or *uterus*. Hiranyagarbha therefore literally: **the Golden Womb** or **the Golden Germ**.

---

### The cosmological structure of the myth

Hiranyagarbha in the Rigvedic hymn is not a description of a physical process. It is a **cosmogonic image** — a way of thinking about the origin of existence that works with symbol, not with a causal chain.

Before Hiranyagarbha there is nothing — or more precisely: there is *the dark, undifferentiated that* (*tamas*), primordial water, from which nothing has worked itself free. Then — suddenly, without a cause from the observer's point of view — a golden light appears in the darkness. An egg. A germ.

From Hiranyagarbha **Brahma** is born — the creator. Not Brahman (the impersonal absolute), but Brahma (the personal creative principle). Brahma is the one who creates multiplicity — worlds, beings, forms, time.

But Brahma did not arise from nothing. He arose from the Golden Womb that was earlier — and that remains his foundation. **Creation is not a falling away from oneness. It is the unfolding of a germ that carries oneness from the beginning.**

This structure is essential: the world is not a mistake. Multiplicity is not a fall. Forms are not an illusion (maya) that must be rejected. They are the blossoming of a germ whose foundation is golden consciousness.

---

### Hiranyagarbha in the Upaniṣads and Purāṇas

The Rigvedic image is developed further in later texts:

In the **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** (approximately 800–600 BCE) it is described how Brahma sits inside a golden egg in the cosmic ocean for a thousand years, meditates and out of his contemplation creates the world. Time inside the egg is different from time after creation. The golden egg is outside time — as consciousness is outside time.

In the **Viṣṇu Purāṇa** (an early medieval compilation) Hiranyagarbha is identified with Vishnu as the germ before the cosmic cycle. This identification joined Vaishnava theology with Rigvedic cosmogony.

In the **Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad** — a text central to Advaita Vedanta — *turīya* is described, the fourth state of consciousness beyond waking, dreaming and deep sleep. Turīya is described as *śuddha-caitanya* — pure consciousness, the basis of the other states. Hiranyagarbha and turīya are in the commentarial tradition of Advaita (Shankara, 8th century CE) related notions: the germ of consciousness as the condition of the possibility of any experience.

---

### The golden egg as a universal archetype

It is remarkable that the golden cosmic egg is not a specifically Indian image. Analogous myths occur in:

- **Greek Orphic cosmogony**: the egg of Phanes, from which Eros/Protogonos hatches and forms the world
- **The Finnish Kalevala**: the egg of a duck laid on the knee of Ilmatar, from which sky, earth, sun and moon arise
- **Egyptian mythology**: Ra in his solar egg, born daily from the primordial ocean Nun
- **The Chinese Taoist myth**: P'an-ku inside an egg whose cracking separated heaven and earth

In Jungian terms: the golden cosmic egg is an **archetypal image** — arising independently in different cultures because it reflects the structure of the human psyche and its intuition about the relation of oneness and multiplicity, of the potential and the actual, of the germ and the full form.

## Chapter 13 — The Golden Orb as a modern Hiranyagarbha

*From the ancient egg to a physical symbol*

---

### A bridge between tradition and the present

The Ekam line takes a remarkable step: it took one of the oldest cosmological images of the Vedic tradition — Hiranyagarbha — and embodied it as a physical object in the centre of its holiest space.

The Golden Orb is not only decoration. It is a **deliberate materialisation of an archetypal image**. Anyone who enters the Sanctum of the Ekam temple stands face to face with a symbol thousands of years old, grounded in Rigvedic theology, rendered into the present language of form.

This choice is theologically considered. Ekam does not claim to bring a new teaching. It claims to **awaken what has always been**. Hiranyagarbha in the Vedas and the Golden Orb in the temple are an attempt to say: *The germ of golden consciousness existed at the beginning. It exists in you now. All that matters is to feel it — not to understand it.*

---

### The symbolic structure of resonance

A table of parallels between Hiranyagarbha and the Golden Orb:

| Dimension | Hiranyagarbha (Vedas) | Golden Orb (Ekam) |
|---|---|---|
| Form | An egg/sphere in darkness | A sphere at the centre of the Sanctum |
| Colour | Golden (hiranya) | Golden |
| Position | At the beginning, before creation | At the centre, before individual prayer |
| Function | The germ of the universe | The germ of a new identity |
| Relation to consciousness | Golden consciousness as substrate | A symbol of the present consciousness of every person |
| Time | Outside time (cosmic) | Outside time (meditative presence) |

The analogy is not accidental. It is the **design intention** of Amma and Bhagavan: to create a physical place where an old cosmogonic image lives in the bodily presence of every visitor.

---

### The sphere as the womb of a new human being

An extension of the metaphor: the Ekam temple as a whole is the **macrocosm**, in which the Golden Orb is the *sanctum* — the Sanctum — the microcosm. Entering the temple = entering the golden egg. Sitting in meditation by the Golden Orb = sitting inside Hiranyagarbha.

What Bhagavan tries to make possible is an experiential moment in which a person feels: **I am inside the germ, not outside it**. I am not a separate individuality asking about meaning. I am an expression of the golden germ — its manifestation, not its product.

This experience, if it occurs, needs no philosophical justification. It comes as immediate sensing. Precisely therefore it needs **a physical environment that opens to it** — architecture, light, symbol, quiet space.

---

### The personal Hiranyagarbha

The most important shift that Ekam offers is not from the golden egg in a text to the golden sphere in a temple. The most important one is the shift from the temple to the body:

*"The golden germ does not lie in India. It lies in you. At the centre of your being, beneath all the noise of the mind, beyond all fear, is this golden silent consciousness. Hiranyagarbha is your deepest nature."*

— Bhagavan, a teaching quoted at Oneness University, 2006

This personalisation of a cosmological image is typical of the Ekam line: the Vedas are maps. The goal is the direct experience of what the maps point to. The Rigvedic hymn is a pointer. The Golden Orb is a pointer. Your body in the present moment is the place where golden consciousness actually exists — or rather, where it is as yet undisclosed.

## Chapter 14 — Cosmology, quantum physics and the bridge between worlds

*The golden germ in the language of the 21st century*

---

### The problem of translation

One of the greatest challenges of every spiritual movement in the postmodern age is the question of language: **How to speak of experiences for which we have no adequate conceptual apparatus without lying or simplifying beyond the admissible limit?**

Two errors are symmetrically dangerous:

1. **Archaic language without translation** — to speak only in Sanskrit and refer to the Vedas will convince only those who are already within the tradition. The rest of civilisation, raised on scientific thought, will not hear.

2. **Pseudoscientific translation** — to use the vocabulary of quantum physics manipulatively to "confirm" spiritual claims that physics does not in fact make. This error led to the wave of *quantum woo* in the 1990s and 2000s (Deepak Chopra, "What the Bleep Do We Know" etc.), counterproductive for serious inquiry.

Ekam does not avoid this problem. Krishnaji and Preethaji in their modern books (*The Way of the Awakened*, 2019; *Four Sacred Secrets*, 2019) use quantum language with an awareness of its limits: **as a metaphor, not as a physical claim**.

---

### Analogy, not equivalence

This book adopts an analogical, not an equivalential stance towards physics and spiritual experience:

**Quantum superposition** — until the moment of measurement a system exists in several states at once — is analogous to the meditative experience of an open field of possibilities in which identity has not yet decided for any meaning. But the Rigvedic *Hiranyagarbha* does not need quantum mechanics for its justification. It is consistent as a symbol in itself.

**Quantum discontinuity** (jumps of energy levels without a classical transitional trajectory) is analogous to the description of *śaktipāta* — Deeksha — as a discontinuous jump of consciousness that cannot be explained by the linear accumulation of practice. But from the physical point of view it remains a metaphor.

**Field as the basis of particles** (in Quantum Field Theory "particles" are excitations of fields) corresponds to the intuition that consciousness is a field and individual "selves" are its excitations — transient shape waves, not solid entities. But Quantum Field Theory does not study consciousness; the claims are on different levels of description of reality.

---

### What remains once the language is set aside

If we set aside both the Rigvedic language and the quantum language, what is Ekam actually touching?

**A phenomenological claim**: people who have gone through intensive Deeksha practice or Ekam meditation processes repeatedly describe strong experiences whose common denominator is:

- The vanishing of the feeling of a separate boundary of the individual
- A feeling of consciousness "spilling" into space
- A deep quieting accompanied by alertness
- The absence of fear
- A feeling of germinality — as though the "I" were a new, pure beginning without the weight of the past
- A paradoxical experience: more present, individual, concrete — even though the boundaries of the "I" have loosened

This phenomenology is consistent across cultural, linguistic and psychological contexts. It has been described — in different words — by St John of the Cross, Rumi, Ramana Maharshi, Nisargadatta, the Buddhist Prajñāpāramitā texts, the Sufi tradition of *fana*, William James in *Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), and modern scientists such as Andrew Newberg (**neurotheology**, fMRI research of mystical experience, 2001–2010).

**The golden germ is an image for this experience** — the experience of a consciousness that realises its own germinality: it is the basis, not the product; it is the carrier, not the content; it is silent and golden beneath all the noise.
