# The First Gate — gate
## Chapter 1 · Leaving Babylon

*MYTH: the little Buddha and his path are the story of this book, not an event that happened. Babylon is an archetype of the old world in the corpus — not a specific city.*

---

## The Story

The boy was born in Babylon, like you, Surta.

Babylon was not an evil city. That would be simple — and bad stories are always simple. Babylon was a big, shiny and loud city. It had shops that never closed, screens that never slept, and people ran through it so fast that if one of them stopped and looked back, they would see only their own shadow trying to keep up.

The boy lived in a house with warm heating, a full fridge and a mother who loved him. He had nothing to complain about — and yet one evening, as he entered his thirteenth year, he asked a question for which Babylon has no answer:

"And what is behind all of it?"

No one answered him. Not because they did not want to — because asking was unusual in Babylon, like asking at a bus stop where the wind blows from.

That night he dreamed of an egg. Not an ordinary one — **a golden egg**, the golden womb of Hiranyagarbha, glowing on a dark bottom while something inside breathed, stumbled, lived. And when the boy woke, he understood two things at once: that the world he lived in was not the whole world — only its first room. And that the egg from the dream was nowhere out there — **that he carried it within himself from birth, that each of us carries it, only few hear it breathing.**

But growing up takes a while — and the question waited. The boy went to school for two more years, played, grew — and the egg in him kept breathing, only louder. **And then, when he was fifteen and had the sense for it**, he stood before the door. The mother watched and understood what cannot be understood: that some departures are not flight but obedience.

"Will you come back?" she asked.

"When I have something to bring you," the boy said, not yet knowing that the words he spoke were a bodhisattva's vow — only in a child's garment. He had promised it long ago, in fact — on the beach in Tenerife, seven years old, with a little boat in his hand, thinking about how to save the world and help everyone.

And he went.

That is the whole first chapter, Surta: **a boy in the doorway, a backpack still too big for him, and an open road.** To leave is the first mantra. *Gate* — you went. Nothing more can be said, because nothing more can be done at that moment.

Beyond the city, where the last lamp ended, the mountains waited.

## A treasure for Surta

Listen well: **the first gate is always departure.** Not from people — from what tells you that the world is only what can be bought and looked at.

To leave does not mean to demolish the house. It means to ask the question for which the shop has no price tag: *And what is behind all of it?* You have already done it — children ask like this by nature, before Babylon unlearns it in them. Keep that question like a stone in your pocket. When the world one day asks you why you are still not silent, take it out and look it in the eye.

And remember: the mother asked whether he would return. **Whoever leaves with a promise of return does not leave from love — they leave from love.** That is the difference between a path and an escape.
