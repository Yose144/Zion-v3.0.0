# Episode 4 — The Unbeaten Path

*Where the map ends, the compass begins — and so does the bridge no one will build unless they start before anyone asks them to.*

> *"Where does your network lead when there is no map for it?"*
> *"I do not know exactly where every road leads. But I know which way north is."*

---

## The Story

On the morning after the shepherd's song, three more came to Tasteless — and for the first time they did not come along the road by which everyone else arrived. They came through the forest, where there was no road, because no one from the palaces ever went that way willingly.

**Rama** had a bow slung across his back, but he was not going to war. He was a king who had willingly left his own realm — fourteen years of exile, not as a punishment but as a test of whether he could keep his shape even where no throne enforced it.

**Sita** did not follow him as a husband's shadow. She went because the forest was as much hers as the palace — she was *Bhūmija*, daughter of the earth itself, lifted from the furrow of the plough. Where else would she belong but to the land that gave her birth?

And **Hanuman**, who was neither king nor queen, only a servant with such strength that he could leap across an ocean in a single bound, came last. Not because he was the least — because his task always comes last: to build the bridge others will cross.

At the old fountain the carpenter, the pilgrim with the bowl and the shepherd with the flute were waiting for them — and with them all that remained of Tasteless: the water carrier, the soldier, the woman who had stopped counting what was left.

"You have a parable, a test and a song," said Rama as he sat down by the fire. "We have only a forest without a road. How will salt, dissolution and music help us where there is no map?"

"How did you walk through the forest when you had no map?" asked the carpenter.

"By dharma," Rama answered. "Not a rule carved in stone, but the question we ask again with every step: what is right action, here, now, regardless of what it costs?"

Sita knelt and gathered clay from the edge of the dry fountain into her palms. "This land is no less mine because I am a stranger in it," she said. "The test does not come in a palace, where it is easy to be honest when it costs you nothing. It comes in a forest where no one is watching — and you remain who you are anyway."

Meanwhile Hanuman bent over the empty channel of the fountain and began to clear the stones that had blocked it for years. No one had asked him to.

"What are you doing?" asked the soldier.

"Building a bridge," Hanuman answered simply.

"There is no ocean here."

"Not yet," said Hanuman, lifting a stone three men could not have raised together. "But the water this shepherd sang about will come here one day. And when it comes, it will be of no use if it has no way to flow to the people who have waited years for it and stopped believing it would ever come."

The woman who had buried three children sat down beside Sita and handed her the rest of the salt she still carried in her pocket. She did not have to explain — Sita understood at once, because that itself was dharma: to give what you have to the one beside you without counting the days until repayment.

"The strength you have you will not know until you forget that you have it, and leap," Hanuman murmured, more to himself than to the others, and threw another stone out of the path of the future water.

That night no one in Tasteless knew exactly when the water would come. But for the first time since anyone could remember, the channel of the fountain was passable again — clean, empty, waiting. And the waiting, for the first time, was not desperate. It was patient.

## What it means

Rama could not use a map — the Dandaka forest was on no map, because no one from the palace ever went there willingly. The only thing he had was **dharma**: not a command written on stone, but a compass that asks again at every moment what right action is, regardless of the cost.

**Sita** defined herself neither by the palace she left nor by the forest she entered. She was a daughter of the earth — and therefore belonged to both equally. Her test was not whether gold and power would hold, but whether fidelity to what is true would hold even in the hardest choice.

**Hanuman** was not on the road to conquer anything for himself. He was there to serve — and his strength showed not in a throne but in a bridge over which a whole army, a whole people, anyone who wanted to cross to the other side could pass.

This is exactly the image ZION needs for the road from here onward: **a compass instead of a map, fidelity to the earth instead of fidelity to the palace, and a bridge instead of a throne.**
