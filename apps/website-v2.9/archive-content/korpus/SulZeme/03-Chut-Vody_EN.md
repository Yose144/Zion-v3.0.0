# Episode 3 — The Taste of Water

*The greatest power does not boast of a throne. It hides in the taste of the water you drink without thinking about it.*

> *"I am the taste of water, the light of the sun and the moon... the ability in a human being."*
> — Bhagavad Gita 7.8

---

## The Story

The third pilgrim did not come along the road. He came with music.

Tasteless heard him before it saw him — a quiet, gentle tone of a flute that carried over the walls like the smell of rain whose nature no one remembered. People came out of their houses, not because someone had called them, but because the sound loosened a knot in them they had not known they had.

A shepherd with dark skin and a smile that did not look as if anyone had ever managed to take it from him sat down on a stone by the fountain, where water had once been and now only a dry channel remained. He put the flute to his lips again and played on, as if the emptiness of the fountain did not trouble him at all.

"The fountain is dry," the woman told him — the woman who by then knew the whole story of Tasteless better than anyone else: the salt from the carpenter, the dissolution from the pilgrim with the bowl, and now this.

"The water is here," the shepherd answered, "it has only forgotten how it tastes."

"What do you mean?"

The shepherd set down the flute and looked at her in a way that made her, for a moment, feel not like a woman who had lost three children but like someone he had known forever. "Ask me who I am," he said.

"Who are you?"

And to that simple question the shepherd did not answer with words. He closed his eyes, and those standing nearest — the water carrier, the soldier, the woman, the carpenter who happened to be returning from the field at that moment, and the pilgrim with the bowl who had come from the other side of the town — all of them saw, for a moment, more than they could name. They did not see a king. They did not see a god on a throne with thunder in his hand. They saw **everything at once**: a drop of rain falling into a river, a star dying above the mountains a thousand years before their birth, a child being born at that very moment three towns away, and their own breath going out and returning, as if it had never begun and never stopped.

For a moment it was unbearable. And then it was gone, and before them stood only the shepherd with the flute, his smile a little sad, a little amused.

"Why did you show us that?" asked the soldier, his voice shaking for the first time since he had arrived in the town.

"Because you asked me who I am," the shepherd answered, "and I gave you the truth, not politeness. I am not the king of this town. I am the taste of the water you drink. I am the light by which you see. I am that ability in you which you use when you decide to do the right thing even though no one is watching."

"And what are we to do with that?" asked the water carrier, who had spent his whole life caring only about getting water from the well to the houses.

"Nothing extra," the shepherd answered. "Do your work — the water carrier carries water, the soldier guards the gate, the carpenter builds, the mother cares. But do it without expecting a reward for the result. Krishna does not tell Arjuna to put down his bow. He tells him to fight without clinging to victory. The work itself is the offering, if you do it with your whole heart and let go of the fruits."

That night the fountain did not fill. The water did not start flowing again through the channel that had been drying for years. But the water carrier, as he carried his usual ten buckets from the river to the houses of the old women, noticed for the first time in years how the water cooled his palms, how it shone in the moonlight, how it tasted when he allowed himself a single sip on the way. And he thought that perhaps this — the ordinary, endlessly repeated step — was the whole prayer he knew.

## What it means

In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna begs Krishna to show him his true form. Krishna gives him the **vishvarupa** — the universal form in which all beings, all worlds and all times appear at once in one body. It is a majesty that is not pomp. It is the revelation that **the greatest is hidden in the smallest.**

Krishna does not say "I am the king of water". He says **"I am the taste of water"** — that invisible property that makes water something worth drinking.

Krishna's flute is hollow. If it were full, it would not sound. Its music passes through the emptiness inside it. That is how a network that wants to carry value without hoarding it works — it is a channel, not a vessel.

Krishna's message to builders is direct: **act without clinging to the fruits of your action** (karma yoga). The work you do with your whole heart is meaning in itself — not a means to a reward you calculate in advance.
