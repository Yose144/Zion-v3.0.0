# Episode 2 — Dissolution

*A heap of salt just sits there. Salt in water gives taste to the whole river.*

> *"As a river flows to the ocean, let your mind flow too — without clinging, without resistance."*
> — a paraphrase, inspired by the Buddhist teaching of *anattā* (non-clinging)

---

## The Story

A week later, a second pilgrim came to Tasteless. He had no bow, no sword, nothing that could be used as a weapon or as a proud proof of the road. He had only a begging bowl, and he walked slowly, as if on the road he had never cared about anything more than the step he was taking.

The water carrier, the soldier and the woman, who still carried a handful of salt from the carpenter in their pockets, met him by the well.

"Who are you?" asked the soldier, who trusted no one without a weapon — because in a town where taste had vanished everyone survived in their own way, and defenceless men were usually either saints or dead within a week.

"I was a prince," the pilgrim answered. "I had a palace, a wife, a son, endless stores of rice, and servants who lit incense for me before sleep. And I was unhappier than anyone else in my kingdom, because I saw beyond the walls old age, sickness and death, and I could not unsee them."

"And now?" asked the woman.

"Now I have a bowl," he said, and raised it. "And I know it is enough."

They sat down beside him at the edge of the well. He was a pilgrim in no hurry to go anywhere, and that in itself was strange in Tasteless — everyone else was rushing after the taste they had lost, as if a faster step could catch it.

"The carpenter gave you salt," the pilgrim said when he saw the white grain in the soldier's palm. "Salt is a good thing. But I will ask you a different question: what will you do when you are given too much of it?"

No one answered at once.

"If you had a whole warehouse of salt," he went on, "it would stop tasting. Salt that does not dissolve is only stone dust. A heap of salt preserves nothing — it just lies there and waits to become property that people will fight over."

"So what are we to do with it?" asked the water carrier.

"Dissolve it," said the pilgrim. "Not store it. Not own it. Dissolve it — into water, into bread, into the soup you give a neighbour without expecting it back. Salt you keep in your fist only cuts you. Salt you release into the river gives taste to the whole village downstream."

The soldier frowned. All his life he had believed that survival meant accumulating — food, weapons, scars as proof that he had outlived others. "That sounds like weakness," he said.

"I tried the power of the palace," the pilgrim answered calmly. "I had an army. I had gold. And still I woke at night with a question no army can answer: *what is it all for, when it ends?* The Middle Way does not mean giving up everything or clinging to everything. It means acting — and letting go. Dissolving the salt and letting it do its work without watching whether the river is stealing it from you."

That night no one in Tasteless grew rich and no one grew poor. But the woman who had buried three children took a handful of salt from her pocket for the first time in a long while and gave it to a neighbour about whom she knew only that she had a hungry child. She expected nothing back. And yet, when she returned home, her own supper tasted different — not because she had more, but because she had stopped counting what was left.

## What it means

Siddhārtha Gautama left the palace and did not look for a new throne. He looked for the **Middle Way** — between indulgence and renunciation, between clinging and indifference, between accumulation and emptiness.

His wisdom can be summed up in three gestures:

- **Recognize** — suffering exists, and denying it does not diminish it.
- **Release** — its root is clinging, not the world around us.
- **Walk the middle** — act with full commitment without clinging to the result.

The Buddha's salt is not accumulated. **Salt dissolves.** When it is thrown into water it stops being "salt we own" and becomes the taste of the water that everyone who drinks it has. It does not own the water. It does not demand a share. And yet without it the water is only dead liquid.

That is the question the Buddha would put to every network that calls itself salt: **Does it accumulate, or does it dissolve?**
