## The Story

The ark grew slowly. The ribs of the hull could already be seen from the other end of Tasteless, but the deck was only half laid and the door the builder had spoken of did not yet have hinges.

The blacksmith, who had lost his workshop in a fire years before, came every morning first. He did not know how to build boats — he knew how to forge nails, bands and hinges, and so he did exactly that until his hands turned black. The farmer from the surrounding fields came right after him, with a sack of planks he would otherwise have burned in his stove. Neither of them had been at the first fire where the carpenter opened the salt. They came later, when there was something to do with your hands, not only to listen.

The rest of the town laughed at them.

"Carpenters of the rain," the tavern keeper in the market called them, and the laughter carried across the whole square. "Hauling wood for a flood that may never come. That plank would earn you more as a sack of flour than that madman's boat ever will."

At night the blacksmith woke with a doubt he never admitted aloud: *What if the tavern keeper is right? What if I am forging hinges for something that will rot on the shore before a single drop falls?* His hands ached from the hammer and he had no certainty at all — only the builder's word and a compass with north carved into it.

One evening a merchant appeared at the fire, returning from a long journey overseas. He did not know Tasteless, did not know the ark; he only saw the light and sat down.

"You are building a boat on dry land," he said, more amused than mocking. "That reminds me of something I heard far from here, in a land beyond the sea, where people began to dig up a strange stone that cannot be touched or weighed — only counted. It was so worthless that one man gave ten thousand pieces of that stone for two flatbreads with sauce, and he was glad that anyone took food for the stone at all. People laughed at him, just as they laugh at you. They told him he was wasting time playing with numbers that would never be worth anything."

"And what happened then?" asked the farmer.

The merchant shrugged. "I do not know for certain what is happening now; I was on the road for years. But I know this: those who dug up that stone while everyone was still laughing had more of it, earlier and cheaper, than those who came later, once it was talked about in every tavern. I do not know whether the stone will one day become a treasure, or whether it will remain only a stone. No one knows that, not even the man who gave ten thousand pieces for flatbreads. But I know that the first hammer fell before it started raining — not because the carpenter knew exactly when the storm would come, but because he decided to build before anyone could prove him wrong."

The blacksmith was silent for a long time, looking at his blackened hands.

"So then," he said at last, more to himself than to the merchant, "I will keep forging hinges. Not because someone promised me gold at the end. But because when the water comes, I will know which way the doors open — and whoever just sits in the tavern today waiting to see what happens will not know that."

That night no new pilgrim joined the fire with a name anyone would remember. Only the blacksmith and the farmer joined, a little more tired and a little more certain, and the hammers beat on the wood long after dark.

## A true story, not a parable

Here the telling pauses for a moment, because what the merchant said is not invented. It really happened.

On 22 May 2010 the programmer **Laszlo Hanyecz** paid **10,000 bitcoins** on the Bitcointalk forum for two Papa John's pizzas that another forum user ordered for him in Florida in exchange for the coins. At that time there was no exchange, no dollar price worth discussing — bitcoin was a curiosity of a handful of people who mined it on ordinary home computers because the idea of money without a central bank interested them, not the prospect of profit. The reward for one mined block was then **50 BTC**, and the network difficulty was so low that a laptop could handle it.

That day is now celebrated as **Bitcoin Pizza Day** and is the most cited example of how no one — truly no one, including Laszlo himself — knew what would happen to that network next. It was not a clever investment. It was a man who wanted to eat, and who had at hand a stone no one was sure what it was for.

That is not proof that anything similar will repeat with ZION, with any other network, or with anything at all. **It is only a fact about what the first day of every open network looks like: few people, zero certainty, and the possibility of mining something whose future value is decided by none of those sitting at the fire today.**

## What this means for ZION's builders and miners today

The compass from episode 6 does not only show the direction of the story. It also shows this: **a network that is small today will not stay small forever — and what is ordinary today may become rarer tomorrow, not more common.** That is not a prophecy about price. It is a description of how the rules are written, rules you can read right now, without fortune-telling.

Three things are written in ZION's code so that they hold regardless of who reads them:

**The block reward only falls, it never rises.** ZION is today in its first decade (2026–2036), when the reward for a mined block is **5,400.067 ZION** — the highest the protocol will ever pay. With every further decade it falls by one fifth (a factor of 0.8), until after a hundred years it settles at an eternal reward of 724.78 ZION per block. Whoever mines today mines in the decade with the highest reward this protocol will ever know — that is not a promise, it is a schedule written into consensus.

**Network difficulty follows how many people are mining right now, not how many will mine in a year.** Difficulty is recalculated by the LWMA algorithm over the last 60 blocks (with a ±25% clamp) — it responds to the network's current performance, not its future. The fewer machines search for a block today, the larger the share of the blocks found goes to each individual machine that joins now. Once the network fills with more miners (and that is the goal — a network no one mines is not secure), difficulty will rise with them and the same performance will suffice for a smaller share.

**Entry is the same for everyone who comes — but not equally easy at every moment.** Fair launch means that no one received VIP access at the beginning. It does not mean that the beginning lasts forever. The genesis block fell on 1 January 2026; every day that passes is a day more of history, a day more of miners and a day closer to the moment when "starting from zero" will no longer be possible — because zero was long ago.

This is exactly what the blacksmith in the story did: he did not forge hinges because someone guaranteed him a reward. He forged them because he understood the mechanics of rain — that water does not wait for everyone to decide to believe.

## What this chapter does not promise you

Salt that knows it is salt says this out loud too:

- **No one promises that ZION will one day have the value of bitcoin, a fraction of it, or any value at all.** Bitcoin Pizza Day is a historical fact about another network, not a prediction about this one. Nine out of ten stones that people laughed at in taverns over the past fifteen years remained stones.
- **Mining costs real money already today** — electricity, hardware, time — regardless of what value ZION will have tomorrow, in a year, or never. Do not count on a reward you cannot afford not to receive.
- **ZION is Mainnet Alpha/Beta.** A young network, a small number of nodes, open development — mistakes happen and are fixed publicly, not hidden, but they do happen. Whoever enters first also carries the first risk, not only the first opportunity.
- **This is not investment advice.** It is a description of how the mathematics of emission and difficulty works on an open network you can verify yourself in the code, not a reason for a decision you should not think through with your own head.

The compass shows north. It does not show whether it is raining now, or will rain in a year. That is a question every builder must answer themselves — like the blacksmith whom no one guaranteed that the ark would one day sail.
