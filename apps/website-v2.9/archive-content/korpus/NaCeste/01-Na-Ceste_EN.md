# On the Way

*A note outside the story: everything that follows is MYTH — one of the possible HORIZONS, not an event that happened, nor a promised schedule. Lumen, the captains and the whole voyage are invented; the concept and the names of the ships exist, their sailing form and the form of the communities are only an idea; the geography is real, but no one in it speaks for real people, for hosts or for guardians of places. What is documented and what is not is set out in [`02-Kotva-Pravdy.md`](./02-Kotva-Pravdy.md).*

---

## I. The gate and the question

Lumen liked things that could be verified.

She had once entered ZION as an explorer and a player, without great words, only to look. Then she came to the OASIS gate, where seven paths were offered, and out of them she chose the Path of Knowledge herself. The first answer she gave there was inconspicuous: that she first wanted to know where those words come from. From that day she carried in her head the question *How do I recognise the truth?* like a stone in her pocket. Over the years she wore it smooth and firm, and she touched it whenever she was unsure. When no firm shape was at hand, she imagined one.

The later path of the builder was not another gate that someone put in her way. It was a free choice she came to on her own, the way one comes to spring work: only when the soil has warmed.

In one possible future — not the one that will come, but the one that can be imagined — Lumen stood in April on the quay in Pontevedra with a backpack holding three volumes of notes, a backup drive and, above all, a binder. The binder was her pride. She had written into it every step from the train out of Bohemia through Porto to here, with date, hour and a photograph of the stamp. She believed that whoever has a record cannot get lost.

The water in the river Lérez had the colour of old tin. Above the bay stood a ship, smaller than she expected. Its hull was painted a dark red — not royal red, more the red of wine that has lain long in a barrel — and above it sails of pale canvas with thin stripes of solar fibre woven in. On its side in white letters: SANTA MARÍA LA MAYOR.

"Are you the record-keeper?" asked the woman on the bridge. She was broad-shouldered, around fifty, her hands cracked from ropes, and she spoke Spanish with a Galician accent that Lumen had to unravel for a while.

"I hope so. I have a place on trial."

"We are all on trial. Inés Varela, captain. We don't sail tomorrow. Tomorrow you work in the garden."

"In the garden?"

"Our ship has a garden. You don't yet know what it is good for." Inés looked at the binder Lumen held under her arm like a shield. "And what is that?"

"My journey. So far."

"So far," said Inés, and smiled for the first time.

In the evening she led her to a cabin beneath the bow. On the wall hung an embroidered red cloth, similar to a queen's mantle the way a picture is similar to what it depicts, and beneath it, on a nail, a thin book with stiff covers. Inside there was not a single stamp, not a single name.

"An empty credencial," said Inés. "They say the Pilgrim once left it behind, the one who went further than the last town before the river. You may call it a story. I call it a book that is written in only after the journey. Not before it."

Lumen took it carefully and put it back. The Pilgrim was only an image, a shape into which anyone who reached the water and had the courage to return could fit. No person, no name. She liked that, even though it could not be verified.

* * *

The garden lay behind the docks, in a former salt warehouse, where beans, dill, mustard and a row of tomatoes that refused to believe in April grew on terraces. Soil, water and a bad joke were shared here. For two days Lumen dug, weeded and carried buckets. Her hands ached differently than her fingers did from a keyboard — more deeply — and in the evening she fell asleep before she could write anything down.

On the third day old Xoán came with a long spade over his shoulder and watched her filling in the columns.

"What are you writing there?"

"That I transplanted twenty seedlings. And that the soil in the mustard bed has a pH of seven point two."

"And do you know it, or do you think it?"

"I measured it."

"With what?"

Lumen was silent. She had measured it with strips she found in the cupboard. No one had verified them.

"The strips are old," she said at last.

Xoán nodded and handed her a handful of soil. "Then write it like this: *pH seven point two according to old strips, uncertain.* That is an honest record. And now tell me how the tomato feels."

"The tomato?"

"You are a scientist. Look at it."

She looked. The leaf was curling downward, the lower one yellow. "It is afraid?"

"It is thirsty at the roots and has too much water on top. You water it badly." Xoán laughed, not unkindly. "No one here is judging you. The soil will let you err and will forgive you for it. If you want to know the truth, begin by not being afraid to be wrong."

That night Lumen did not open the binder for the first time. She looked from the deck at the lamps in the harbour and felt the soil under her nails growing cold. Above the garden stood a row of channels into which rain water ran down from the terraces, and beside each a small label saying who had last repaired it. That was the custom here: everything that flowed could be taken apart and mended.

## II. Numbers at sea

They sailed in May and knew the voyage would take many months, because ships wait for favourable seasonal windows and no one drives them. The way to Pontevedra led through a garden: in the Nabão valley, between Fátima and the Templar town of Tomar, in the parish of Sabacheira, stood the Genesis Garden with a small albergue for pilgrims. There Lumen first rubbed soil under her nails, and there she got the first stamp in her credencial — the carimbo of the Garden. And there they learned how water is managed: rain was caught from the roofs, well water pumped from a borehole, and fresh water separated from utility water, because polluted water would kill the garden within a few years. One woman exchanged part of their supplies for two jars of honey. Most of the cargo had a traceable origin and the necessary documents, which Lumen protected in her binder. At its bottom, however, lay Xoán's little bag with nothing but a hand-written note.

Then they went out onto the Atlantic. The first night was calm. On the second morning the colour of the sky turned leaden and the waves began to have a will of their own: they did not hesitate, they came in long ranks, lifted the ship and let it drop. The crew took down part of the sails. Lumen sat in the cabin with her feet braced against a locker, sick and pale, trying to keep a record of supplies, because that was her work and because hands that write dampen nausea a little.

She added, subtracted, and the numbers did not agree.

She leafed back. On the day of departure from Pontevedra she had entered in the column *drinking water, tank no. 2: 640 litres*. In fact, as she now saw from the port technician's handover sheet that she had stuck to the back cover, only 460 had flowed into the tank then. She rewrote two digits. Since then the rations had been counted according to her number.

She worked it out in pencil in the margin, then again and again. It came out that tank no. 2 would run dry several days earlier than everyone was being told.

The ship rose and fell. Her heart hammered in her ears. No one will find out, it occurred to her. It is enough to quietly correct the number, there is plenty in tank no. 1 here, and nothing has happened. The binder stays clean.

But it was precisely the binder that had taught her that a record is a covenant with the people who will rely on it. And those people were now sitting one deck up, eating soup according to her number.

She climbed the ladder with a burning face and a sheet of paper in her hand. Inés stood at the helm, lashed with a rope, her eyes narrowed against the rain.

"Captain, there is a mistake of mine in the ship's record. I overwrote a number. Tank no. 2 had less water than I stated. We have been counting the rations since Pontevedra according to a wrong number."

Inés looked at her for a long time. The wind tore a strand of hair across her face.

"How much?"

"A hundred and eighty litres. And the longer we sail by that number, the more it will be."

"Right." Inés nodded to the man beside her, who took the helm. "Come down."

In the cabin she stood over the chart and was silent. Lumen waited for an explosion and did not get one.

"Do you know what a bad record-keeper would have done? Corrected it quietly. What would an average one have done? Admitted it only once something had happened. You came before something happened. That is not praise. That is a statement." Inés tapped the chart. "So: the plan changes. We head for the nearest port where water can be replenished, and that is La Palma. We will not push ourselves to meet a deadline, we will not save on water to keep it. Lives before the plan."

"We will be delayed."

"We will be delayed. And it will be in the record: who found the mistake, what it was, when, and what we did. Not a deletion, an addition. The date of the correction, the original number, the correct number, your name."

"So that it looks as though I ruined it?"

"So that it is visible that we can read that record and trust it. That is the gift I want from you. Not flawlessness. That when you get it wrong, it will show."

That night Lumen wrote in the binder the first sentence that was not certain: *I got it wrong. Here is how.*

They reached La Palma at dawn. The hill above the town was covered with volcanic sand, and people here, as one girl with curly hair put it, "had learned to live on a mountain that can always change its mind". No one invited them into their sanctuary, only into the kitchen. It smelled of cumin and fresh bread and no one asked why they were tired. They filled the tanks, mended a torn sail, and Lumen scraped potatoes for three days and listened to arguments about the price of water. For the first time in her life she accepted help without having to earn it. In the evening, over a pot of soup, she remembered an image from a page of MariaCaminho: María de las Nieves in a golden mantle with a child who stands alone — the Little Prince, the child who once sailed from a sea land to Prague and whom her country has cherished for four centuries. It was only a drawing in her own book, not a statue nor a proof, and yet it warmed her.

## III. The sea overland

From La Palma a crossing of the Atlantic awaited the crew. It took three weeks. During it Lumen stopped being sick and began to look: at dolphins, at white birds, at the evening sea that turned the colour of a ripe plum. Only at its end did they land on the Caribbean side of Costa Rica, where people with long tables and a question Lumen had not expected were waiting for them.

"What are you carrying, and whose is it?"

Between the two oceans lies a country. Ships do not cross here; one crosses the isthmus on land and the crew carries what it can carry or what the hosts allow it to transport. No canal runs through Costa Rica, and the old Camino de Cruces trail that Lumen had read about in winter lies in Panama, a little further on. Here it was a matter of other paths, worn by other people.

The girl who was called Marisol and who led the handover laid the boxes from the ship on the table and opened them one by one.

"Seeds. From where?"

"From Sabacheira and from Pontevedra. We received them in exchange."

"Do you have that on paper?"

Lumen had papers for everything, except for one bag from Pontevedra marked only with a note: *from Xoán, for other gardens*. No record of origin, no agreement.

"This does not pass," said Marisol calmly. "And if there were more like it, nothing that lacks a document would pass."

"They are ordinary beans."

"Perhaps. I don't know from whom. I don't know whether that person was allowed to pass them on, or whether they are part of something belonging to a community that grew them two generations ago. We suspect no one here. We only ask."

Lumen felt her ears redden. She wanted to say that it was a bag of beans. That Xoán is a good man. That asking about every seed is pedantry.

Then she remembered the soil.

"I don't have it," she said. "I will write to Xoán. We will wait."

They waited four days. The answer came: yes, the beans are from his sister's field, the sister is glad to give them, and the record even included the name of her field. Lumen breathed out.

Marisol nodded and then refused.

"The origin is explained. That is good. But seeds across borders need more than a sister's good will. They need the relevant phytosanitary documents and also permits for the countries into which you want to bring them. You do not have that and will not get it in four days. The bag goes no further."

"But the rest..."

"The rest of your cargo has documents and has passed. This has not. You took it without ill intent and without a permit, and the latter decides. A donor's consent is not a permit to transport across a border." Marisol picked up a small round stamp. "I will write it truthfully. Not *passed*. Not *failed*."

In the protocol she wrote: *origin explained, donor's consent documented; transport suspended, permit missing.* And beneath it she pressed the stamp. Lumen stared at that line for a long time. It was the first record in her life that said neither yes nor no, and yet was precise.

"What happens to the bag?"

"It stays under the control of the handover point. It will go back or to an official solution. Nothing will be sown and nothing moved anywhere. If it ever comes through the proper channel, that will be another story."

So into the Pacific went only a copy of the record and a note with the name of the field — a label without a seed. Lumen put it in her binder. It stayed there as a trace of something that did not arrive, and that taught her more than anything that did.

On the last day the long column walked: the old trail over the ridge, with mules and rucksacks, the load divided among everyone, including those who had never sailed on a ship. Lumen carried a crate of tools. In the evening she wrote in the binder: *Relationships are not cargo.* She crossed it out, because it sounded like wisdom from a calendar. Then she wrote it again, in smaller letters, because it was true.

## IV. White night

The second ship was called NOSSA SENHORA DE FÁTIMA and it was not like the first. Two white hulls joined by a crossbeam like two palms laid together, a light deck, panels spread like scales. The captain, a short woman named Teura with fine wrinkles around her eyes, spoke little and held the helm the way one holds a child.

On the dry wall of the common cabin a white orchid was painted. No one had picked it; it recalled a flower that can be admired without having to be owned.

"An old book says that heaven and earth are married," Teura explained when she noticed Lumen's gaze. "A mystical wedding. I take it as an image. A union that binds no one, that takes no freedom. No new revelation. A book, an image, an orchid on wood, and that is all."

"It is not about faith?"

"To believe or not to believe is each person's business. We are about the ship."

Twelve days out from the coast of Costa Rica, Lumen learned what nothing is. The Pacific is large in that there is nothing in it but itself. Water, sky, and between them the ship's shadow trailing behind the twin hull. On the fourth night it clouded over without a moon. The stars did not appear, only a warm and silent dark. Teura dimmed all the lights in the cabins but left the position and navigation lights burning as the regulations require, and on the bow, in a well-secured glass case, she lit a small LED lantern.

"I always leave it there," she said when Lumen sat down beside her. "Not for ships. They can see without it. For us."

"Why?"

"In the dark it is hard to tell what is from what could be. The lamp is that difference. Whoever looks at it knows that they still have something to stand on."

Lumen gazed at the small quiet point of light. It made sense to her the way things make sense to children when they point at something adults have decided not to see. At that moment she realised that after days of silence she had stopped asking *How do I recognise the truth?* and was asking only *What do I see now?* That was a different question, a smaller one. It could be answered.

"In two days the wind will worsen," said Teura. "It will be unpleasant, not dangerous. But I want you to know that if need be, I will change course. It may look like a retreat. Will you write it down?"

"I will write down what happens."

"You will write down why as well." Teura looked at her properly for the first time. "Do you know what is interesting about you? You ask like a person who wants to hold the journey in their hand. And a journey cannot be held in a hand. But it can be listened to. It is the same effort, only in another direction."

Lumen wrote in the binder: *To listen is not the same as to believe. It comes earlier.*

* * *

They reached Raiatea at dawn, when the lagoon was still grey. The harbour was ordinary, with a pier and a little shop where sweet water and bread could be bought. On the beach behind it stood a few people; no one waved. Beyond the harbour the gardens and houses began, private and closed, and Teura let no one go further than the paved path to the shop.

"We buy water in the harbour," she said. "No one has invited us into the community. It is their home; we sail on."

Lumen wanted to say that the books write about a place where something beautiful has been grown for years. That she would like to see the gardens. That a journey must lead somewhere.

She stood on the deck and looked at the shore, where children were playing with a ball beside the shop. She did not want to look like a visitor. She wrote: *Raiatea, water bought in the harbour. No one invited us.* Then she wrote the words *it is all right*, crossed them out and left them crossed out. They were not hers.

## V. No

West of Raiatea the Pacific was long and tedious, and then, one morning, a coast came that stretched along the horizon like one long, even breath. Australia. Teura anchored in a bay to ride out the wind, and in the evening spread out on the cabin table a large paper chart with the route marked on it.

"Here we are," she said, and put her finger on the coast. "From here we go on west."

Lumen leaned over the table. Her gaze stopped in the middle of the continent, there where nothing happened on the chart: no town, no harbour. Only one name.

"Uluru," she said.

"Deep inland. It is not a harbour."

"I have a whole paragraph in my notes. I read who looks after that place and how they speak of it." Lumen bit her lip. "I thought we would go."

"They have not invited us."

"No. But we could try. Write..."

"To whom?" Teura did not say it sharply. She said it as a question. "And about what? About a visit we want for ourselves? A letter that those who look after that place would have to feel obliged to answer?"

Lumen was silent.

"If they ever invite us, we will go overland, on their terms, with what they say. Not before. As long as no one invites us, the journey is only a line on a chart, and a line is better not crossed."

"That is hard," said Lumen after a while.

"It is. The hardest thing is not overcoming fear. Anyone can do that. The hardest thing is to say *no* to yourself when no one is stopping you and you could." Teura spread the chart out. "For me that is one of the initiations. The later ones tend to be worse."

Lumen laughed briefly, with one sharp corner of her mouth. Then she ran her finger over the chart and let her hand come to rest on the bay.

That night she copied nothing from the notes about Uluru. She left them as they were, and for the first time it did not bother her that they would remain unused.

## VI. The golden ship

From Sri Lanka, where they spent a week on the island of Bodhi Lanka among mango trees and broken boats, a white ship sailed into the harbour at Chennai. Lumen stood on the bow and looked at the yellow-brown line of a coast rising out of the heat.

From there they continued overland into the interior, in a small group of six, to the physically existing Ekam, where in this imagining visitors may come. No one was waiting for them and no one had invited them other than as other guests. They entered a marble hall without columns, where they sat on the floor among many other people to whom no one explained anything. On one wall a temple symbol shone gold, its shape something Lumen could not grasp — and perhaps did not want to.

She sat at the back, closed her eyes and waited.

She waited for what she had expected after so many years of questions: an answer. Satori, clarity, stillness. At least a single moment when the stone in her pocket would dissolve.

Nothing came. It did not come after an hour, it did not come after two. Her legs ached and she found in herself only what she already knew: restlessness, a little pride and a great need to write it down.

When she went outside she stood for a while in the paved courtyard and did not know what to do with her hands. Then she came back down to earth to the others and helped roll up the mats. That was all.

In the evening, back in the harbour at Chennai, she sat on the pier and looked at the water. The captain Anjali sat down beside her — a slender woman with a deep voice, who was to take over the third ship.

"Disappointed?"

"More ashamed. I expected... I don't know what I expected."

"Most people expect something."

"I told myself that when a person goes so far, something must give them confirmation. That someone will look and say: you have it."

Anjali laughed, quietly. "And who would say it? Me? You yourself? None of us has such a stamp. Whoever issues it is lying. Even to themselves." She scratched her chin. "I would say that if it is real, it is recognised differently. By the fact that you then carry water and tell no one."

"That is a platitude."

"It is. Forgive me." Anjali smiled. "Then write it down as: *I sat, nothing happened, I helped tidy up.* That is also true."

Lumen wrote it down.

The third ship waited at the quay. Sails and a narrow golden stripe along the side caught the last light. MARÍA DE LAS NIEVES. It was to sail the next day; the last coat of varnish was still drying on the bow. On a smooth plank someone had fixed a child's drawing of a standing child with a lantern — and above its head the draughtsman had added a small crown and into its palm a sphere, as though he knew about the Little Prince. No one knew who had drawn it, and Anjali said she would leave it hanging there.

"I am leaving it," she said. "A child who stands alone and holds a light. It reminds me that anyone can stand up and go, and that no one has to be carried."

## VII. The call

After a week the Indian Ocean was calm as a blackboard. On the eighth day something began that could not be read off a chart: the air thickened, the clouds assembled into grey masonry and the wind roared low over the water. The crew silently took down the sails, lashed loose objects, cleared the decks.

In the evening of that day an emergency signal sounded.

The radio operator, a thin boy named Tomás, went pale. "Captain. We have picked up a distress call. A fishing boat reports a damaged engine and water in the hull. She is far, but within range of our report."

In the cabin there was silence.

Lumen looked at Anjali. She knew the decision was not hers and that she would not have known what to do. She had not been trained for it, nor was it her place.

"Report it to the rescue coordination of the relevant area," said Anjali calmly. "Precisely: our position, the position of those calling, the state of the sea, and what we know about our ship. No enthusiasm. Facts."

"Yes, captain."

"Then we tell the coordinators what we can: we are nearby and have a crew and equipment, but in this weather we cannot safely approach the damaged boat. They will coordinate the available help. We stay where they need us, only as long as it is safe."

"We are not going there?"

"If coordination asks us to and it is safe, yes. If not, no." Anjali gripped the edge of the table. "I am not here so that things get written about us. I am here to bring everyone home, including people I do not yet know."

Then followed hours that cannot be told in order. The sea rose and fell, people on deck held on to ropes, the radio crackled with the voice of a foreign language and of a coordinator in which there was nothing heroic, only long, calm sentences. The ship circled in long arcs, held position according to instructions, shone a searchlight into the dark until the dark answered nothing. Lumen, braced against the wall in her cabin, wrote. *Signal received. Coordination informed. Instruction: hold position. None of us can see anything.* Once, when the ship slid sharply, she raised her pen and stopped. What if someone said this was great material for a report? What if someone wanted a photograph to show how brave they had been? No one would say it aloud. But she felt it, like a temptation in her pocket.

She wrote nothing extra. Only what was.

In the morning, when the sea eased into long swells, a message came from coordination: the nearest other vessel had reached those calling and taken the people on board. How many there were and how they were, coordination did not say. Anjali closed her eyes. "Good," she said. "Thank you."

Lumen wrote it down precisely: *We were in the area. We did not manage to reach them. Another vessel took them on board; we do not know their condition. Our role: report, position, testimony, holding position according to instructions.*

Anjali read the entry and was silent for a long time.

"I would like something more," she said then. "I ask myself whether I could have gone closer. And then I tell myself that if I had gone, I would then have had to rescue myself as well." She shook her head. "Write it as it is: *I decided according to what we knew. Another vessel took the people on board; we do not know their condition. It could have turned out differently.*"

She wrote it exactly like that.

## VIII. The cape

They reached Cape Agulhas at daybreak. The cape was lower than Lumen had expected, without drama: a low rock, a lighthouse, white foam on the stones. It is precisely here that, according to the charts, two oceans meet — not at the famous Cape of Good Hope, but here, at the southern tip of Africa.

On the water before them a red ship was waiting.

Santa María la Mayor, weathered, with new patches in her sails, and on the bow Inés Varela, who raised a hand like a person who had waited a long time.

The meeting was short. The two captains shook hands and exchanged weather reports and documents. Anjali took Lumen aside.

"Here our ways part. You go back to the red one. Not because you have finished your task, but because the journey turns and you are to carry it home."

"I would like to stay."

"Do you know why you will not?"

Lumen thought for a long time. "Because I no longer need another ocean. I need to bring back what I learned."

"That is a better answer than I expected."

Inés stood on the bow of the red ship and marvelled: "I hardly recognised you. You have hands like mine."

Lumen looked at her palms. They were calloused, with dark creases and a scar from a rope on her wrist. "My binder got soaked in the Indian Ocean," she said. "In the storm it fell into the flooded hold. The notes have run, half of them unreadable."

Inés looked at her. "And the ship's log?"

"That is fine. We have it in a safe copy, as one does. Only mine was destroyed."

"And now?"

"I fill in what I do not know from the people who were there. Where that is not possible, I write: *gap, I do not know.*"

Inés nodded. "That is better than invented completeness. A record that admits a hole is more honest than a record that pretends to be whole."

* * *

The voyage home took six weeks. They stopped at St Helena, where the local fishermen let them replenish water but wanted nothing more than that, and at the Azores they met another ship whose crew laughed and played the guitar. Lumen learned to keep the night watch, to read the sky and, above all, to be silent.

In Pontevedra, in August of the following year, a test awaited her that she had not known about.

Xoán led her into the garden, now full of ripe tomatoes, and handed her a spade.

"Dig up a row of potatoes. All of them. And then sort them."

She dug for two hours. Sweat ran down her back. The potatoes were small, some rotten, others as large as a fist. She sorted them into three crates: good, half-and-half, lost.

Xoán glanced into the crates and said: "Good."

"That is all?"

"That is all. Now you know what is finished and what is not."

In the evening captain Inés handed her an envelope. Inside was a sheet of paper with the ship's stamp and a few lines: *Lumen, record-keeper. According to the shared ship records: Pontevedra – Lisbon (Sabacheira) – La Palma – (overland across the isthmus) – Pacific – Sri Lanka – Chennai – Indian Ocean – Cape Agulhas – Pontevedra. I confirm only what I know from the records that reached my hands and from what I saw myself. I do not speak for other crews.* And lower down, in different handwriting: *The accounts are open. You may leave whenever you wish.*

"Is it a certificate?"

"It is a letter. You may leave it here, you may burn it, you may keep it. No one will appeal to it."

Lumen was silent for a long time. Then she folded the sheet and put it in her pocket.

She left Pontevedra at the end of September by train and then by road, and one late afternoon she got off in Bohemia, where the land sloped down to the river and the leaves were turning rust. The house stood as she had left it. In the yard a garden overgrown with weeds was growing. She set down her backpack, took off her boots and knelt in the soil.

## IX. We do not know

In the village she returned to, people met on the meadow behind the school, where a wooden building stood with a roof of solar shingles and inside it a repair workshop, where broken things were brought and where people learned how to mend them. In it hung a sheet of principles, written in pencil so that it could be changed, and beside it a book of open accounts that anyone could look into. Tomorrow there was to be a meeting about the well. Whoever disagreed with a proposal could raise an objection and the proposal changed until a solution was found that could be lived with; whoever wanted to could also leave, and no one judged them for it. The accounts were public, without personal details, and no one sat over them as an authority.

In March of the following year Lumen held an evening for the children on the meadow. No ritual, no lectures. Only a blanket and tea.

The night was clear. Above the ridge the sky opened so deep that one could get lost in it.

A girl with thin fair hair, perhaps nine years old, pointed. "What is that over there?"

Lumen narrowed her eyes. "That is Vega. Or perhaps Arcturus. I don't remember."

"You don't know?"

"I don't know. But we will look." She took a star chart out of her bag, lit a small lamp, and put constellations together with the children, running a finger from star to star. In the end they agreed: Vega.

"And up there?" asked a boy. "I heard that people will live up there."

"No one lives on Issobella yet, because that station does not exist," said Lumen. "Other stations up there do, and people live on them. Issobella is a question that scientists are working on. Whether it will ever come to be, I don't know."

"Then why do you talk about it?"

Lumen laughed. "Because first we have to take care of the land down here. Of the water, of the garden, of one another. Whoever cannot manage that will build nothing good up there."

The boy nodded, as though she had said the most natural thing in the world.

Then she took from her pocket a folded sheet and the children peered over her shoulder. She read them Inés's letter, and at the end, beneath Inés's line, she added her own in pencil: *I correct: I was not honest. I was willing to admit mistakes. The rest I am still learning.*

"Can you write it like that?" asked the girl.

"I can. It is my record. And whoever finds a mistake may correct it too."

"I would write there that you were also kind."

"Not in this letter — it has to stand up to people who do not know me. But in your own notebook you may write it. It is your testimony and you may sign your name under it."

The girl took a notebook out of her pocket and wrote: *I saw that Lumen helped us.* She signed it and then looked at Lumen proudly.

Lumen smiled. Then she raised her eyes.

Above them the stars were shining. The light of many had travelled towards us for a long time, and yet it was here. One of them, the smallest, wavered above the horizon like a lamp in the hand of someone who is not afraid of the dark. She did not know what it was. But she knew that she could ask, and that no one would be finished as long as they kept asking.

The children rose one by one. One went home across the meadow, another up to the ridge, another to the bench where the star chart could be read again. Each went their own way and Lumen let them go.
