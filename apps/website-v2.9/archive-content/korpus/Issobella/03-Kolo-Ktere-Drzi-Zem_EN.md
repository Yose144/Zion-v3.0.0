## The Story

A pilgrim who has long sailed the sea knows the night by the stars and the day by the weight of his legs. So went an old saying. But what if a person leaves even the sea — and with it the weight?

In the camp of the printers of truth a model hung on a string: a ring of lighter wood, fixed by threads to a rod in the middle. When it was spun, the little sticks on its inner side leaned outward, as though something were holding them from below.

*"This is how we hold the Earth when we leave it,"* said the elder of the apprentices. *"Not by magic. By rotation."*

He explained how they calculate it. A hundred metres from the centre, almost two revolutions a minute — and a foot on the floor of the ring feels about a third of Earth's weight. *"Not full. A third — as on Mars."*

*"Why not full?"* asked the carpenter, who always wanted to know how much of anything it can carry.

*"Because a wheel that turns fast pulls at your head. You feel a wind you cannot see; whatever you throw flies askew. You get used to it — but the body does not get used to it for free. That is why we choose a slow wheel. Two revolutions a minute do not take your breath away — and they still hold your bones together."*

*"And when I need more?"*

*"Then you go to the small wheel. At the centre of the station stands a second one — smaller, faster — entered the way one enters a sauna. Twenty minutes a day of full Earth weight. The crew goes there to exercise and come back stronger. Whoever neglects it comes home with bones like a swallow's."*

The pilgrims held the model in their hands for a while and passed it around. It was light — but when it turned, it held like a stone.

*"And remember,"* added the elder, *"the most important thing is how the wheel starts up. From rest to one revolution a minute takes it a day. In space, haste is more expensive than fuel."*

## What it means

**Artificial gravity is the first "ecology" of Issobella.** Bodhi Gaia learns to live with the soil; Issobella learns to live **without it** — and therefore must manufacture gravity. The only known path that needs no miracle is rotation: centrifugal acceleration in a large, slow torus.

From the research bundle `L6data/Umela_Gravitace.md` come concrete choices that are not random:

- **0.38 g as home.** Not full Earth weight — roughly Martian. Enough that the body does not drift away, and at the same time a preparation for worlds where gravity is lower.
- **200 m diameter, ~2 RPM.** A larger ring = lower revolutions = fewer Coriolis effects and less gradient between head and feet. Slowness is a property, not a compromise.
- **An exercise centrifuge in the middle.** Short doses of full g (1.0 g, ~20 min a day) as a "vitamin" for the skeleton.
- **Spin-up over 24 hours.** The crew adapts gradually; even a machine learns to become a home slowly.

Three construction variants (a large torus, a tether centrifuge, a hybrid) are laid out in the bundle as open solutions — and the book takes from them an image, not a commitment: **Issobella remembers that gravity is a service, not an ornament**.
