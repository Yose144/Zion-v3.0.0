# PROLOGUE — The table with no head

*The Circle of Goddesses · the revealed future · all seven worlds at once*

---

First there was a table.

Not a throne — thrones belong to Babylon, and Babylon lies on the bottom of the flood. A table. Round, as is proper, so that no one sits on its head. The carpenter built it — the same one who once planed the keel of the ark and carved lanterns from its cedar — and when it was finished he bored no name into its wood. A table should have no owner. It should have guests.

And around the table there were seven chairs. Six for the earth, which someone holds, and the seventh for the sky, which holds all.

---

## They came one after another

**The first to come was a woman with clay in her palms.** She arrived from the west, from the sea where Europe ends and where the slope above the Atlantic smells of spruces and salt. Her hair was stuck together with dust, for she had worked the whole way — by the road there remained the cut of her hoe, from which you will remember only this much: that hands matter to her more than a name. On the table she laid a handful of clay from her garden and one seed that she had swallowed for so long that she saw morning in the dark through it.

*"I have brought the only thing I have,"* she said. *"Clay in which something sleeps. Whoever plants, administers. I know no more."*

She sat down where the sunset falls. They called her **the Daughter of Clay** — and she did not yawn at that name, because clay is the truth you carry under your nails, not a title.

**The second to come was a woman with a coal in a shell.** She had travelled longer than the others — across the ocean from the island where the volcano breathes through the night and where a silence hangs over the crater that is not born, only learned. She held the coal in the shell as carefully as one holds the last fire before winter: she did not own it, she **carried** it. When she laid it on the table, the warmth came from it, but not the flame. The flame she left at home, beneath the seven domes that will one day stand there — and by her seat she set one more well, a smaller one, closed, which she shared with no one and which was nevertheless not hers.

*"This well is for everyone,"* she said. *"I only sit by it. To hold, and not touch — that is my whole work."*

She sat down between the west and the south. They called her **the Keeper of Fire and Silence**.

**The third to come was a woman out of the water.** Her hair still dripped salt, even though the journey from the lagoon had taken months. She was born where the stars learn to be a map and the waves learn to be a speech — in places where the old ones say that a ship finds the shore when the crew serves the direction and not the helmsman. On the table she laid a knot — not a knot of rope, a knot of **direction**: wavy lines in the way the old seafarers bound reeds together so that they could see where an island lies that you cannot yet see.

*"I do not read maps,"* she said. *"I read what the water does when it thinks no one is listening. The lagoon is a navel — and a navel is never owned."*

She sat down to the south. They called her **the Daughter of the Lagoon**.

**The fourth to come was a woman with a circle of salt.** From the middle of the land, where pilgrims meet beneath a mountain that is not the highest but is the first — a mountain by which people stop, because where a circle gathers, no throne may stand. In her palm she carried a circle drawn on linen, and into the centre of the circle she poured salt — the same salt once given into the bread of the pilgrims on the first evening by the fire.

*"I do not carry land,"* she said. *"Land is carried foolishly. I carry the way it is decided about — and the rule that one person never decides."*

She sat down to the east, where the sun rises. They called her **the Keeper of the Circle**.

**The fifth to come was a woman with a leaf.** She had travelled furthest of all — from the island where a tree stands so old that empires remember only its shadow. She did not break the leaf off; the tree itself set the leaf aside for her when she had sat beneath it so long that the tree stopped distinguishing where the tree ended and where the one listening began. She laid the leaf on the table and the leaf was green even though detached — because a tree that outlives empires does not hurry to wash its leaves for death.

*"People sat beneath my tree when Babylon did not even exist,"* she said. *"I learned one thing: what outlives an empire is not an institution. It is a tree. That is why we plant."*

She sat down to the north-east. They called her **the Keeper of the Tree**.

**The sixth chair remained empty.**

No one sat on it — and yet the circle laid a lamp on it. A small lantern, of the ark's cedar, of the kind carried by the child who is said to be not yet born. The chair pointed to the place where two continents kiss — to the isthmus, where the oceans do not meet directly, but where people walk to answer the oceans. There, the old ones said, a bridge must stand. And a bridge is not owned; a bridge is crossed.

*"Why does no one carry that lamp?"* asked the Daughter of Clay at last.

And the Keeper of Fire answered for all: *"Because she who comes will take it herself. A seat is not entrusted by those who want to sit — it is entrusted by those who walk the path. She Who Comes has not yet finished the path. We keep the place warm, not occupied."*

**The seventh came last — and did not sit down at once.**

She stood by the circle as though waiting for all to be seated, and then she did what none before her had: she looked upwards. She was born by the same water as the Daughter of the Lagoon — they say the same wave cast them into different bays — but her waves went upwards. She surfed on the crests that break towards the sky and learned to read what the sky says about the earth. On the table she laid a mirror — small, smooth, in which light was reflected by day and stars by night, and the mirror showed where a finger cannot reach.

*"I do not hold a seat on the earth,"* she said quietly. *"My world hangs higher — but I am yours. The Lagoon taught me to read water. I will teach her to read stars."*

She sat down — and as soon as she sat, the seventh point of the circle closed. They called her **She Who Holds the Sky**.

---

## And then the child appeared

She stood at the edge of the light, barefoot, with a lantern of the ark's cedar. They all knew her — not by name, but by the way the light behaved around her: a lantern that shows two things, what is and what could be.

The child looked at the table for a long time. Seven chairs. One lamp on an empty one. Five women and one who holds the sky, and none sitting at the head of the table.

Then she asked the question children ask, and children are the only beings who may ask it aloud:

*"Who rules here?"*

And the circle answered — not with one voice but with six, woven together like the waves the Lagoon can read:

*"No one. We hold here."*

*"To rule is the work of thrones — and we drowned thrones in the flood. We hold: clay, fire, water, circle, tree, bridge and sky. We hold until the soil calls. And when she who comes appears, the seat will be warm — not sold."*

The child nodded, as one nods when the answer does not taste like a lesson but like bread. Then she laid her lantern on the empty seat beside the lamp — and the lights came together.

And the circle remained seated. For that is what this whole book is about: **not how a circle takes power — but how a seat is held while the world calls for care.**
