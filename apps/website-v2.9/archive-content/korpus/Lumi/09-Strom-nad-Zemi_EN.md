# LUMI — Chapter 9: The tree above the Earth
## Korma · L6 — the hand that waters, and only then the window with the Earth; the slow wheel, the schedule of the body, and the inheritance of stations

> *"Even here someone has to water every day."*

---

## The Story

First she saw the hand.

An old hand with earth under its fingers held a tin watering can and poured water to the roots of a small plant in a clay vessel. The water soaked in slowly, as everywhere. The plant had five leaves and a sixth was unfolding.

Only then did Lumi raise her eyes and see behind the hand a window — and behind the window, below her, a blue-white arc turning endlessly slowly. The Earth.

It was not a place from the songs. It was a ring. A wide corridor that curved up before her and behind her and ended nowhere; the floor beneath her feet held, but differently — more lightly, as though Lumi weighed a third of herself. People worked here, ate, repaired, slept, argued and laughed. Along the outer wall ran a long band of green behind glass.

*"Slowly,"* said the man who caught her trying to run. They called him Korma and he was a navigator. *"One does not run here. Whoever throws a ball throws it askew. Whoever breaks into a run falls somewhere other than they expect. The wheel turns and you turn with it."*

*"Why does it turn?"*

*"Because there is no Earth under your feet here. So we make one. Not by magic — by rotation. The ring is two hundred metres across and turns twice a minute. Enough that you do not float away. Little enough that it does not pull at your head."*

*"And why not full weight, as at home?"*

*"Because a wheel that turns fast hurts. You feel a wind you cannot see; the body gets used to it, but not for free. So we chose a slow wheel and a third of the weight. And when someone needs more, they go into the little wheel in the middle — there it is full Earth for twenty minutes a day, like a sauna. Whoever neglects it goes home with bones like a swallow's."*

---

On the wall where elsewhere a menu would hang, there was a schedule. Not a duty roster. **A schedule of the body.** Half an hour against resistance, half an hour of walking, twenty minutes in the little wheel, a quarter of an hour of stretching. Every day.

*"Not because you are supposed to be strong,"* said Korma. *"Because your bones forget that they are supposed to be bones."*

He showed her the cabins — small, each with a door that others opened only when called. *"What you think is your own. What you say is the crew's. What you do is the ship's. Whoever has nowhere to go alone goes after the others."* And the common zone, where every evening people ate together and where the week was counted even though there was no Sunday there. *"In a closed world, time without celebration dissolves like salt in water."*

At last he led her into a long gallery. On the walls hung pictures of old ships that had once orbited the Earth: a small tin station with one window, a larger one with great wings, a long construction of modules that two quarrelling nations assembled together and that outlasted their quarrel.

*"These are our ancestors,"* said Korma. *"We learn from them what to copy — and what never. They taught us that it is possible to live up there. And that people up there do not die of a valve. They die of having stopped talking to each other."*

---

They returned to the window with the tree. The plant in the vessel — only now did Lumi notice that beside it stood a larger one, and behind it a larger still, up to a young tree whose crown touched the ceiling of the corridor.

*"Why do you have a tree? You cannot eat it."*

*"No. But it is proof that something other than us can grow. On the old stations they said they talked to the plants more than to the control system. A plant never asks when the next ship arrives."*

Lumi looked at the tree and at the Earth behind it. And she understood a thing that she then said aloud and that is not repeated in the book, because it need not be:

*"Even here someone has to water every day."*

Korma handed her the watering can without a word.

---

The lantern showed today.

The light narrowed and the ring vanished. Lumi saw what of the tree above the Earth actually exists in 2026: **nothing up there.** No module, no wheel, no crew. Down on the Earth lay a bundle of papers — the architecture of the station with its orbital altitude and power input, the calculations why two hundred metres and why two revolutions, a table of exercise, a list of findings from the old stations and from experimental closed stays on Earth — and that bundle was publicly readable so that anyone could criticise it. She saw a web page with a picture of a ring and a roadmap headed *vision*. And she saw that no one has yet selected a single crew member, because there is nowhere to send them.

*"So the watering can is the only true thing,"* she said.

*"The watering can and the papers,"* said Korma. *"Papers can be read. A watering can can be lifted. The rest is a promise."*

---

## What it means

**Issobella is learning to live without soil — and therefore must manufacture gravity and raise a crew. But the chapter begins with a hand holding a watering can, not with a ring, because even the most distant home needs the same small acts as the garden below.** The Book of the Sky described the wheel (ch. 3), the crew (ch. 4) and the inheritance of stations (ch. 5). This book assembles them into a single walk and adds a single sentence — Lumi's.

1. **Gravity as a service, not an ornament.** The research bundle chooses 0.38 g (roughly Martian weight) in a torus about 200 m in diameter at ~2 revolutions per minute: a larger ring = lower rotation = smaller Coriolis effects and a smaller difference between head and feet. Short doses of full 1 g in a small centrifuge (~20 minutes a day) as a "vitamin" for the skeleton. Spin-up from rest over 24 hours so that the crew adapts gradually.
2. **The crew is an agreement of the perfectible.** The schedule of the body, privacy as infrastructure (a cabin opened only on invitation; psychological data protected), rituals of common time. Analogue missions on Earth have shown that isolation breaks people first in small things — in noise, in smell, in the lack of room for solitude.
3. **The inheritance of stations.** Salyut, Skylab, Mir, the ISS, Freedom and Dual Keel are real history, from which one learns what to copy and what never. Issobella is not a successor to any of these programmes; it draws lessons, not an inheritance.
4. **Plants as a bridge L5 ↔ L6.** The greenhouse on the ring is in the book the same thing as Hanuman's bucket in the garden: care that is recognised only at the root. A plant does not ask when the next ship arrives.
5. **There is nothing up there today — and the book says so first.** There is a public research bundle, a web page with a roadmap marked as a vision, and a canonical service that down on Earth counts how many units from each block rise into the fund of the sky (ch. 10). That is all. It is little — and it is exact.
