# THE ROSE STORY — IX. Both shores
## Lumi returns to the river — and knows the first rose not by its body, but by the manner it taught the garden

Returning took longer than leaving.

Lumi walked through worlds — she did not count them — and the lantern was carried across all of them the same way: to show what is, and what could be. The seed in the vessel she planted far from here, by other water, where it grew and where a garden may now be fragrant that this book does not know about. And then one day, which differed from the others in nothing but direction, she walked to the river again.

She recognised the gate from afar — and did not recognise the garden, because the garden was no longer by the gate. The garden was the gate. Roses grew on both banks of the river — on the one where Sarah had once loosened the soil, and on the opposite one, where the water had carried the seeds when no one was watching.

The first flower might no longer be there. Lumi did not know and it could not be known — roses do not grow with little labels. Perhaps the oldest bush grew from her root, perhaps from her seed, perhaps from the seed of a long-ago descendant. Flowers do not count years; they count how many times they have bloomed.

Lumi laid by the oldest roots the alabaster vessel, which she had brought back empty. It was not a relic nor a marker of the first place. It was space for what would come next.

Lumi bent to the oldest rose and waited — for what, she did not know.

"Do you know me?" she asked at last.

The flower leaned — and in that leaning there was something Lumi recognised before she understood what.

"I do not know whether I remember your face," she said quietly. "But I know your manner. You ask before you touch. You water, but you do not sell. You let go — and therefore someone can return freely."

And then she saw what the garden really does. People came through the gate — and no one locked the gate, because no one wanted to lock it any more. Children carried water in jugs, but first they asked whether the flower wanted it. The merchant — the same one, or another who had learned the same — stood by the river with a little bottle turned upside down and simply smelled. And when someone came with scissors, the garden did not have to defend itself: it had taught people to ask on their own.

"You taught them," said Lumi.

"I taught one," said the Rose — or all of them at once. "And that one taught another. That is how gardens grow: not by the flower multiplying, but by the manner being passed on."

Lumi sat by the river until evening and watched how both banks held the same flowers — not the same as copies, but the same as a sentence spoken in two voices. And she understood that the first Rose does not live in any single flower. She lives in the manner — in someone asking before touching, in caring without owning, in water belonging to the thirsty and not to the one who offers the most, in letting go without exacting a return.

The Rose was not the only one. She was the first that someone cared for without appropriating — and therefore a garden could grow from her.
