# THE ROSE STORY — VII. A night without witnesses
## Drought comes quietly — and the flower learns to care for the one who cares

Then came the drought.

Not a storm — drought comes more quietly. First the grass stopped being soft. Then the river drew back a step from the bank, imperceptibly, like a guest slowly leaving the table. And the earth at the roots hardened into a crust that repelled water instead of drinking it.

Lumi carried water in her palms — it was slow, but faithful. Then in a jug that someone had left for her by the gate. She walked from the river to the roots ten times, twenty times a day — and when people had gone and the garden fell silent, she walked on. Into the dark. At night, when no one saw her, she carried water drop by drop, because she knew that roots drink even in sleep.

The Rose saw it — flowers see well, because they have nothing else to do — and she saw what was happening to Lumi. Her steps grew shorter. Her cheeks went pale. And once, when she sat down by the river to draw water, Lumi did not draw any for herself. She forgot to drink.

"Are you drinking?" asked the Rose one evening.

"I am watering," said Lumi — and it was not an answer, and both of them knew it.

The drought went on. The Rose held — her roots were deep and her leaves could catch the dew. But Lumi was weakening, and a flower that needs a caretaker sees her exhaustion before she admits it herself.

One morning, before the sun, the Rose did something flowers do not often do: all night she gathered dew — a drop on every leaf, a smaller one on every thorn — and when Lumi bent to water her, the Rose leaned toward her and let all the water slide into Lumi's palms.

"Drink," said the Rose.

"That is for you," said Lumi. "That is your water."

"Water I hold while you are cracking does not taste good to me," said the Rose. "Drink."

And Lumi drank — and it was the first water she had remembered all that long day.

"Why did you do that?" she asked.

"Because care that goes only one way is not care," said the Rose. "It is exhaustion with a nice name. You water me — and I hold you so that you can water again tomorrow. It is no great science, Lumi. It is the exchange that keeps things alive."

"But you are suffering. The drought—"

"I am suffering," said the Rose. "But the drought will pass — and the garden stays only if the one who waters it stays too."

Lumi wanted to object — that she was here precisely in order to care — but the Rose did not let her finish.

"I do not want to bloom at the price of you drying up," said the Rose.

"Then what am I to do?"

"What I do," said the Rose. "Hold on. Drink. Sleep. And let me care too — in my own way. Did you see those drops? All night I held them for you. That is my care — different from yours, because I am a rose. But it is care. And if you refuse it, you tell me that my gift is not worth receiving."

Lumi understood — and it was a harder understanding than any of the previous ones. She had been confusing the feeling of being needed with the feeling of being loved. But there is a difference between them: need ends when it is fulfilled; love remains even when nothing is missing.

From that day Lumi carried water in two jugs — one for the garden, one for herself. And when it seemed to her that she had no time to drink because the roses needed her, she smelled the Rose and reminded herself that she too is someone's garden.

And the river flowed on — for all who bent to it. Water that nourishes is not locked into little bottles nor into reverence; it is given to the thirsty and fulfils its meaning only when it is drunk.

To be needed is not the same as to be loved — and a flower that can tell the difference truly cares.
