# THE ROSE STORY — VIII. Permission to leave
## The lantern is carried onward — and the rose you love is not held by a promise or by scissors

The lantern lit differently.

Lumi recognised it at once — the light that had all along glowed quietly near the ground rose one evening and pointed toward the gate like a finger showing the way onward. It was time to go. The lantern is carried onward; that was its whole meaning.

Lumi came to the Rose with legs as heavy as a child should never have.

"I have to go," she said.

"I know," said the Rose. "The wind told me this morning — and the thorns straightened, because even they feel when something is coming to an end."

They sat together for a long time — so long that the river managed to change its voice and bring it back again.

"I am afraid," Lumi admitted at last. "When I leave, what if you dry up? What if someone comes who will pick you, and I am not here?"

"And what if you do not leave," said the Rose, "and stay — not because you want to, but because you are afraid? Then you would be holding me as fear, not as care. And I would be blooming in a guardhouse, not in a garden."

Lumi played with a petal the wind had brought her — fallen, soft, no longer on the flower.

"If I leave, will I abandon you?" asked Lumi.

"Only if you stopped being who you are because of me," answered the Rose.

Lumi fell silent. Because that was all of it: abandonment is not in the legs, but in the light. If she stayed and the lantern went out — if she stopped carrying what she carries — she would be beside the Rose, and yet she would have abandoned it, because she would have abandoned herself.

"Promise me that I will come back," said Lumi — and at once she recognised what she had said.

"No," said the Rose quietly. "A promise of return would hold you like a rope. I do not hold you. If you return, you will return freely — and it will be joy. If you do not return, you will still be the one I knew — and the garden must not stand in your way."

"And you?" said Lumi. "Should I take you with me? Pick you and carry you, so that I have you always?"

The Rose trembled — not with fear, but with the importance of the question.

"A flower you pick so that it stays with you stays only until it withers," she said. "And then you carry the dead body of what you loved. Leave me in the earth, Lumi. Hold me in yourself, not in your hand. A hand holds for a while — what you carry in yourself you carry always."

And then something small and great at once happened. The Rose leaned — slowly, the way flowers can — and one ripe seed, a single one, came loose and fell. Not into the soil. Into the vessel — the same alabaster vessel that had stood by the roots all along, empty and honest in its emptiness.

"Here," said the Rose. "Not me. A seed. That you may carry — and wherever you plant it, a garden will grow there that will not be this one, and yet will come from it. Seeds are not children you hold by the hand. They are letters you send ahead."

Lumi held the vessel with a single seed inside — and suddenly it did not seem empty nor full. It seemed ready.

"Do you wish me to come back?" asked Lumi.

"I wish it," said the Rose. "But a wish is not a rope. If you return — and I hope you return — you will come not as a debtor, but as a guest. A return is not a debt, Lumi. It is a possibility that stays beautiful only when no one exacts it."

In the morning Lumi left through the open gate — with the lantern in one hand and the vessel in the other. At the threshold she stopped and said into the wind what flowers can hear:

"I am not leaving you. I am carrying you."

And the gate stayed open — both ways, as gates can be when no one locks them.

A departure that honours what was is not abandonment. It is the way to carry a relationship further than a hand can reach.
