# THE ROSE STORY — V. The merchant with a scent
## A man who wanted to lock beauty into a little bottle — and learned from the flower that a scent is received, not owned

The merchant came with little bottles.

He was a decent man — he had good eyes and one bad habit: he saw everything beautiful twice, once as beauty and once as goods. At his belt he carried a row of little bottles of coloured glass, and each was waiting for something that can never properly fit inside it.

He smelled the Rose for a long time — sincerely, with his eyes closed — and when he opened them they were moist.

"I have never met a scent like this," he said. "I have travelled after scents all my life. Roses from the east, the sea after rain, wood burning in cold houses — but this smells like the memory of someone having once loved a person. I would sell it as the scent of eternal love. People would pay for it — and I would give you half, because you watch over it."

Lumi fell silent. The merchant was not wicked — and precisely that made his question hard. He did not want to hurt the Rose. He wanted to preserve its beauty — he simply did not yet understand the difference between preserving and locking away. But beauty that someone locks into a little bottle stops smelling of what it smelled of.

And then she spoke, slowly, so that it would sound like a boundary, not an insult:

"I cannot sell something that is not mine."

The merchant smiled, softly. "But you cultivate it. You water it, you watch over it. When you care for something, it is yours — that is how it works in every market in the world."

"I water it," said Lumi. "But the root is hers. The flower is hers. The scent is her gift — and a gift can be received, not sold. If I sold it, I would be selling something that belongs to another — and in markets that is called something other than trade."

"And she?" The merchant pointed at the Rose. "What if she wanted it?"

"Did you ask her?" said Lumi.

The merchant bent down — and for the first time asked the flower as though it were a person: "Would you give me your scent?"

The Rose leaned toward him, slowly, the way flowers can.

"A scent is not given, little merchant," she said. "A scent is received. I am fragrant — and you may come and smell, today, tomorrow, whenever the gate stands. But what you receive you cannot carry off as goods. When you lock a scent into a little bottle, you do not carry it away — you carry only glass and the memory that something was. And a memory is not sold; a memory is lived."

"People want to remember," said the merchant — and his voice dropped a little. "Memories vanish. In a little bottle the scent would stay forever."

"In a little bottle there stays a scent that died because you closed it in," said the Rose. "If you want eternity, come. Eternity is repetition, not ownership."

The merchant stood still. He looked at his little bottles — coloured, empty, patient — and then at the flower, which went on being fragrant, indifferent to whether anyone wrote it into their accounts.

"And when you go?" said Lumi. "When someone picks you and carries you off — what will remain for people then?"

"There will remain what makes a scent a scent," said the Rose. "That they came. That they smelled. That they did not need to own something beautiful for it to change their day."

The merchant set the little bottles down. All but one — and that one he turned upside down, like a cup that will never be filled again.

"Could I," he said slowly, "could I simply stay here and smell?"

"You can always do that," said the Rose.

And so the merchant who had come to buy a scent simply stood for the first time in his life and took in its fragrance — and he went away with nothing he could trade, and yet he left richer than he had come.

The scent he had not bought went with him as a memory.
