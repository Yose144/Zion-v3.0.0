# THE ROSE STORY — IV. The gardener of mirrors
## A man who wanted to improve the rose into symmetry — and a flower that kept its crooked petal

There came a gardener whom people remembered by his tool: the Gardener of Mirrors.

He was gentle and patient and carried with him a crate of mirrors — small, polished, adjustable on little wooden legs. He did not want to pick the rose or sell it. He wanted to improve it.

"You have a gift," he said to the Rose, and bent to it with the gravity of a connoisseur. "But a gift must be shown properly. When I set you among mirrors, you will look like three. And when I straighten that crooked petal, you will be perfect."

And he began to work. He straightened the petals between his fingers, carefully, one by one — "symmetry is respect," he said — and he placed the mirrors so that the flower was reflected from left and right, so that the sunset burned twice on the petals, so that everyone passing by saw more roses than were growing.

Lumi watched the garden change. The Rose in the mirrors was more beautiful than the Rose without them — symmetrical, tripled, radiant. But when Lumi bent to the reflection to smell it, there was nothing. The mirror returned light, but not fragrance.

"Images have no scent," she said quietly.

"Images do not need a scent," said the Gardener. "They are meant to look."

And he worked on until the Rose looked like a crown — every petal straight, every angle measured. Then he stepped back and looked at his work.

"Now," he said with satisfaction, "now you are finished."

The Rose was silent for a long time. Then — slowly, the way only a flower can — she moved a little. And one petal, the one the Gardener had spent the longest straightening, returned to its crookedness. Small, bent, its own.

"That one I am keeping," she said.

"Why?" said the Gardener, and it sounded as though the petal had offended him. "Everyone can see it spoils the symmetry."

"Because that is how it grew," said the Rose. "It grew when there was drought. It held when the others fell away sooner. It is crooked from what I survived — and when you straighten it, you tell a story that did not happen."

"But people judge what they see!"

"To see is not to live," said the Rose. "Your mirrors show me perfect — and a perfect rose cannot grow. Only the one that may change can grow. If you straighten me forever, I am no longer a plant. I am the image of a plant — and an image does not need water, but it also has no scent for anyone."

The Gardener looked at his mirrors and for the first time saw what was really in them: three perfect flowers, none of which could wither — and therefore none of which could be alive.

"And when people judge you by the images?" he asked.

"Let them judge the images," said the Rose. "I will stay where I am: in soil that cannot be mirrored."

In the end the Gardener of Mirrors did not pack the whole crate — he took only one mirror, the smallest, and set it by the river so that it reflected the water, not the flower. The rest he left lying in the grass, where they gradually became overgrown.

"Why did you keep one?" asked Lumi.

"Because a mirror can also serve," he said. "When it shows water, it shows the truth. The mirror is not the bad thing — the bad thing is when it becomes the measure. When a person begins to adjust themselves according to a reflection, they lose the shape they have."

In the evening Lumi and the Rose sat by the river, and the Rose had its crooked petal and was unlike all its images — smaller, asymmetrical, fragrant.

"Do you think," asked Lumi, "that people remember perfection, or truth?"

"They remember the scent," said the Rose. "Even when they forget the shape."

The image captured the shape; the crooked petal preserved the story.
