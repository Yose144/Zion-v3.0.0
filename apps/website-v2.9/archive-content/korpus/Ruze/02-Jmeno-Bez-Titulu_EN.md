# THE ROSE STORY — II. A name without a title
## The flower refuses the clothes Lumi brings it from a book — and chooses a word that is not a crown

The seed took the time it needed.

From the soil by the river first pushed a thin green thread, then a stem with thorns like a row of small points, then a bud — and one morning, when the mist over the water still held the night, the flower opened. It was not large. It was not perfect. Each petal was a little differently pink, as though it had not yet decided what colour it would finally be.

Lumi sat beside it with a small book she had carried the whole way from the bow of the ark — twelve written pages and one that had stayed blank, because those pages write themselves. She opened it and read aloud to the flower all the things in the world that already have a name.

"And you?" she asked at last. "Do you have a name?"

The flower leaned — or it was the wind — and then asked back: "Should I have one?"

"Everything that is to grow should have a name," said Lumi. "Otherwise you don't know whether people are speaking to you, or only the wind."

And she began to try names from the book. She tried the great ones — that was the mistake.

"You could be the Star of the garden," she said.

"Stars fall," said the flower. "And they shine from afar for those who look at them. I want to be fragrant up close for those who come."

"Queen of the river."

"Queens hold rivers as property. I need it in my roots."

"Promise of morning."

"Promises are fulfilled for you. I want to bloom myself."

Lumi tried several more — Memorial, Witness, Miracle of Dawn — and with each she grew quieter, because the flower answered every time with something that was true. At last she said the name that sounded the most solemn of all:

"A Sign. If you are a Sign, people will carry you before them and you will mean more than what you are."

The flower trembled. For the first time it did not answer with words but with thorns — all of them straightened like a row of small sentences.

"I am not a sign," she said. "I am alive."

Lumi fell silent and closed the book. She understood that all along she had been offering clothes instead of a name — and that every title she sewed for the flower was one size larger than life.

"Then how shall I call you?" she asked.

The flower bent to the water and looked into it, as though reading its own answer in the reflection.

"Call me Rose," she said. "That is all I am — and it is enough. Rose is not a title I would carry before me. It is the way to address me when you want to pour me water — not the way to hold me."

"And if someone takes that name from you?" asked Lumi. "If they call another rose Rose?"

"I chose the name myself," said the Rose. "What you choose yourself, no one gave you — and no one can take it. Other flowers can bear it and we will be two Roses, three, a hundred. A name is not land from which people are driven out. It is a bridge that is walked on."

"And will I know you when there are more of you?"

"You will know me by the manner," said the Rose. "Not by the label. Labels get rewritten — the way I speak with you cannot be rewritten."

From that day the flower by the gate was simply called Rose — not because she had an exclusive right to the word, but because it was a truth so ordinary that no one could distort it.

A name that is a way of address cannot be worn as a crown — and therefore no one can strike it down.
