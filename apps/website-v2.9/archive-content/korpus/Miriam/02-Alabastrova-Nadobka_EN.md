# MIRIAM — Chapter 2: The alabaster jar
## Stone · anointing — oil for three hundred denarii, the breaking of the jar, and a sentence that outlived everything else: "it will be told in memory of her"

> *"A jar that has given everything away holds longer than a jar that is full."*

---

## The Story

On the second evening Sarah brought, besides bread, a question she had carried the whole way from the town.

*"They say you were the woman with the jar. That you anointed his feet and wiped them with your hair."*

Miriam took in her hand a small vessel of alabaster — smooth, heavy, empty for countless years now — and turned it to the fire so that the light would enter the wall.

*"Listen to how stone is told,"* she said. *"Matthew and Mark tell of a supper in Bethany, in the house of Simon. A woman came with an alabaster jar of genuine nard — an oil so precious that it cost three hundred denarii, a labourer's yearly wage. She broke the jar and poured the oil on his head. The men at the table were angry: why this extravagance? It could have been sold for the poor! And he said the sentence that outlived all the others: 'She has done a beautiful thing. You have the poor always with you, but you will not always have me. What she had, she did — she has anointed my body beforehand for burial. And wherever the good news is proclaimed, what she has done will be told in memory of her.'"*

*"John tells of a similar supper in Bethany and calls her Mary — Mary of Bethany, the sister of Martha and Lazarus. Luke tells of a different supper, at the house of Simon the Pharisee, of a woman he calls only 'a sinner in the town' — she wept on his feet, wiped them with her hair, kissed them, anointed them with oil. Three scenes. One jar. Four names of women — and the fourth is missing."*

Sarah looked at the vessel in her hand. *"And you? Were you there?"*

*"I was at many suppers,"* said Miriam. *"And I will not resolve the story of the name for you — the sources argue about it among themselves. But I will tell you what I saw at all those suppers: that the woman who did the most beautiful thing remained without a name in the oldest records — and that every later age wanted her deed for some name of its own. Learn from it: the deed outlives the name. The dispute about the name outlives nothing."*

---

*"And one more thing I saw: the jar was not broken by accident. Those jars were sealed tight — the neck was snapped off, and what was inside could not be taken back. Three hundred denarii in a single gesture, with no discount, no calculation of 'a little now, a little later'. The men calculated. She broke.*

*That is why I carry the jar empty. Full, it would lie to you that something is still left in it. Empty, it tells the truth: everything was given away — and it still holds. A jar that has given everything away is not empty. It is ready."*

---

**Stone, clay and dream.**

*"Stone: the anointing is in the records — Mark 14, Matthew 26, John 12 and Luke 7. Three hundred denarii. The anger of the men. The sentence about burial and the sentence about memory. That is stone so hard that you read it even after two thousand years.*

*Clay: that the woman was me. The records are silent about it or argue — John says Mary of Bethany, Luke's sinner has no name at all. That we were one was decided only by later tradition — and in the fifth chapter you will see what that does to names.*

*Dream: that there was a magic potion in the jar, a secret of blood, a sacred recipe. People want secrets to be in jars. In this one there was only oil — and the courage to pour it out at once."*

---

## What it means

**The parable of the jar is a parable about how giving is decided — and about how the deed outlives the name.**

1. **Break before you calculate.** Three hundred denarii was not an amount someone "decided" to share out — it was a gesture before calculation. In the corpus it has an exact counterpart: from every block, 5 % for the earth and 5 % for the sky are set aside *at the birth of value*, before anyone can decide whether they feel like giving today ([`BodhiGaia/01`](../BodhiGaia/01-Semeno-a-Sul.md); the `internal repository`). A covenant carved into a beam, not a mood.
2. **"You have the poor always with you."** For two millennia the sentence has been abused as an alibi for leaving the poor poor — and yet it stands beside the scattered jar as a reminder that gesture and duty are not competitors. The corpus holds it the same way: the fund accumulates, and its spending is governed by the protocol, not by mood ([`BodhiGaia/09`](../BodhiGaia/09-Kotva-Pravdy-a-Hranice.md), G10).
3. **The deed outlives the name.** The oldest text left the woman without a name, and later tradition gave her three. That is the whole history of Miriam in one scene: *memory is more faithful than attribution*. The ledger of the corpus — an indestructible record — is the architecture of the same truth: what is written down survives the disputes about whose it was.
4. **An empty jar that holds.** Whoever has given everything away and keeps the list going is a guardian without a key — an image the corpus knows as "the one who counts has no key" ([`Lumi/06`](../Lumi/06-Semeno-pod-Domem.md); the read-only L5/L6 trackers). A key can be stolen; a list can only be checked.
