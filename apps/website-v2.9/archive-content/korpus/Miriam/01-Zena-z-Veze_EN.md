# MIRIAM — Chapter 1: The woman from the tower
## Stone · Magdala — a tower by the lake, seven darknesses healed in parts, and a woman who carried bread for the whole company

> *"Ask me about the tower first. A name is born from a place, not from slander."*

---

## The Story

The cave stood high in the mountain above the sea — so high that the waves below murmured like rain in a distant garden.

A girl had just come into the cave with a basket of bread and an armful of firewood. She was dark as earth after rain, and they called her Sarah. The old woman by the fire looked at her for a long time, as though seeing her for the first time — although she had known her for years.

*"People tell all sorts of things about me,"* said Miriam. *"That I sat here for thirty years. That angels carried me to heaven seven times a day. That my bones lie in gold in the town below the mountain. Listen carefully: the stories I will tell you have three kinds of clay. Some of it is stone — it has been preserved in old records and you can read it. Some of it is clay — thousands of hands have shaped it over centuries, and it holds together even though it is not stone. And some of it is dream — people needed to dream it, and a dream is honoured by naming it a dream."*

Sarah sat down. *"And the cave?"*

*"The cave is clay,"* Miriam smiled. *"But the fire is true. Begin to preach."*

---

*"By the lake stood a tower. The town was called Migdal — tower — and it was a town of fishermen and dyers. Boats caught fish at night, women salted them in vats, and from shell after shell a dye was boiled that people far from the sea came to buy. A person could grow rich in that town, even though no poetry was written in it.*

*There was a woman there whom they called Miriam of the tower — Miriam of Magdala. She had a name written by the town, not by lineage, and that says more about her than anything else: she was no one's daughter and no one's wife. She stood alone.*

*And she was ill. The records say that seven demons lived in her. When I tell you this, I am not telling you about a devil that roars. I am telling you about seven dark rooms in one house — about sleeplessness, about the days when the hand is heavy, about a sorrow that has voices, about a fear that has an address. Seven rooms, Sarah. Not one. And they did not open all at once.*

*The one we today call the Carpenter passed through the town and did not hurry. He did not rub against her like a miracle on demand. He did what he knew — he made room. Room by room they began to be cleaned, and one day she found that she lived in the house again.*

*The illness left in parts. That is also why I understand people who wait for one great step to come from you — and I tell them: seven rooms are not tidied with a single wave of the hand."*

---

*"And then she did a thing that is little told, because it works no wonders: she began to carry bread.*

*The record says that the women who went with him provided for him out of their own means. She, Joanna, Susanna and many others. A company of pilgrims that lived from gifts — and the gift was carried by men of distinguished lineage, and by the woman from the tower and women like her. When a boat had to be paid for, the boat was there. When bread was needed, bread was there.*

*And when the Carpenter by the lake told parables — about the sower, about the mustard seed, about the pearl for which a man sells everything — the crowd listened and went home. Miriam stayed. And she asked: and what does the seed mean? and why do you say it to the people in images and to us plainly?*

*Because she understood one thing sooner than most: that the one who sows must not look at every grain. The grain that comes up is hope. The grains that do not come up are not a loss."*

---

**Stone, clay and dream.**

Miriam divided the story into three little heaps, like a sorter separating seed on a table.

*"Stone: in the record of Luke it stands that Miriam of Magdala — she from whom seven demons had gone out — went with him through towns and villages, and that she, Joanna, Susanna and many others provided for them out of their own means. In the record of Mark it stands in the appendix that he appeared first to Mary Magdalene, from whom he had cast out seven demons. The tower by the lake really stood — they uncovered it under the ground, with a synagogue and a stone on which the ark of the law is carved. That is stone.*

*Clay: how wealthy she was, how she looked, how old she was, how she came to the company — the records are silent. Thousands of hands coloured it in; clay holds, but it is not stone.*

*And dream: that she was a sinner they found in order to show her as a lesson. There is not a word of it in the records. The dream was born much later and it looks fine — but I stood there and I know what I was. Not a sinner. A witness."*

## What it means

**Miriam enters the story not as a figure at the margin, but as the one who carries the whole mission on her back — and her first lesson is about resources, about patience and about how healing really happens.**

1. **A tower, not a lineage.** Migdal = tower. Her name is derived from a place, not from a husband or a father — in the early world that is in itself a remarkable trace: a woman standing on her own foundation. The books of the corpus know this: a name as a promise, not a deed of ownership ([`Issobella/01`](../Issobella/01-Jmeno-z-Hvez.md)).
2. **Seven rooms, not one miracle.** "Seven demons" is in the oldest layer an image of an illness healed gradually — and Miriam turned it into an experience, not a stigma. The parable that real tidying happens in parts holds for people and for networks alike: difficulty is adjusted, not overwritten (`difficulty.rs`, LWMA-60).
3. **The woman who carries bread.** "They provided for them out of their own means" — before legends were told about her, she carried real logistics: a boat, a night, bread. In the corpus this is a line that must never be silenced: the fund is public, the purpose documented, nothing is given quietly (`StatusV3.md` — the premine in 14 public outputs; [`BodhiGaia/01`](../BodhiGaia/01-Semeno-a-Sul.md) — "nothing is given quietly").
4. **The one who asks.** Miriam stays when the crowd leaves — and asks what the seed means. The figure of the book is guided precisely by this: whoever stays and asks receives the explanation; whoever passes through with the crowd hears a parable. Both are honest, they are simply not the same.
