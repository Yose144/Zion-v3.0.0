# The Story of Eleven Stops

### I. The Salt of the Earth
*The salt you receive for free is of no use to you until you knead it into your own bread.*

> *"You are the salt of the earth. But if the salt loses its taste, how shall it be salted? It is no longer good for anything except to be thrown out and trampled underfoot."*
> — Matthew 5:13

---

## Story

The city was called Tasteless, though it had never wanted to be named so. Once it was a city like any other — markets fragrant with cumin and fresh bread, children laughing in the courtyards, old women at the fountain trading stories of who had managed to survive the famine. But the years passed, the merchants grew rich, the king built higher walls, and the food sold in the markets began to taste different. Not worse — different. The bread was white and soft, but without taste. The wine was old and expensive, but without taste. The promises of politicians were long and carefully crafted, but without taste.

No one knew exactly when it happened. Perhaps it was slow, the way river water evaporates a few drops a day, until one morning you find the bed dry and no one remembers when it last flowed.

One evening, as the sun fell behind the walls like an ember doused in water, a man from Galilee came into the city. He was not a king, nor a scholar with tablets, nor a general. He was a carpenter who knew how to work with wood, and even better with silence.

He sat down beneath an old fig tree at the edge of the market, where those gathered whom no banquet would take in — water carriers, mercenaries without pay, women who had buried more children than they had raised. From his satchel he drew a small clay cup of coarse salt — the kind people made in those days by evaporating seawater or mining it from salt deposits — and set it on the ground among them.

"What of it?" asked one of the carriers. "It's just salt. We're hungry, not saltless."

"Try your bread without it," the carpenter replied.

The men laughed, but one of them, an old mercenary with a scarred hand, took the flatbread he carried at his belt, dipped it with salted fingers, and bit. He stood still for a moment in silence.

"It tastes different," he said at last. Not more. Not less. Different. As if he had remembered a taste he had lost without ever noticing when.

The carpenter nodded. "You are the salt of the earth," he said quietly — not as a sermon, but as an observation he had been permitted to speak aloud. "Not the king. Not the general. Not the wine merchant whose stock ages comfortably in a cellar. You — the ones the city forgets about until they are needed — you are what gives flavor to everything else."

"And what if that salt loses its taste?" asked the woman who had buried three children and still carried water to the well, because it was the only thing she knew how to do for the living.

The carpenter looked at her long, not with pity but with gravity. "Then it is no longer good for anything but to be thrown out and trampled," he said. "That is not a punishment. It is merely a description of what happens when salt ceases to do the one thing it is for."

None of them grew rich that night. No one received an answer as to how exactly they would bring taste back to Tasteless. But when they parted in the morning — the water carrier to the well, the mercenary to the gate, the woman to the neighbors' children she now helped, because her own were gone — each carried a handful of coarse salt in a pocket. Small, unremarkable, cheap. And yet the very thing by which the bread they baked themselves would, at last, be worth eating.

## What it means

Salt in Jesus' time was not a garnish spice. It was a covenant, a preservative, and a medicine in one. Without it, meat spoiled, wounds festered, bread went moldy. It was small, cheap, unremarkable — and yet a handful was enough to transform an entire pot.

Jesus did not tell his disciples "you are the gold of the earth" or "you are the sword of the earth." He said **salt**. That is a precise choice of word, not an accident:

- **Salt works by touch, not by command.** It must dissolve, blend in, disappear into the food. The less it is seen, the better it does its work.
- **Salt has covenantal power.** The "covenant of salt" (*berit melach*) in Levitical law meant: what we have tasted together holds, even without words.

**ZION as salt:** ZION is not a kingdom, a bank, or a new temple that would seek to cover the sky with borrowed authority. It is a network that blends in among miners, a humanitarian fund, communities, and blocks — and gives them the flavor they individually lack: **trust without the need to trust a single person.** Salt does not rule the food. ZION is not meant to rule the world. It is meant to guarantee that the rules hold — without words, simply by being verifiable.

### II. Dissolution
*Salt in a pile just lies there. Salt in water gives taste to the whole river.*

> *"As a river flows toward the ocean, let your mind flow too — without clinging, without resistance."*
> — paraphrase, inspired by the Buddhist teaching of *anattā* (non-attachment)

---

## Story

A second pilgrim arrived in Tasteless a week later. He carried no bow, no sword, nothing that could serve as a weapon or as a proud proof of the road traveled. He had only a begging bowl, and he walked slowly, as though the journey never mattered to him more than the step he was taking.

The water carrier, the mercenary, and the woman — who still carried a handful of the carpenter's salt in their pockets — met him at the well.

"Who are you?" asked the mercenary, who trusted no one without a weapon — because in a city where taste had vanished, everyone survived by their own means, and unarmed men tended to be either saints or dead within a week.

"I was a prince," the pilgrim answered. "I had a palace, a wife, a son, endless stores of rice, and slaves who lit incense before I slept. And I was more wretched than anyone else in my kingdom, because beyond the walls I had seen old age, sickness, and death, and I could not unsee them."

"And now?" asked the woman.

"Now I have a bowl," he said, and lifted it. "And I know it is enough."

They sat with him on the edge of the well. He was a pilgrim in no hurry to get anywhere, and that alone was strange in Tasteless — everyone else rushed after the taste they had lost, as though it could be caught by walking faster.

"The carpenter gave you salt," said the pilgrim, seeing the white grain on the mercenary's palm. "Salt is a good thing. But I will ask you a different question: what will you do when you have too much of it?"

No one answered right away.

"If you had an entire warehouse of salt," he went on, "it would cease to taste. Salt that does not dissolve is only mineral dust. Salt in a pile preserves nothing — it just lies there, waiting to become property that people will fight over."

"So what are we supposed to do with it?" asked the water carrier.

"Dissolve it," said the pilgrim. "Not store it. Not own it. Dissolve it — into water, into bread, into the soup you give your neighbor without expecting it returned. Salt you clench in your fist will only cut your hand. Salt you release into the river gives taste to the whole village downstream."

The mercenary frowned. His whole life he had believed that survival meant hoarding — food, weapons, scars as proof that he had endured more than the rest. "That sounds like weakness," he said.

"I tried the strength of the palace," the pilgrim replied calmly. "I had an army. I had gold. And still I woke at night with a question no army could answer: *what is it all for, if it ends one day?* The middle way does not mean giving up everything, nor clinging to everything. It means acting — and letting go. Dissolving the salt and letting it do its work, without watching to see whether the river is stealing it from you."

No one in Tasteless grew rich that night, and no one grew poorer. But the woman who had buried three children, for the first time in a long while, took the handful of salt from her pocket and gave it to a neighbor she knew only by this: that the neighbor had a hungry child. She expected nothing back. And yet, when she returned home, her own supper tasted different — not because she had more, but because she had stopped counting what she had left.

---

## What it means

Siddhartha Gautama left the palace and did not seek a new throne. He sought the **middle way** — between indulgence and austerity, between clinging and indifference, between hoarding and emptiness.

His wisdom can be distilled into three gestures:

- **Recognize** — suffering exists, and denying it does not diminish it.
- **Release** — its root is clinging, not the world around you.
- **Walk the middle** — act with full commitment, without grasping at the result.

Buddha's salt does not accumulate. **Salt dissolves.** Cast into water, it ceases to be "salt that we own" and becomes the taste of the water that belongs to everyone who drinks. It does not own the water. It demands no share. And yet without it, the water is only dead liquid.

That is the question Buddha would put to any network that calls itself salt: **Does it accumulate, or does it dissolve?**

### III. The Taste of Water
*The greatest power does not boast of a throne. It hides in the taste of the water you drink without thinking about it.*

> *"I am the taste of water, the light of the sun and the moon... the faculty in man."*
> — Bhagavad Gita 7.8

---

## Story

The third pilgrim did not arrive by the road. He arrived with music.

Tasteless heard him before it saw him — a quiet, gentle flute tone that drifted over the walls like the scent of rain no one could quite remember the quality of. People came out of their houses, not because anyone had called them, but because the sound loosened a knot inside them they had not known was there.

A dark-skinned shepherd with a smile that looked as though no one had ever managed to remove it sat down on a stone by the fountain, where water once flowed and now only a dry channel remained. He raised the flute to his lips again and played on, as though the fountain being empty did not trouble him in the least.

"The fountain is dry," said the woman, who by then knew the whole story of Tasteless better than anyone — the salt from the carpenter, the dissolution from the pilgrim with the bowl, and now this.

"The water is here," the shepherd answered, "it has only forgotten how it tastes."

"What do you mean?"

The shepherd set the flute down and looked at her in a way that, for a moment, made her feel not like a woman who had lost three children, but like someone he had always known. "Ask me who I am," he said.

"Who are you?"

And to that simple question the shepherd did not answer with words. He closed his eyes, and those who stood closest — the water carrier, the mercenary, the woman, the carpenter who happened to be returning from the field just then, and the pilgrim with the bowl who had come from the other side of the city — all of them saw, for an instant, more than they could name. They did not see a king. They did not see a god on a throne with thunderbolts in hand. They saw **everything at once**: a raindrop falling into a river, a star extinguishing above mountains a thousand years before their birth, a child being born at that very moment three towns away, and their own breath going out and returning, as though it had never begun and never ceased.

For a moment it was unbearable. And then it was gone, and before them stood only the shepherd with the flute, his smile a little sad, a little amused.

"Why did you show us that?" asked the mercenary, his voice trembling for the first time since he had arrived in the city.

"Because you asked me who I am," the shepherd answered, "and I answered you with truth, not courtesy. I am not the king of this city. I am the taste of the water you drink. I am the light by which you see. I am that faculty within you which you use when you choose to do the right thing, even when no one is watching."

"And what are we supposed to do with that?" asked the water carrier, who had spent his whole life caring only about how to get water from the well to the houses.

"Nothing more," the shepherd answered. "Do your work — the water carrier carries water, the mercenary guards the gate, the carpenter builds, the mother tends. But do it without expecting reward for the outcome. Krishna does not tell Arjuna to lay down his bow. He tells him to fight without clinging to victory. The work itself is the offering, if you do it with your whole heart and release the fruits."

That night the fountain did not fill. Water did not begin to flow again through the channel that had been dry for years. But the water carrier, as he carried his usual ten buckets from the river to the houses of the old women, noticed for the first time in years how the water cooled his palms, how it gleamed in the moonlight, how it tasted when he allowed himself a single swallow on the way. And he thought that perhaps this — the ordinary, infinitely repeated step — was the entire prayer he knew.

## What it means

In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna asks Krishna to reveal his true form. Krishna grants him the **Vishvarupa** — the cosmic form, in which all beings, all worlds, and all times manifest at once in a single body. This is a majesty that is not pomp. It is the revelation that **the greatest is hidden in the smallest.**

Krishna does not say "I am the king of water." He says **"I am the taste of water"** — that invisible quality which makes water something worth drinking.

Krishna's flute is hollow. If it were solid, it would make no sound. Its music passes through the emptiness inside it. So too does a network that wishes to carry value without hoarding it — it is a channel, not a vessel.

Krishna's message to the builder is direct: **act without clinging to the fruits of your action** (karma yoga). The work you do with your whole heart is itself the meaning — not a means to a reward you calculate in advance.

### IV. The Road Untrodden
*Where the map ends, the compass begins — and the bridge that no one will build unless someone starts before being asked.*

> *"Where does your network lead when there is no map for it?"*
> *"I don't know exactly where every road leads. But I know which way is north."*

## Story

The morning after the shepherd's song, three more came to Tasteless — and for the first time they did not come by the road everyone else had taken. They came through the forest, where no path existed, because no one from the palaces ever walked there voluntarily.

**Rama** carried a bow slung across his back, but he was not going to battle. He was a king who had voluntarily left his own kingdom — fourteen years of exile, not as punishment, but as a test of whether he could hold his shape even where no throne enforced it.

**Sita** did not follow him as a husband's shadow. She walked because the forest was as much hers as the palace — she was *Bhūmija*, daughter of the earth itself, risen from the furrow of a plow. Where else would she belong but the land that bore her?

And **Hanuman**, who was neither king nor queen, only a servant with such strength that he could leap an ocean in a single bound, came last. Not because he was the least — because his role always comes last: to build the bridge over which the others will cross.

At the old fountain the carpenter, the pilgrim with the bowl, and the shepherd with the flute were waiting for them — and with them all that remained of Tasteless: the water carrier, the mercenary, the woman who had stopped counting what she had left.

"You have a parable, a trial, and a song," said Rama, as he sat by the fire. "We have only a forest without a road. How will salt, dissolution, and music help us where there is no map?"

"By what did you walk through the forest when you had no map?" asked the carpenter.

"By dharma," Rama answered. "Not a rule carved in stone, but a question we ask anew at every step: what is the right action, here, now, regardless of what it costs?"

Sita knelt and scooped earth from the edge of the dry fountain into her palms. "This land holds no less because I am a stranger in it," she said. "The trial does not come in the palace, where it is easy to be honorable when it costs you nothing. It comes in the forest, where no one watches — and still you remain who you are."

Hanuman, meanwhile, bent over the fountain's empty channel and began clearing the stones that had clogged it for years. No one had asked him to.

"What are you doing?" asked the mercenary.

"Building a bridge," Hanuman answered plainly.

"There is no ocean here."

"Not yet," said Hanuman, lifting a stone that three men together could not have raised. "But the water that this shepherd sang of will arrive here one day. And when it does, it will be worth nothing if it has no channel to flow through to the people who have been waiting for it for years and have stopped believing it will come."

The woman who had buried three children sat down beside Sita and offered her the last of the salt she still carried in her pocket. She did not need to explain anything — Sita understood at once, because that itself was dharma: to give what you have to the one beside you, without counting the days until recompense.

"You will not know the strength you hold until you forget you have it and leap anyway," Hanuman murmured, more to himself than to the others, and threw another stone aside for the water that was coming.

That night no one in Tasteless knew exactly when the water would arrive. But for the first time in as long as anyone could remember, the fountain's channel was passable again — clear, empty, waiting. And that waiting, for the first time, was not desperate. It was patient.

**Hanuman** was not on the road to conquer anything for himself. He was there to serve — and his strength did not manifest in a throne, but in a bridge over which an entire army, an entire people, anyone who wished to cross to the other side, could pass.

## The Story

The ark grew slowly. The ribs of the hull were already visible from the far end of Blandness, but the deck was only half laid, and the doors the shipwright had spoken of still had no hinges.

The blacksmith, who had lost his workshop in a fire years ago, came first every morning. He did not know how to build ships — he knew how to forge nails, bands, and hinges, and so he did exactly that until his hands turned black. The farmer from the surrounding fields came right after him, with a sack of planks he would otherwise have burned in his oven. Neither of them had been at the first fire where the carpenter unpacked the salt. They came later, when there was already work for hands, not just ears to listen.

The rest of the town laughed at them.

"Carpenters of the rain," the tavern keeper at the market said to them, and laughter carried across the square. "Hew wood for a flood that may never come. That plank would fetch you a sack of flour sooner than your fool's ship ever will."

The blacksmith woke at night with a doubt he admitted to no one aloud: *What if the tavern keeper is right? What if I'm forging hinges for something that will rot on the shore before a single drop falls?* His hands ached from the hammer, and he had no certainty — only the shipwright's word and a compass with north engraved upon it.

One evening a merchant appeared at the fire, returning from a long journey across the sea. He did not know Blandness, did not know the ark; he simply saw the light and sat down.

"You're building a ship on dry land," he said, more amused than mocking. "It reminds me of something I heard far from here, in a land across the sea, where people began to mine a strange stone that cannot be touched or weighed — only counted. It was so worthless that one man gave ten thousand pieces of that stone for two flatbreads with sauce, and was glad someone took food for the stone at all. People laughed at him, just as they laugh at you. They told him he was wasting his time playing with numbers that would never be worth anything."

"And what happened then?" asked the farmer.

The merchant shrugged. "I don't know for certain what is happening now — I have been on the road for years. But I know this: those who mined that stone when everyone still laughed at it had more of it, earlier, and more cheaply, than those who came later, when it was talked about in every tavern. I don't know whether the stone will one day become a treasure, or whether it will remain just a stone. No one knows that — not even the man who gave ten thousand pieces for the flatbreads. But I know that the first hammer fell before the rain began — not because the carpenter knew exactly when the storm would come, but because he decided to build before anyone could prove it to him."

The blacksmith was silent for a long time, looking at his blackened hands.

"Then," he said at last, more to himself than to the merchant, "I will keep forging hinges. Not because someone promised me gold at the end. But because when the water comes, I will know which way the doors open — and the man who today only sits in the tavern and waits to see what happens will not."

That night no new pilgrim with a memorable name joined the fire. Only the blacksmith and the farmer joined, somewhat wearier, somewhat more certain, and hammers struck wood long after sundown.

## The True Story, Not a Parable

Here the telling pauses for a moment, because what the merchant said is not invented. It truly happened.

On May 22, 2010, programmer **Laszlo Hanyecz** paid **10,000 bitcoins** on the Bitcointalk forum for two pizzas from Papa John's, which another forum user ordered to his Florida address in exchange for the coins. At that time there was no exchange, no dollar price worth speaking of — bitcoin was a curiosity of a handful of people who mined it on ordinary home computers because they were interested in the idea of money without a central bank, not a vision of profit. The reward for a single mined block was then **50 BTC**, and the network difficulty was so low that a laptop could handle it.

This day is now celebrated as **Bitcoin Pizza Day** and is the most-cited example of how no one — truly no one, including Laszlo himself — had any idea what would become of that network. It was not a clever investment. It was a man who wanted to eat, and had on hand a stone that no one was sure what it was actually for.

This is not proof that anything similar will repeat with ZION, with any other network, or with anything at all. **It is only a fact about what the first day of every open network looks like: few people, zero certainty, and the opportunity to mine something whose future value is decided by none of those who sit at the fire today.**

## What It Means for ZION Builders and Miners Today

The compass from Episode 6 does not point only in the direction of the story. It also points to this: **a network that is small today will not stay small forever — and what is the rule today may tomorrow become rarer, not more common.** That is not a prophecy about price. It is a description of how the rules are written, rules you can read right now, without divination.

Three things are written into ZION's code in a way that holds regardless of who reads them:

**The block reward only decreases from here — it will never grow.** ZION is today in its first decade (2026–2036), when the reward for a mined block is **5 400.067 ZION** — the highest the protocol will ever pay. Each subsequent decade it drops by a fifth (factor 0.8), until after a hundred years it settles on an eternal reward of 724.78 ZION per block. Whoever mines today mines in the decade with the highest reward this protocol will ever know — that is not a promise, it is a schedule written into the consensus.

**Network difficulty is governed by how many people are mining right now, not by how many will be mining a year from now.** Difficulty is recalculated by the LWMA algorithm over the last 60 blocks (with a ±25 % clamp) — it responds to current network hashrate, not to the future. The fewer machines searching for a block today, the larger the share of found blocks goes to each individual machine that joins now. When the network fills with more miners (and that is the goal — a network no one mines is not secure), difficulty will rise with them, and the same hashrate will yield a smaller share.

**Entry is the same for everyone who comes — but not equally easy at every moment.** Fair launch means no one got VIP access at the start. It does not mean the start lasts forever. The genesis block fell on January 1, 2026; every day that passes is a day more of history, a day more of miners, and a day closer to the moment when "starting from zero" will no longer be possible — because zero was long ago.

This is exactly what the blacksmith from the story did: he did not forge hinges because someone guaranteed him a reward. He forged them because he understood the mechanics of rain — that water does not wait for everyone to decide to believe.

## What This Chapter Does Not Promise You

Salt that knows it is salt will say this aloud too:

- **No one promises that ZION will one day have the value of bitcoin, a fraction of it, or any value at all.** Bitcoin Pizza Day is a historical fact about a different network, not a prediction about this one. - **This is not investment advice.** It is a description of how the mathematics of emission and difficulty works in an open network you can verify yourself in the code, not a reason for a decision you should not think through with your own head.

The compass points north. It does not tell you whether it is raining already, or will be in a year. That is a question every builder must answer for themselves — just as the blacksmith, whom no one guaranteed the ark would ever sail.
