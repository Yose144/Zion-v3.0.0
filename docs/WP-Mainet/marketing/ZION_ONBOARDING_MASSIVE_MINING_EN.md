# PART ONE — THE FIRE
## A story that hits everyone

### I. The city that forgot the taste

Once there was a city called **Tasteless** — though it never wanted that name.

It had everything: markets, temples, banks, promises. And yet everything tasted the same: like nothing. The bread was white and soft, but tasteless. The wine old and expensive, but tasteless. Money dissolved faster than people could earn it. Contracts held only for those who could afford lawyers. Systems promised security and delivered fees.

Nobody knew exactly when it happened. Maybe slowly — the way a river evaporates a few drops a day, until one morning you find the bed dry and nobody remembers when it last flowed.

Then, one evening, the pilgrims came. Not all at once. One by one, each with a different gift, each to the same fire at the edge of the market, where those gathered whom no inn would take inside.

### II. The Salt — the first pilgrim

The first was a carpenter from Galilee. Not a king, not a general. He sat under the old fig tree, took out a small clay cup of coarse salt, and set it on the ground.

"What's that? It's just salt. We're hungry, not salt-less."

"Try your bread without it," he answered.

An old mercenary dipped his flatbread into the salt, bit down — and stood still. "It tastes different." Not more. Not less. **Different.** As if he had remembered a taste he lost without noticing when.

"You are the salt of the earth," the carpenter said quietly. "Not the king. Not the merchant. You — the ones the city forgets until it needs them. Salt is not the main course. Salt works by touch, not by command. It must dissolve, blend in, disappear into the meal — and yet without it, everything tastes dead."

**ZION as salt:** not an empire, not a bank, not a new temple. It is a network that blends in among miners, funds, and blocks — and gives them what each lacks alone: **trust without the need to trust any single person.** Salt does not rule the meal. ZION does not aim to rule the world. It guarantees that the rules hold — wordlessly, simply by being verifiable.

### III. Dissolution and the Taste of Water — the second and third pilgrims

The second pilgrim brought the middle way: salt is meant to **dissolve, not to pile up**. Whoever heaps it has a warehouse, not a flavor. Neither reckless speculation nor fear of the new — code as a practice of attention.

The third played a song about **the taste of water** — about the greatest things hiding in the smallest. Work, but do not cling to the fruits. Mining is not just income: it is work that keeps the network safe while feeding a fund that feeds a child you will never meet.

### IV. The Untrodden Path — a bridge across the ocean

Three more came through the forest where no path led, carrying **a compass instead of a map**. And one of them — the one who could carry stones — began to build **a bridge anyone can cross**.

The bridge has a name in our world: bridge and WARP. The link between ZION L1 and the other worlds — Base, EVM chains, wZION. Not so ZION dissolves into foreign cities, but so nobody has to choose between the island and the mainland.

### V. The Ark — a ship before the flood

The last pilgrim arrived carrying the dust of the longest road and said: "The water will come harder than you expect. Not punishment — **physics**. A world overloaded — too many tasteless promises, too much gold without work — will one day overflow. And you will need a ship, not a palace."

"And those who stay on the shore?" asked Sita.

"Nobody forces them aboard. The ark does not save by force. It saves by being **open and visible long enough in advance** for everyone to choose for themselves. And it has **one door, not two** — not one for kings and one for the poor."

That night, for the first time, it was not only the early ones who gathered in Tasteless. A farmer came with planks. A blacksmith came who had lost his workshop to fire. And for the first time, the night was not about waiting for a taste that had vanished. **It was about building.**

# PART TWO — THE HOUR BEFORE THE RAIN
## Why mine right now — math, not prophecy

### VI. The blacksmith they laughed at

The ark grew slowly. The blacksmith arrived first every morning — he could not build ships, but he could forge hinges, so that is exactly what he did. The rest of the town laughed: *"Rain-carpenters! Hauling wood for a flood that may never come!"*

One evening a merchant from a far country sat down by the fire: "You remind me of people I heard of across the sea. They dug a strange stone that could not be touched, only counted. It was so worthless that one man gave **ten thousand pieces for two flatbreads** — and was glad anyone took them at all."

That is not a parable. It happened. On May 22, 2010, **Laszlo Hanyecz** paid 10,000 bitcoins for two pizzas. The block reward was 50 BTC then, and a laptop was enough to mine it. That day is now celebrated as **Bitcoin Pizza Day** — not as proof it will repeat, but as a fact about **what the first day of every open network looks like: few people, zero certainty, and the chance to mine before every tavern talks about it.**

"So then," said the blacksmith, "I will keep forging hinges. Not because anyone promised me gold. But because when the water comes, I will know which way the doors open — and the man who only sits in the tavern today will not."

### VII. The math of the first hundred miners

Here the story stops and only the numbers speak. Three things are written in the code so that they hold no matter who reads them:

**1. The block reward only falls from here — it never rises.**

| Period | Block reward |
|---|---|
| **Decade 1 (2026–2036) — NOW** | **5,400.067 ZION** — the highest in the network's entire history |
| Decade 2 (2036–2046) | 4,320.05 ZION (−20%) |
| Decade 3 (2046–2056) | 3,456.04 ZION (−20%) |
| … every following decade | −20% |
| Tail from ~2126 | ~724.78 ZION/block forever |

**2. PPLNS splits 89% of every block among those mining right now.**

The pool pays via PPLNS — proportionally to accepted work within the window. And here is the thing nobody has to promise you, because it is plain arithmetic:

> The network finds **~1,440 blocks a day** (one every 60 s). Each block carries **4,806 ZION for miners** (89% of 5,400). That is **~6.9 million ZION per day**, split among all active miners on the network.
>
> **While dozens of machines mine, that pie is split among dozens.** When there are thousands, the same pie splits among thousands. The emission schedule will not change — only your share will. The first hundred miners mine under conditions that will structurally never return.

This is not a price promise. ZION has no guaranteed market value today — just as bitcoin had none in 2010. It is a description of **the emission schedule and share arithmetic**, which you can verify in code before you switch on a single machine.

**3. Difficulty responds to today, not tomorrow.**

LWMA recalculates difficulty every 60 blocks from the network's current hashrate. Few machines = lower difficulty = a bigger chance for your rig. The network will grow (that is the goal — an empty network is not a safe one), and difficulty will grow with it. Whoever learns today learns under the gentlest conditions there will ever be.

### VIII. What you gain even if the price is zero

Honesty demands this too: maybe ZION will never hold the value bitcoin holds. Nine out of ten stones people laughed at stayed stones. But the early miner gains things that cannot be bought later:

- **Knowledge.** Wallets, rigs, monitoring, security — learned in calm, not in a crowd.
- **History.** Your address, your shares, your blocks — written into the chain from the beginning.
- **A voice.** A young network listens to its first builders. Your bug report changes code today. In five years it will change a ticket queue.
- **A share in the funds.** Every share you submit feeds the humanitarian fund and Issobella. Even if ZION stays a stone, the meal that its 5% paid for was not a stone.
